Anthropic Rings the Bell: $83 to $96.86 on Its First Day as a Public Company

From the saves — Financelot, Anthropic, Uday Kotak, Jugeshinder Robbie Singh, Ashton Kutcher, Aviral Bhatnagar & Sridhar Ramaswamy

On Thursday, the first pure-play frontier AI lab in history started trading on the Nasdaq — and the market did not hesitate. Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 a share on Wednesday, raising about $81.3 billion at a valuation near $1.5 trillion. By the time Dario Amodei had finished his opening remarks and the bell had rung, the stock was printing at $96.86, up 16.7% on the issue price.

It is worth pausing on the scale of what that sentence contains. A company that did not exist five years ago is now worth more than the combined market capitalisation of India’s ten largest companies — the comparison Uday Kotak put to his followers on Thursday morning, and the one that set off the day’s sharpest argument. But first, the ceremony itself, because Amodei used his six and a half minutes at the podium to say something more interesting than a listing-day platitude.

The speech: a father, a century, and a race to the top

Amodei opened with the personal. He and his sister Daniella — Anthropic’s president, who introduced him — lost their father to a disease that became curable just a few years later. He started his career in biology, he said, hoping to help future cures come sooner, and in the lab he saw “the vast thicket of complexity that scientists face when they’re trying to understand disease and develop new treatments.” The tools that cut through that thicket — gene editing, gene synthesis, controlling brain cells with light — all “started as pure ideas. In other words, they came from thinking, from intelligence.”

That was the bridge to his central claim, and it is the one to remember from the day:

“I believe sufficiently powerful AI, working in concert with humans, could compress a century of scientific and medical progress into a decade.”Dario Amodei, Nasdaq opening remarks, 19 November 2026 — transcribed from the bell-ringing clip

He was careful to add the caveat himself — “at this point, all we have are early results” and clinical trials will still take time — but the direction of the argument was clear: medicine is “a particularly vivid example” of what Claude can already accelerate, from protein folding to clinical-trial administration, and the same intelligence is being applied by customers “to stimulate economic growth, lift people out of poverty, protect democracy and free societies, even make everyday services like the DMV work better.”

Then came the part of the speech that matters most for anyone holding the stock, or competing with the company. Amodei has always been honest, he said, that the technology carries very real risks — misuse by individuals or nation states, economic disruption, losing control of AI systems — and “we discuss these risks not because we think they are inevitable and we are building anyway, but because we think they are avoidable.” Avoiding them, he argued, “depends on the choices made by the people building AI,” and Anthropic will pace the training and deployment of its systems “taking as long as is necessary to have confidence that they are safe and will remain under human control. We will not bend to either business nor political pressure.”

His closing frame was the “race to the top”: if Anthropic can show it is possible to make the right choices on safety and still build an incredibly successful company, others in the industry will be inspired to do the same. “When the race to the top is working properly, it doesn’t matter which company has the best models, everyone wins because everyone is building safely.” It is a remarkable thing to say on the morning your company is valued at $1.5 trillion — a promise, made in public, that the market can now hold him to.

The film: “What’s the best that could happen?”

Anthropic also released a 71-second film on the day, and it is the opposite of a listing-day sizzle reel. There is one line of voiceover — “I mean, what’s the best that could happen?” — over a quiet montage of human moments, and a single caption: “What we make of this moment, will make this moment.” The frame below, verified from the film itself, is a doctor holding an elderly patient’s hand, with the word “more” on screen. The company is selling optimism, deliberately, on the day it becomes a public company — and the film’s restraint is the point: no benchmarks, no demos, just the question.

The $1.5 trillion question: undervalued India, or a new regime?

Kotak’s post was short and deliberately provocative: Anthropic, “an AI company known for Claude, listed at a valuation of $1.5 trillion. It did not exist till ~5 years ago. This is more than the combined market cap of the top 10 Indian companies. Are we undervalued, or do we need to catch up in the new world? Or both?”

The sharpest answer came from Jugeshinder Robbie Singh, the Adani Group’s CFO, who called the comparison a “cross-regime category error” — “the equivalent of comparing the market value of a steel plant with the value of the steam engine in the early 19th century.” His argument, in full, is worth reading carefully because it is really an argument about what a market is for:

India, he wrote, “is being priced by the market as a linear, industrial economy. Anthropic is being priced as a non-linear, exponential intelligence infrastructure.” India’s top ten are “mature, cash-generative, low-multiple businesses. They represent the current economy. Anthropic represents the operating system of the future economy.” Legacy valuation frameworks — DCF, P/E, EBITDA multiples — “are built for industrial-era assets. They break down when the asset is intelligence itself. Anthropic’s ‘revenue’ is not the product. It is a side-effect of compounding intelligence. We are not pricing a company. We are pricing a new factor of production.”

“The market is not valuing Anthropic’s present. It is valuing its optionality on a civilisational shift: from human capital to synthetic intelligence.”Jugeshinder Robbie Singh, Group CFO, Adani Group

His conclusion: “The $1.5T valuation is not a bubble. It is a signal that the market has already moved to a new regime. The question is whether India’s capital markets will follow, or remain anchored to the industrial past.” You do not have to agree with him — plenty of careful people think $1.5 trillion for a company with a $900 billion computing bill ahead of it is a stretch, and that question is exactly what Social Capital’s deep dive (carried over to tomorrow, when its free version publishes) sets out to answer. But Singh’s framing is the right one for the debate: the argument is not about a multiple. It is about whether intelligence is a new factor of production, and if so, who owns it.

The people who were there first

Three posts from the day captured what the listing felt like to the people who had a stake in it before it was a $1.5 trillion company. Ashton Kutcher — whose fund Sound Ventures backed Anthropic, and who wrote to “the LPs that supported us” — put it as a story about time, not money: “People usually see the one deal and think that’s the whole thing, but the ones who know realize that it’s years of work, micro victories and failures stacked on top of one another. It’s really the manifestation of trust.” And then, with feeling: “thank you to those who didn’t believe in us. You made us work harder.”

Sridhar Ramaswamy, who led Google’s search ads team through roughly 35% compound growth for fifteen years, offered the day’s best compression: “@AnthropicAI feels like it has squished 15 years of growth and history (and euphoria, power and ambivalence) into < 3 years!”

And Aviral Bhatnagar did the arithmetic that makes the whole week feel slightly unreal. Zoom’s market capitalisation is $27 billion, down 60% from its $63 billion IPO. Zoom invested $50 million in Anthropic’s 2023 Series A. That stake is now worth $12 billion — “half of Zoom’s market cap is from the new red-hot IPO’s valuation of $1.4T.” One $50 million cheque, three years ago, is now worth nearly half the company that wrote it.

The takeawayDay one gave us three things to track: whether the $96.86 open holds once the ceremony fades; whether Amodei’s public safety promises survive the first quarter as a listed company; and whether Singh is right that the market has moved to a new regime — or whether, as Kotak implied, the rest of the world simply needs to catch up. The bell is rung. The argument starts now.

Mailchimp, Sold for Less Than a Third of What Intuit Paid

Federico Simionato — via the Business Wire release, 19 November 2026 · verified against Reuters, WSJ and Intuit’s own announcement

Bending Spoons has agreed to buy Mailchimp from Intuit for $3.68 billion — $3.31 billion in cash and $368 million in Bending Spoons stock, with the deal expected to close in early 2027. Intuit paid $12 billion for Mailchimp in 2021, the largest acquisition in its history. Five years later it is selling at less than a third of that price.

The numbers tell the story of a deal that went wrong slowly. Mailchimp’s revenue fell 1% to $313 million in the quarter ended 31 October, while Intuit’s total revenue rose 12%. The email-marketing business that was supposed to extend Intuit’s small-business platform ended up slowing its growth instead, and Intuit — which announced the sale alongside its fiscal first-quarter results — will use the cash proceeds to buy back shares.

The buyer is the interesting half of the story. Bending Spoons, the Milan-based holding company run by Luca Ferrari, has built a portfolio by buying faded software brands cheap and running them for cash: Evernote, WeTransfer, Vimeo, AOL, and Airtable, bought in September for about a tenth of its former value. The company, which recently attracted a major investment from Berkshire Hathaway, raised $1.3 billion in a private placement of 32.5 million shares alongside the Mailchimp deal. Intuit, for its part, is reinvesting $368 million of its proceeds into newly issued Bending Spoons equity — a vote of confidence in the consolidator, or at least a hedge on the asset it is selling.

Mailchimp’s round trip

The takeawayThere are two ways to read this deal. One: Intuit overpaid in 2021 and is cutting its losses. The other, more interesting one: Bending Spoons is assembling the world’s largest portfolio of software that the market has given up on — and the market keeps handing it inventory. Watch what it does with Mailchimp next.

The 23-Year-Old Who Sold an 18-Month-Old Startup for $4.5 Billion

Madhav Chanchani — verified against Palo Alto Networks’ announcement, the WSJ and Moneycontrol

Yash Patil was supposed to graduate from Stanford last year. Instead he took leave, spent two years at OpenAI, and started Applied Compute — which Palo Alto Networks agreed this week to acquire for $4.5 billion in stock. He is 23. The company is 18 months old. He and his two co-founders are, by one breakdown of the deal, worth about $2 billion between them.

Applied Compute does post-training and inference for open-weight AI models: the unglamorous, enormously valuable work of taking a general model and turning it into a specialised system that runs on a company’s own data, inside its own walls. Its customers include Nvidia, DoorDash and Microsoft; it is working with Nvidia on post-training its open-weight Nemotron models to build complex AI agents. The team is about 70 people, mostly former OpenAI researchers who worked on Codex and o1. After the deal closes, Patil becomes Palo Alto Networks’ co-chief AI officer.

The speed is the story, and the speed has a logic. A fortnight before the acquisition, Applied Compute raised $350 million at a $3.25 billion valuation. Palo Alto Networks, run by Nikesh Arora, paid $4.5 billion — a 38% premium to a round priced two weeks earlier — because it is not really buying revenue. It is buying the team that knows how to make open-weight models safe and useful inside enterprises, at the exact moment every large company is deciding whether to run AI on open models, closed frontier models, or both. Palo Alto’s shares rose more than 9% in after-hours trading on the news.

The investor arithmetic, from Paul Bonnet’s breakdown of the deal: investors share roughly $1.85 billion of proceeds on $510 million invested, about a 3.6x blended multiple, for a company founded 18 months ago. That is a very good return. It is also, notably, less than half the deal value — the founders and employees keep the rest, which is how a 23-year-old who left Stanford ends up, on paper, a billionaire before his class graduates.

The takeawayPost-training — the work of adapting models to a company’s data, tools and constraints — is where the enterprise AI money is moving. The labs sell intelligence; the layer that makes it usable inside a real business is now worth $4.5 billion, and the people who can build it are worth about $64 million each.
From the Saves • India • The Dilemma, Survived

Urban Company and the Innovator’s Dilemma — a Year Later

Rahul Mathur — from the post and the Urban Company charts it carries

Exactly a year ago, Urban Company was in the position Zomato found itself in back in June 2022, when Blinkit launched to take on Zepto, Instamart and the rest. Two upstarts — Snabbit and Pronto — were raising large rounds and scaling aggressively in home services. The unbundling of Urban Company, Rahul Mathur writes, “was a real threat.” A year later, the stock has done a round trip — down 36% in six months, then up 36% in the six months after — and the threat, for now, has not materialised.

The post is a small masterclass in what the innovator’s dilemma actually feels like from inside. Urban Company could have done the easy thing, Mathur argues — the thing DMart and Reliance Retail did: nothing. Observe, pray, and hope to pick up a salvageable asset in three to five years. Instead the team bet that “the catch up game in Consumer doesn’t work” — that in consumer businesses, once you fall behind, you do not catch up by watching. Investors who wanted immediate profits lost faith while the company spent three quarters executing “on all fronts”; the stock fell by more than a third. Then the execution showed up in the numbers, and the stock recovered by a similar amount.

Urban Company (NSE: URBANCO) — the round trip

There is a longer arc underneath the stock chart, and it is the part that makes the story more than a trading note. Urban Company was one of about 300 home-services companies in 2015. It is the only one that listed a decade later. Survival in that market was itself the moat — and the year Snabbit and Pronto came for it was the year it had to prove that survival was a strategy, not an accident.

The takeawayThe innovator’s dilemma is usually told as a story about incumbents who cannot move. Urban Company’s version is the mirror image: an incumbent that moved, was punished for it for six months, and was then rewarded for it. In consumer businesses, Mathur’s rule holds — the catch-up game doesn’t work, so you either defend the market or lose it.

GTA VI Day: 100,000 Searches, Zero Sick Days

Aadit Palicha, Parker Conrad, Kalshi & the gameplay clips — 19 November 2026

GTA VI came out on Thursday — Rockstar’s announced release date was 19 November — and India felt it before the rest of the world woke up. At the Zepto office, Aadit Palicha reported “GTA 6 Mania”: more than 100,000 users searched for GTA 6, PS5 and controllers on Zepto in a single week, and the company sold out “faster than we have ever seen.” The launch was, in other words, a real demand event, not just a timeline event.

Which raises the obvious question for every employer in the country: how many people called in sick? Parker Conrad, the Rippling chief executive, asked his own company’s AI exactly that — “Is there an unusual number of sick days or vacation days scheduled for the day that GTA 6 is released (or the day after)?” — and posted the answer, slightly disappointed. There was no spike at all. Approved vacation on release day was 1.7% below the Thursday average, and 7.2% below the Friday average, even counting pending requests. Either gamers booked their time off honestly, or — the more likely explanation — they simply played after work. The AI, which worked on the question for 55 seconds, found nothing to report.

Meanwhile the game itself was doing what GTA does. The clips in the pile tell the story: an NPC burning himself just to sue a company (with IShowSpeed reacting in the corner), a streamer trying to reach the maximum wanted level and escape a full police chase, and Jason sneaking through an airport terminal to steal a commercial plane. Thirteen years and a reported $1.6 billion of development later, the joke is that the most realistic thing in Vice City is still the lawsuits.

Paytm Puts &rupee;200 Crore on Southeast Asia’s Small-Business Lending

The Arc — from Paytm’s disclosure letter to BSE and NSE, 19 November 2026

Paytm’s Singapore subsidiary has approved an investment of up to &rupee;200 crore in Funding Societies, the Southeast Asian SME lending platform. The first tranche is &rupee;162 crore for a 19.1% stake in CardUp, a Funding Societies company, with a further &rupee;38 crore potentially going into a Funding Societies affiliate. Alongside the money, Paytm will partner with CardUp to provide its payments and lending technology in Singapore, while the local partner handles sales, distribution and on-ground operations.

It is a neat division of labour and a familiar Paytm pattern: export the technology stack, let a local partner own the last mile. The disclosure, filed under Regulation 30 of SEBI’s listing rules, lands as Paytm continues its slow climb back — the stock has roughly doubled from its May 2024 lows, and the company has been rebuilding its lending and payments businesses after the RBI action on its payments bank. A &rupee;200 crore bet on Southeast Asian SME credit is small against that arc, but it is pointed in the direction Paytm wants to go: technology provider, not just balance-sheet lender.

The RSI Debate: Two Moderates Argue the Curve

Interconnects — Nathan Lambert with Jean-Stanislas “JS” Denain of Epoch AI · 22 September 2026 · 65-minute episode, transcript read in full

The most useful hour on AI progress this week is two months old, and it is two people who disagree politely about the most consequential question in the field: is AI about to start improving itself, faster and faster, in a loop nobody controls? Nathan Lambert and Epoch AI’s JS Denain both land on the moderate side — and the interesting part is where, exactly, their moderation differs.

Denain’s starting point is that the public evidence for imminent recursive self-improvement is weaker than the discourse suggests. OpenAI and Anthropic have both published posts about AI accelerating AI research, and the most striking number in them is that researcher spending on Codex is doubling every month. That is evidence the tools are valuable, Denain says — not evidence that “in six months we have a software intelligence explosion.” The distinction he keeps returning to is between a single researcher and a whole lab: the idea-to-experiment cycle may soon be ten times faster, but that does not mean the median researcher is ten times more productive, and it certainly does not mean Anthropic as a company produces ten years of output in one. “A 10× researcher is not a 10× lab,” as Lambert puts it. The bottlenecks that do not speed up — compute allocation, research direction, the sheer difficulty of knowing what to try next — are the ones that decide the pace of the field.

The second half of the conversation is the sharpest public accounting available of the US-China gap. Denain puts it at six to eight months on public models, measured by Epoch’s capabilities index — though Lambert notes that Kimi K3 and GLM 5.2 looked closer to two to four months on some benchmarks. The puzzle is that the gap in money and compute is measured in years, while the gap in capability is measured in months, and the episode is mostly a list of candidate explanations. Distillation — training on a stronger model’s outputs — is the one Denain has updated most on: the Stolen Thoughts paper suggested it is relatively easy to extract useful training data, and Anthropic’s Claude routers hand a lab realistic prompt-and-answer data rather than benchmark-shaped data. Without distillation, he estimates, the gap would drift from about six months to eight or nine within a year. Lambert is less convinced, and offers the competing explanations: Chinese labs have far less of a compute delay in labour and data than in chips; there is a second-hand market in reinforcement-learning environments, with data vendors selling the environments a frontier lab paid to develop at a tenth of the price; and keeping up is simply easier than inventing — the four-minute-mile effect, where proving something is possible saves everyone else the cost of exploring dead ends.

Two other threads are worth carrying out of the episode. On safety, the pair discuss the news that three people used public Claude models to hack OpenAI and gain internal access, and the uncomfortable conclusion that safeguards are permanently leaky: if you host an API at all, assume the capabilities can be extracted. And on what a frontier lab actually does all day, Denain sketches the post-training recipe as he understands it — domain sub-teams curating data and reinforcement-learning environments, bidding for a share of one big final RL run — and admits how much of it is guesswork. Epoch’s job, as he describes it, is to find the evidence that is lying around in public: job postings that reveal a lab building its own data centres, hiring patterns that reveal a product strategy. “The biggest safeguard,” Denain says at one point, “is just there’s not that many people who want to do horrible things. Fingers crossed.”

Why it matters to youThe RSI debate is the frame behind everything in this edition — the $1.5 trillion valuation, the $900 billion compute bill, the race to the top. Lambert and Denain’s version is the sober one: acceleration is real, the explosion is not yet in evidence, and the binding constraints are the boring ones — evals, data, research taste, and who gets to decide what to build next.

Hide Your Slop: Simon Willison’s One-Line Fix for Agent-Written PRs

Simon Willison’s Weblog — 9 November 2026 · read in full

Most of the changes Simon Willison makes to his projects these days arrive as pull requests opened for him by Claude Code or Codex. The agents are, he writes, “great at writing PR descriptions” — and that is precisely the problem. “An agent-written PR description is accurate and comprehensive. It describes the problem, the detailed solution, and the steps taken to verify it. That’s a lot of information to absorb.” It is not necessarily slop, he says. “But it reads like slop!” His instant reaction, even to his own agents’ work: “I’m not reading all of that.”

The fix fits on one line of Markdown. Open the PR with a short paragraph a human has approved — “the shortest text a human can read that will help get them up to speed” — and put the full agent-written essay inside an HTML <details><summary> block, labelled with the model name and effort level, for example “Claude Opus 5.5 Medium”. GitHub Markdown renders the block natively, so the reviewer sees the human judgement first and the agent’s avalanche one click away. The information is all still there; the eyes stop glazing over. Willison gives five recent examples from his datasette and llm projects, including PR #3052, and a mnemonic for the tag order: always say “details-summary” — two syllables, then three — and you will never forget which tag wraps which. He closes by wishing GitHub would add a toolbar button for the tags, “partly to save me painstakingly typing it on my mobile keyboard, and partly to help spread the word.”

Why it matters to youThe pattern generalises beyond PRs: wherever agents write for humans, the scarce resource is no longer the writing — it is the reader’s trust that a human has decided what is worth their time. A two-sentence opener that carries a person’s judgement, with the machine’s completeness folded behind it, is the cheapest interface fix in the agent era. Try it on your next agent-opened PR.
