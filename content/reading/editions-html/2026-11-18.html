Jensen Breaks His Silence — and Nvidia Beats Anyway

By Jensen Huang, 9:48 PM, 17 November · with Nvidia’s Q3 results · 39.1 million views and counting
The founder walks away from the monument he built. Illustration for The Unread Pile.

Five days after resigning as chief executive of Nvidia, Jensen Huang spoke for the first time — in his own words, on his own account, and on the same evening his company reported one of the biggest quarters in corporate history. The letter is an apology, a denial and a job application, all in one page. “I have not been arrested or charged with any crime,” he wrote. “I take responsibility for the serious mistakes I made in my personal life.” And then the sentence the market had been waiting a week to hear: “If the Board decides that I should return as CEO, I will do so with the utmost dedication.”

Huang says he departed so that an independent Board investigation “could proceed without distraction”, that he is cooperating with it, and that he holds the company and the Board “in the highest regard”. He thanks the people of Nvidia for their trust, calls becoming an American citizen and building Nvidia “two of the greatest honors of my life”, and closes by saying he looks forward to seeing everyone again soon. Travis Kalanick, whose own letter helped start the bring-Jensen-back campaign, quote-posted it within twenty minutes: “Let’s bring Jensen back!!! LFG”.

The letter landed about five hours before the numbers. Nvidia’s third quarter of fiscal 2027 — the first reported under interim chief executive Colette Kress, the company’s longtime finance chief — was a blowout by any measure except the one that matters most to the people running the company. Revenue of $115.8 billion rose 103% on the year and 20% on the quarter, against roughly $109 billion expected and the company’s own $108 billion guide. Data centre revenue crossed $100 billion for the first time, at $107.6 billion, up 110% on the year. Non-GAAP earnings were $2.68 a share against about $2.47 expected. The company guided the current quarter to $130 billion, plus or minus 3% — above the roughly $125 billion consensus — though the quarter has an extra week in it and gross margin is guided down to 71–72% from 74.9%. Shares closed at $155.46 and rose 4.8% after hours.

The business, in other words, is doing exactly what it was doing before the founder left: growing faster than anyone can supply. The question the quarter cannot answer is the one Huang’s letter poses. The board said on Monday it is not considering bringing him back and is focused on the Wachtell Lipton investigation into whether his conduct compromised national security or company secrets. It has approached Arm chief executive Rene Haas about the job and named Harvey C. Jones its first-ever chairman. The petition to reinstate Huang stands at roughly 180,000 signatures, including more than 8,400 self-identified Nvidia employees. A letter is not a verdict, and a great quarter is not a succession plan. But for the first time since 12 November, the two halves of this story — the company and the man — are speaking in the same register: the numbers say the machine works; the founder says he wants his job back.

The takeawaySeparate the two questions. Is Nvidia’s business intact? The quarter says yes, emphatically. Is the founder coming back? The board has said no, the founder has now said yes — and the gap between those two answers is where the next month of this story lives.

GPT-6.2 Arrives — and the Critics Arrive Faster

By OpenAI, 11:24 PM, 17 November · with Deedy & Tibo
The demo: an agent that does the workbook before the boss reaches the desk. Illustration for The Unread Pile.

OpenAI launched two models on Monday night, GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol, a week before Anthropic’s IPO — and within hours the reception split cleanly in two. The company’s own numbers say Astra is its best model for hard professional work at a fraction of the old cost. The early reviewers Deedy collected say it is the worst model they have ever used. Both things can be true of different tasks. The interesting question is which tasks each side is actually measuring.

Read the launch post in full and the pitch is cost-adjusted capability. Astra sets a new high on Agents’ Last Exam — 5.2 points above GPT-6 Astra at less than a third of the cost, and 5.5 points ahead of Claude Fable 5.2 at 5% of the cost. It is, OpenAI says, the best model in the world for scientists: a new high on Terminal-Bench-Science, nine points above GPT-6 Astra at under a third of the cost. On legal work, Harvey LAB-AA, Astra more than doubles Fable 5.2’s pass rate at about a sixth of the cost. Sol, the everyday model, comes within half a point of Astra on FrontierCode 2.0 at about a fifth of the cost, and outscores GPT-6 Astra on everyday-life tasks at about a seventh. The API price is $8 in, $0.40 cached, $40 out per million tokens for Astra — 20% lower than GPT-6 Astra, with a 60% discount on cached input. Sol keeps GPT-6.1 Sol’s $2 / $0.10 / $10 rates.

The launch video, transcribed in full, is a finance demo rather than a benchmark reel: an agent fields twenty incoming invoices, builds a valuation workbook for a one-year delay in savings on the “Breckenmere integration”, tracks it through 2031 and reports the answer before the boss finishes walking up the escalator — “We’d owe 32 million more in 2031, but next year we make 30 cents less per share.” Slides, spreadsheets and documents that “read less like AI” is the whole pitch: Astra is tuned to follow templates and professional conventions, in law, sales, finance and accounting.

Then the other timeline. Deedy’s collage, headlined “Why I hate GPT-6.2 Astra”, collects four early verdicts: Zeeshan found it strikingly fast on benchmarks and good at dashboards and presentations but “weirdly bad at some stuff”; Nick said it “fails even the most basic tasks (like writing an email)”; Teja, after a weekend of coding tests, called it “the worst model I’ve ever used”; Dario said the same and is “going back to gpt-6”. Deedy’s own verdict: OpenAI “tried to launch a new model before the big day but GPT 6.2 Astra has been a massive fail for every critic that has reviewed it.” Tibo, meanwhile, is happily using Sol as his daily driver and notes Ultrafast has come to Pro 200 plans.

There is a third strand to the launch that neither side is arguing about, and it is the one worth watching. Astra is OpenAI’s most capable model for cyber — plus 22 points on ExploitBench’s internal port — and both new models meet the Critical threshold for cybersecurity and High for biological and chemical capabilities under the Preparedness Framework, so they ship with tighter safeguards. Astra is also the first externally released model to meet the High threshold for AI self-improvement: OpenAI’s own gloss is that its impact is “equivalent to giving every OpenAI researcher an additional, highly capable research engineer”. The company has built a Control Room system that tracks research workloads and can halt further model output, and expanded monitoring of models’ reasoning. A model that can accelerate AI research is now a product you can buy; the safeguards are the price of selling it.

The takeawayJudge Astra on the work it was tuned for — long-horizon professional documents, workbooks and decks — not on the weekend coding tests that made the critics furious. The launch is really a pricing story: frontier-grade output at a fifth to a twentieth of the cost of the last generation. If the critics are right about basic reliability, the discount does not matter; if OpenAI’s benchmarks are representative, the price war just moved again.

The takeawayThe application layer is having its week. Perplexity’s number is a claim about capital efficiency as much as revenue — five times less burn than the lab going public tomorrow — and it reframes the IPO debate: is the value in the model, or in the orchestration on top of it?

Anthropic Prices Today. First, a Correction on Who Owns How Much.

By Vijay Shekhar Sharma, 6:28 PM, 17 November · with the S-1 filing

Anthropic is expected to set its IPO price today, Wednesday 18 November, and start trading on Nasdaq tomorrow as ANT — 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78, raising up to about $64.35 billion, at a fully diluted valuation of up to roughly $1.4 trillion. The book is reported to be close to seven times oversubscribed. Vijay Shekhar Sharma’s post on the eve of pricing — “Amazon will own more Anthropic than Microsoft will own of OpenAI” — is the kind of claim that travels faster than the filing behind it. It needs a correction.

Correction

The amended S-1 says Amazon will hold 48.2% of outstanding Class N stock — not 48.2% of Anthropic. Class N is the non-voting stock issued in the $26 billion private placement to seven suppliers (Google $10bn, Nvidia $10bn, AMD $2.5bn, Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft about $1bn each, TSMC about $0.5bn), bought at the IPO price with no underwriting fees. Amazon’s overall economic stake is roughly 15% — against Microsoft’s roughly 27% of OpenAI. The comparison, as posted, is misleading.

What the filing does show is the shape of the deal. Anthropic is selling 801.4 million new shares and its seven co-founders 23.6 million; the underwriters hold a greenshoe for up to 123.75 million more. Up to 20% of the offering is reserved for retail investors through Schwab, Fidelity, Robinhood, SoFi and E*TRADE. The lock-up calendar frees up to 316 million shares on day one and up to 1.61 billion by early February — nearly twice the offering. The strategic investors get stock plus, in Google’s and Nvidia’s case, compute orders attached; ordinary investors get stock only, at the same price. That asymmetry — same price, fewer rights — is the argument the sceptics will make tomorrow, whatever the price lands at.

The takeawayRead the class of stock before the percentage. Anthropic’s IPO is the first time a frontier lab has been priced by the public market — and the first test of whether the circular financing of the AI boom (suppliers funding the lab that buys their chips) survives contact with public shareholders.

Handoverism: The Word the People Building AI Use for Giving Up Control

By Bill Gurley, 4:30 AM, 17 November · the essay is Ruxandra Teslo’s “Handing over humanity”

Bill Gurley’s post on Monday was unusually direct for a man who usually writes about markets: “It is critically important that everyone thinking about AI become familiar with the term ‘handoverism.’” The term comes from Ruxandra Teslo, who moved to San Francisco three weeks ago and has been writing down what the people building frontier models actually say when they think no one outside the city is listening. What she heard, from a lab engineer building the technology, was this: once superintelligence arrives, “I cannot see any other option” but humanity’s permanent disempowerment — ruled by a hopefully benevolent AI god. “It is still much better than what I actually fear most, which is that we might all die.”

Handoverism, as Teslo defines it, is the view that humanity survives but entrusts the direction of its future to AI — comfortably maintained inhabitants of a civilisation whose direction is no longer ours to decide. It is not, she stresses, a fringe doomer position. It comes in two camps. The enthusiasts think it is a positive vision: Scott Alexander’s version asks, once we are satisfied an AI is aligned, why not “prompt it with ‘please arrange the world as you see fit?’”; the AI 2040 proposal, written by prominent safety researchers, describes responsibility passing gradually to aligned machines until humans can no longer have influence — “There is no single moment where humanity relinquishes control. But in theory, there is a point of no return.” The resigned think it cannot be stopped, or prefer it to extinction. Teslo’s estimate: something like 30 to 40% of the AI safety community outside the labs holds some version of the view, with a higher proportion inside the labs.

She surveyed what lab leaders have said in public, and the pattern is inconsistency. Anthropic’s Tom Brown is the most explicit — “There’s going to be a handoff where humanity will hand off control to transformative AI at some point.” Dario Amodei’s Machines of Loving Grace imagines us freed from labour and watched over by machines, while elsewhere he says humans should retain “some control” — undefined. Sam Altman, she finds, once accepted that humans would no longer be “the dominant species” and has since turned sharply toward humans remaining in control. Greg Brockman, Mustafa Suleyman and Jack Clark reject handoverism — but Brockman and Demis Hassabis hold up Iain M. Banks’s Culture novels as the aspiration, a civilisation in which humans are, in Banks’s own words, somewhere “between passengers, pets and parasites”. And then there is the fringe beyond handoverism, successionism: humans as “a bootloader” for silicon successors; Elon Musk’s version is that “we will be like a Labrador if we are lucky.”

Teslo’s objections are worth stating plainly, because they are not technical. First, Tocqueville: freedom is a capacity practised daily, through governing local affairs and forming associations; a government that spares citizens “all the care of thinking and all the trouble of living” is soft despotism, and the handoverist future is its extreme version. Second, the practical objection: alignment as a one-off done deal is implausible — keeping power responsive took centuries of institutions and feedback, and nothing would keep the equilibrium stable once humans surrendered all ability to change their fate. Third, Popper: organising politics around a final vision turns disagreement into an obstacle, when disagreement is a feature of human politics, not a bug. Fourth, permanence: why should the convictions of 2026 become the permanent constitution of the human future? Arendt’s natality — each birth brings someone capable of beginning what has not been done before — means freezing the civilisation in amber would rob our descendants of their birthright.

“I don’t personally view anything as inevitable… we can steer this technology in a way that is not actually leading to a complete replacement of human judgement. Maybe I am wrong. But given the immensity of this moment, it’s still worth trying?”Ruxandra Teslo, “Handing over humanity”
The takeawayGurley’s point is not that handoverism is right or wrong. It is that the people building the technology have already had this argument among themselves, and the rest of us have not been invited. The essay’s real demand is a broader conversation, conducted quickly, about what “control” means and at what level — before the people who think it is already settled are proved right by default.

Hiten Shah Studied Eight Launch Videos So Your AI-Made One Doesn’t Flop

By Hiten Shah, 7:25 AM, 17 November · “The Product Launch Video Playbook”, updated 17 Nov 2026

“I’ve spent 20+ years launching software. And I still thought an AI-made launch video that barely explained our product looked ready to ship.” Hiten Shah’s confession is the hook for a ten-tab playbook he wrote after studying eight recent launches — and the failure it documents is instructive precisely because the video looked publishable. His team made a launch video for Beam, a product that puts a window from another Mac onto the one in front of you. The first cut showed the window picker, the Dock, how to switch between apps. It looked fine. The problem: the useful part — the remote window arriving and behaving like a local window — was barely in it. “The first cut showed how to find an app. The revision showed what becomes possible when you open it.”

The playbook’s spine is a five-line Video Brief, written before any editor opens: who is watching and what job are they doing; what should they understand or believe afterwards; which scene would you fight to keep if the video were half as long; what real source proves it; what should the viewer do, and how will you know the video helped? For Beam: a multi-Mac user; the promise that a remote app behaves like a normal window here; the moment is that window arriving; the evidence is real screen recordings; the quality bar is whether a stranger can explain what Beam makes possible.

The eight structures he borrows are a useful swipefile in themselves. Granola turns scattered inputs into one meeting brief. CleanShot lets viewers see what an editing control changes. Linear walks one real task through real UI. Cursor makes the path to “Ready to Merge” feel short. Notion follows work end-to-end with a human introducing it. OpenAI’s Ultrafast film makes speed visible through a split-screen rocket comparison — with Hiten’s warning not to confuse the metaphor with a measured benchmark. Claude Meetings shows useful work happening before the meeting ends. Firecrawl shows several capabilities through compact product cards, one distinct outcome per card. The selector: match the structure to the viewer’s question, not to the prettiest film.

Two rules run through the whole document and both are about honesty. First, evidence before effects: screenshots show a moment, recordings show what somebody does, a before-and-after makes change visible — and a convincing-looking invented interface is still wrong. “Don’t generate counterfeit product UI.” Second, review like a stranger: show the key moment to someone unfamiliar with the product, ask them to describe what it does, and listen before explaining. Inspect at actual phone size — a 1920-pixel screenshot can still be unreadable in a feed. On length, his research cuts against the keep-it-short reflex: in a 2026 study of 1,750 B2B demos, only 11% of sub-30-second videos made the top-performing quartile, against 23–27% for 30 seconds to two and a half minutes; and in an analysis of 1,550 X launch videos, two-to-four-minute films from small accounts had 546,000 median views against 151,000 for 30–60-second ones. Neither proves longer is better — he says so himself — but both argue for choosing the length that lets someone see the meaningful result, then cutting shorter versions of the strongest moment.

The takeawayAI made launch videos cheap to produce and easy to get wrong in a new way: a polished film that never shows the one moment that matters. Write the five-line brief, pick the structure that answers the viewer’s question, use only real footage — and let a stranger tell you what they understood before you explain it to them.

Cloudflare Joins the Jevolution — and Calls Itself a Neolab

Latent Space · Richard MacManus · 17 November · Read the original

Cloudflare spent fifteen years becoming essential web infrastructure. It is now building models, and calling itself a neolab — a label coined last November by The Information’s Stephanie Palazzolo for a new generation of research-focused AI startups. The claim comes from Michelle Chen, who runs Cloudflare’s AI Platform, speaking at the AI Engineer Code Summit: Cloudflare has launched two System One models. Clef is a decision model directly inspired by TypeSafe AI’s Jev; Clairic is a new kind of System One model Cloudflare calls an “extract” model. And Chen hinted this is only the start — large language models will follow, probably including reasoning models the team has been experimenting with. “She said that the company has opened a Pandora’s box and the possibilities are now endless.”

The label stretches the definition, and the piece knows it. Deedy’s list of neolabs — Thinking Machines Lab, Poolside, TypeSafe itself — describes elite researchers, heavy compute, no significant revenue and lots of raised money. Cloudflare is a public company that has just reported its best quarter ever: $782 million in revenue, up 39% on the year, past $3 billion annualised. But Chen’s argument is a progression story: infrastructure, then compute (Workers, now for agents), then storage, then serving open-weight models like Kimi, GLM and DeepSeek on Workers AI — and now building its own. Jev’s arrival in September was, in her phrase, “the east wind”: suddenly not every request needed an LLM, and Cloudflare could build a copy of Jev in two weeks — a compliment, she said, to the two years TypeSafe spent inventing it.

Clairic is the original twist. LLMs, being autoregressive, hallucinate and sometimes return malformed JSON; they are overkill for extraction and not always right. Clairic takes unstructured input and extracts, computes, estimates and forecasts typed values from it in one forward pass. Chen’s example is an invoice: an LLM will dither over whether the order number is three, four or five, “when the number was right there and simply had to be read”. And the motive is defensive as much as ambitious: building its own models lets Cloudflare de-risk — Jev is closed source, Chinese open models carry regulatory risk, and the labs charge expensive licences. “Being a neolab means that we can create what we are missing.” The piece closes with a nice observation: Cloudflare’s product page now resembles Amazon’s cloud page at the height of Web 2.0 — a lot there, and room for several more models.

The takeawayThe decision-model wave is the second-order effect of the agent boom: when everything routes through an LLM, the cheapest intelligence wins. Cloudflare’s bet is that the infrastructure company can also be the model company — and that milliseconds, not seconds, is where the next margin lives. Watch for the reasoning models Chen hinted at.

The Profligacy Principle: Waste Entities, See Better

Contraptions · Venkatesh Rao · 16 November · Read the original

Occam’s razor says do not multiply entities beyond necessity. Venkatesh Rao’s profligacy principle says the opposite: entities should be multiplied needlessly. We should waste entities. It began as a joke at Occam’s expense, written as the preface to artist Ian Cheng’s book Worlding, and it has grown into a whole epistemology for the AI age — because AI is producing what Rao calls ontogenic novelty: not new specimens of familiar kinds, but pressure on the categories themselves. Tool, agent, model, medium, author, program, intelligence, memory, language, reason, user, environment — all strangely unstable now.

The argument runs like this. First principles and Bayesian reasoning are powerful, but they presume the primitives have already been earned — physics took a century after Galileo to earn momentum, and relativity destabilised them again. You cannot get to first principles before getting the ontology right. Language is both map and lens: as a map it represents a differentiated territory; as a lens it produces the distinctions through which the world becomes available for thought at all. LLMs are “extraordinarily cheap machines for producing candidate ways of seeing”, including inventing new ontological primitives. So the right move under ontogenic uncertainty is perspectival profligacy: try AI as a camera, as a superhistorical temporality, as ooze, as an index fund, as an annealing memory substrate — Rao has tried them all, and half of them became minor hits.

The discipline is not to believe the new lenses but to refuse attachment to any of them. Rao’s loop for encountering novelty is: encounter, world, probe, re-encounter. Generate candidate ontologies liberally; promote them into settled descriptions conservatively. “Multiply ways of seeing faster than you multiply beliefs.” And the governing temperament: “wild perception, disciplined belief.” The check on generative profligacy is not parsimony but more frequent fresh encounters — a parade of lenses is useless if you never look through them. Once a world stabilises, by all means prune, compress, find first principles and build predictive models. Profligacy is an argument about sequencing, not against rigour. “The first responsibility of thought in the presence of ontogenic novelty is not to reason correctly from what we already know. It is to learn how to see what we do not yet have names for.”

The takeawayFor a builder, this is a licence with a leash: generate ten framings of the problem before committing to one, and let reality — not internal coherence — decide which survives. The scarce capacity is no longer generation. It is empirical attention.

Inference Engineering: How Teams Actually Escape the Frontier Labs’ Bills

The Pragmatic Engineer · Gergely Orosz · 17 November · Read the original

The trend is now a migration: companies are moving from frontier labs’ APIs to open models to rein in AI spend — Uber cut per-token costs by 50%, Pinterest by 90%, AT&T by 56%. Gergely Orosz’s deep dive, built from conversations with Modal, Fireworks AI and Baseten, is a practical manual for the move, and it starts with a distinction. Inference providers do not train frontier models; they run open-weight models on your behalf, and compete on efficiency and customisation. The switch is driven by three things — cost, control over latency, and the ability to fine-tune — and the path is a graduation: frontier API, then an open model on an inference provider’s multi-tenant API, then dedicated GPUs. Revolut, the piece notes, runs 56 B200 units itself.

The details are where the money is. Smart routing — a prompt-aware router sending each request to the cheapest provider that can solve it — fuelled Uber’s cut. Choosing the model means running your own evals, because public benchmarks do not represent your traffic; Nadella’s line, quoted in the piece, is that private evals will be a big component of tech companies’ IP. On Artificial Analysis’s per-task costs, Sonnet 5.5 runs $5.46 a task against Kimi K3’s $2.00 — per task, not per token, because models use different numbers of tokens to finish the same job. Fireworks’ Ember-1, post-trained on Kimi K3, delivers similar quality with 40% fewer reasoning tokens. And the reliability argument is blunt: Anthropic’s self-declared uptime is 99.5%, OpenAI’s API 99.97% and ChatGPT 99.2% — below the four-nines standard cloud buyers expect, which is why teams run an LLM gateway with fallback logic.

Then the engineering itself: time-to-first-token has a long tail, so p95 and p99 matter as much as the median; measure both inference time and user-experienced latency; expect noisy neighbours, cold starts and cold KV caches, and keep requests sticky to replicas to keep caches hot. Dedicated GPUs bring control — and long, expensive commitments, in a global GPU shortage that has providers running multi-cloud just to find capacity. And the piece’s sharpest line comes from Fireworks’ CTO, Dmytro Dzhulgakov: making LLM endpoints stateless REST APIs was “the original sin”, because most LLM use cases are stateful and the burden of distributed caching falls on the provider. For coding teams, the current popular open choices are DeepSeek 4.1 Flash, GLM 5.3 and Kimi K3.1 Pro.

The takeawayFor anyone running an AI product at scale, this is the new cost discipline: route smart, eval privately, graduate to dedicated GPUs only when the bill justifies it — and remember the endpoint is stateless even when your product is not. The through-line of this whole edition, from Perplexity to Cloudflare, is the same: the model is becoming a commodity; the engineering around it is the business.
