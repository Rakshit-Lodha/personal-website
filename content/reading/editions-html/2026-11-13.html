Jensen Huang Is Out: Nvidia Loses Its Founder After 33 Years

Shaun Maguire, Aravind Srinivas and Palmer Luckey, via X • Nvidia’s board announcement, 12 November 2026 • Read the release • Maguire’s post

On Thursday, the board of the world’s most valuable company announced that its founder had resigned — as president, as chief executive, and as a member of the board, effective immediately, after 33 years at the helm. The statement gave no reason. It carried no words from Jensen Huang. It published no findings from the independent investigation the board had announced four days earlier. And it named as interim chief executive the woman Huang had, by one account, dismissed in front of his top finance team in August as a “reporting CFO” who was “a deer in the headlights”. That woman, Colette Kress, now runs Nvidia.

The four days that ended a 33-year reign ran like this. On Sunday 8 November, Nvidia’s board announced an independent investigation following reports that Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence effort — reporting that centred on Alice Huang, an executive at a major Nvidia business partner in Southeast Asia now suspected by US intelligence of facilitating restricted chip shipments to China. The same day, Huang acknowledged to employees that he had been unfaithful to his wife. On Thursday, the resignation. This paper has followed the thread for a week: Edition 46 led on the affair becoming a national-security story, and Edition 49 led on the short seller whose May report first raised the China questions. The report was ignored for six months. The resignation took four days.

What the release did say is the shape of the interim regime. Kress, executive vice president and chief financial officer since September 2013, keeps her finance job while serving as interim CEO until a permanent successor is named. The board has begun a search and will consider internal and external candidates. Harvey C. Jones, a longtime director and former Synopsys chief executive, becomes Nvidia’s first-ever chair of the board. Operations, customer commitments and the product roadmap are to continue without interruption, and the third-quarter fiscal 2027 results stay on schedule for Tuesday 17 November, with the earnings call at 2pm Pacific. That call, five days after the resignation, is now the most-watched earnings event of the year.

Four days, and thirty-three years

The reaction in his timeline was a eulogy and a succession debate in the same breath. Shaun Maguire, a Sequoia partner, wrote the long post that anchors this story, and it is worth reading as an argument rather than a tribute. His case for why Huang mattered rests on three attributes: he was technical, he was aggressive, and he had the credibility required to be aggressive. The evidence runs through the post like a CV of the AI age: Huang founded Nvidia in 1993 and pioneered the graphics-chip market when that was non-obvious; in 2012, Ilya Sutskever trained AlexNet on Nvidia GPUs and created the deep-learning age; Huang re-oriented the company towards AI in the mid-2010s while none of his competitors were thinking that way; in 2020 he bought Mellanox for $7 billion — an Israeli company irrelevant to computer graphics but essential to AI compute, because it massively increased the bandwidth between GPUs, which is what makes high-performance AI server racks possible. More recently he was investing in open source, photonics and robotics, and had been a champion of most of America’s leading AI companies when they were unproven. “He made huge mistakes with his infidelity, which is tragic for his family and NVDA,” Maguire wrote. “But simultaneously he will leave a huge void, with the loss of a: technical, aggressive, founder-CEO.”

“The American AI community needs to work double-time in an attempt to fill this void.”Shaun Maguire, Sequoia, 12 November 2026

Maguire’s answer to the succession question is Nikesh Arora. “Now that Jensen is out, my pick for his successor would be Nikesh Arora,” he wrote, hours after the first post. Arora has run Palo Alto Networks for eight years, during which its stock rose roughly tenfold, and Maguire’s argument is that Arora is one of only a handful of people with the credibility to be aggressive on day one — plus being, in his words, a world-class dealmaker with the relationships to keep Nvidia at the centre of the AI ecosystem. It is not the only name in circulation: President Trump has reportedly suggested former Apple chief executive Tim Cook. The board, for its part, has said only that it will look inside and outside the company. The contrast with Apple is the point the Economic Times made and Maguire’s post implies: Apple had a pre-identified successor when Steve Jobs stepped down. Nvidia does not.

Perplexity’s Aravind Srinivas struck the simpler note: “Thank you @JensenHuang for 33 years of bold leadership. You built a small startup into America’s most valuable company, with relentless improvement. Your grit and perseverance should always be remembered.” Palmer Luckey, who has his own history with Beijing’s chip politics, went the other way entirely: “China wants Jensen out, so I want him in.” The Wall Street Journal added the detail that will follow Kress into her first weeks: in an August meeting, Huang summoned his top finance executives and tore into Kress’s presentation, calling her “a deer in the headlights” and a “reporting CFO” — not someone who could help define strategy. She now has to steer the company through the avalanche, as the Journal put it, and a bigger one: federal and internal investigations, a leadership vacuum, and an earnings call in five days.

Why the succession matters beyond one company is the part Tomasz Tunguz’s analysis captures best. For the past four years, he argues, Huang did not just run a chipmaker — he ran what he calls the central bank of AI. The numbers: about $42 billion in 2025–2026 mergers, acquisitions and tech buyouts; $61 billion of cash invested in equity across 229 private startups and 13 public companies; $164.5 billion in lease guarantees and cloud-capacity backstops. Last quarter, Nvidia deployed 112% of its operating cash flow into equity and acquisitions. That is not a supplier relationship. That is a balance sheet underwriting the entire AI buildout — and it was, until Thursday, one man’s judgement call. The market has been pricing the story in stages: the stock is down roughly 20% since Culper’s report in May ($225.83 then, $178.67 on 9 November), and it fell more than 10% in premarket trading on Thursday before the resignation was announced.

The takeawayFounder-CEO succession is the risk nobody prices until it happens, because while the founder is there it looks like a feature, not a risk. Nvidia’s transition exposes the gap: no named successor, an interim chief who keeps the finance job, a first-ever board chair, and an earnings call in five days as the first exam. Watch Tuesday — not for the numbers, which Kress has produced for thirteen years, but for whether she can answer the question the release did not: who is Nvidia now?

Claude Joins the Meeting — and Starts Working While You Talk

Anthropic (@ClaudeAI) • Launch post read in full, 12 November 2026 • Read the launch post • Original post

The shortest announcement of the week may be the most consequential for how work actually gets done. Anthropic’s Claude can now join your video call as a visible participant, follow the conversation, and start on the work while the meeting is still happening — building the doc, the whiteboard, the plan, before anyone has had time to write “as discussed” in an email. It is called Claude Meetings, it is in beta on Team and Enterprise plans, and the demo in the launch post — a one-minute video of Claude building artefacts live in a call — is the whole pitch in miniature: the meeting stops being a conversation about work and becomes the place the work happens.

The mechanics are simple enough to describe in one paragraph, which is the point. Someone adds Claude to the call, or it auto-joins. It appears as a participant and posts a notice in the meeting chat. During the call, anyone can ask it questions out loud or in chat; by default it answers in writing, and it can use connected tools with permission. Say “Claude, start a doc for this plan,” and it writes up what the team has said so far while teammates edit it live. Ask what last year’s event cost and it asks for approval to look it up, posts the figure in chat, and compares it with the current plan. Ask for a timeline and it puts it on a whiteboard. Before the meeting ends, someone asks for a recap — and afterwards the notes, action items, plan and whiteboard all sit on the meeting’s page in claude.ai, where anyone on the invite can revisit them or add Claude to the next one from the calendar.

The guardrails are where Anthropic has clearly thought hardest, because a silent participant that records everything is a trust problem before it is a product problem. Claude saves a transcript, a summary and screen descriptions — not audio. By default it stays out of calls when someone from outside your organisation is invited, and leaves if an outsider joins partway through; an admin can allow it on external calls, where it posts its notice but does not reply in chat, and what guests say is still included in the notes. It uses each person’s connected tools with that person’s permission, asking the first time and applying a yes going forward; when someone asks out loud, it asks each time to confirm who is speaking. And the launch post is blunt about the social contract: “Before you add Claude or turn on auto-join, make sure everyone on your calls is OK with being transcribed.” The beta is not available to organisations with zero data retention, a HIPAA-ready configuration or customer-managed encryption keys. Google Meet is the only platform at launch; support for others is coming, with no date named.

The pricing tells you how Anthropic is thinking about the land grab. Notes and transcripts are free on Team and Enterprise during the beta, as are Claude’s answers in calls — unless an answer uses a connected tool, which counts towards plan usage. The docs, whiteboards and other artefacts Claude makes in a call are free through 10 February 2027. Nothing is published about what any of it costs after that. Partnerships with Gong, Attention, Granola, Fireflies.ai, Otter and Plaud are in progress, so a meeting’s transcript will soon flow into the tools teams already use. The context matters: this arrived on 10 November as part of a package with Claude Whiteboards, Claude Sheets and Claude Data (now generally available) — Anthropic’s direct push into the office software long held by Microsoft and Google, weeks after OpenAI shipped tools that let ChatGPT visualise company data and build presentations. The meeting is the new battleground, and the prize is not the call. It is the record of the call, and everything built from it.

The takeawayNote-taking was the first AI meeting product because it was the safest. Building the work live is the second wave, and it changes the power dynamic: whoever owns the artefact owns the follow-up. The question to ask before turning it on is not whether Claude is accurate — it is whether your team wants its half-formed thinking turned into a document in real time, and who gets to edit it first.

India Inc’s Earnings Came Back Before the Market Noticed

Nithin Kamath (@Nithin0dha), charting IndiaDataHub • 12 November 2026 • Original post

Zerodha’s Nithin Kamath posted a chart this week with a one-line thesis that cuts through months of market gloom: “Even before the markets recovered, the earnings had recovered.” The chart tracks BSE-listed companies quarter by quarter from April–June 2023, and with results still coming in for July–September 2026, the rebound is broad — sales up 21.1% on a year earlier, EBITDA up 21.7%, and net profit up 29.7%. Sales, the chart’s headline, are growing at the fastest pace in three years.

Listed Indian companies, Jul–Sep 2026 — growth on a year earlier

The order of the numbers is the story. Sales first, then operating profit, then net profit growing fastest — the shape of a recovery where volumes return before margins, and margins return before the bottom line compounds. Kamath’s point is about sequencing: the market spent the year arguing about valuations while the underlying earnings base was quietly repairing itself. Two caveats belong with the chart. First, the figures are “so far” — results are still coming in for the quarter, so the final numbers will move. Second, a listed-companies aggregate is not the whole economy; it is the formal, listed slice of it, and the fastest sales growth in three years is a statement about that slice. But as a leading indicator of what the next few quarters of Indian earnings will look like, it is the most encouraging data point in this paper for weeks.

The takeawayMarkets price the future, but earnings pay for it. When sales, operating profit and net profit are all rebounding together — and profits fastest — the argument that Indian equities are expensive on trailing numbers starts to weaken. Watch the rest of the results season: if the “so far” numbers hold, the narrative flips from “expensive market” to “earnings catching up”.

The Jev Revolution: Automate Everything, Reliably

Richard MacManus, Latent Space • 12 November 2026 • Read the original

At this week’s AI Engineer Code Summit in San Francisco, the theme was not the biggest model or the longest reasoning chain. It was the smallest one: system one models — non-generative models that return a structured choice, a score or a probability, instead of a paragraph. They are typically much faster and cheaper than reasoning LLMs, and the product that kicked the trend off less than two months ago is Jev, from Diogo Almeida’s TypeSafe AI. Almeida, who led Jev’s creation, took the keynote and turned it into a rallying cry: Jev is “reliable intelligence” that should be used for “just automating everything”.

The core problem, as Almeida put it in Latent Space’s launch podcast and again on stage, is reliability. A reasoning model is, in his words, “Einstein 95% of the time, but Mr. Bean 5% of the time” — and “if you can’t tell which is which, how the fuck are you going to automate anything”. His answer is to integrate system one calls directly into application code: “code is the language of automation, therefore you need to integrate with code.” Latent Space’s swyx translates that as liberally sprinkling system one model calls everywhere inside application code to make smarter software, instead of increasingly convoluted forms of stringing prompts together and hoping for the best. Almeida’s promise is deliberately grand: TypeSafe AI wants Jev to be “as reliable as a fourth logic gate”, and he closed by calling on “the dreamers and the people who don’t want to be limited by everything that has happened so far”.

The conference floor showed what that looks like in practice, and the examples are the useful part. Glean’s Chinmay Goyal explained that Jev sits alongside LLMs and other classifiers, and is especially suited to “zero-shot flexibility”: as the taxonomy of the decision space changes, Glean does not have to fine-tune models again and again. His customers, he said, do not need to know which type of model is running — “Jev powers a significant number of flows [but] the user just needs to have a product that works.” UiPath’s Chibi Vikram and Kevin Bernstein ran a tournament in which Jev picked the most relevant document chunks from small groups, winners advancing round by round — the whole process took under a second, even with thousands of Jev calls. “We realized that Jev is a building block that we can adjust,” Vikram said, “and we have to find these new strategies where we will be able to apply this building block. It might look less intelligent on its own, but with these strategies and patterns, we can get more intelligent — with even the current Jev model that we have.” And Workday’s Scott Roberts showed a prototype in which an interface starts filling in a time-off card while the user is still typing — going, as he put it, “from predicting what you want to say to predicting what you want to do”.

System one vs system two — the split that matters

Almeida’s closing comparison is the one to carry away: he likened the opportunities to the experimentation of the early internet. “I think that there’s going to be an explosion of what software can do, and we will want people to be exploring those boundaries of software. Not necessarily because it’s valuable, but because they can, because we have new frontiers to explore, and this is what the early internet was like.” A conference attendee sitting next to the author had already done it in his spare time: a website that sorts and ranks fantasy-football news, built with Jev. That is the tell. When a new primitive is real, the first things built with it are toys. The second wave is infrastructure. Glean, UiPath and Workday are already in the second wave.

The takeawayThe agent era’s bottleneck was never intelligence — it was reliability at the point of decision. System one models turn the model from a brilliant, occasionally erratic colleague into a component you can call like a function. The builders to watch are not the ones with the biggest prompts, but the ones sprinkling small, fast, deterministic calls through their code — and discovering that software can start doing things before the user finishes asking.

Network Density: Why “Bigger” Is the Wrong Question About Zomato, Swiggy and Urban Company

The Loggical Investor • 12 November 2026 • Read the original

Spend enough time listening to investors talk about new-age Indian businesses, and the conversation follows a script: the company reports losses, someone declares the model unsustainable, the cash burn excessive, the profitability too distant, and the discussion ends before it begins. The Loggical Investor’s latest deep dive asks whether the discussion is ending in the wrong place — not because losses do not matter, but because the framework doing the judging was built for a different kind of business. The concept at the centre of the piece is network density, and it is the most useful lens in this paper for anyone trying to value Zomato, Swiggy, Urban Company, Delhivery or Rapido.

The distinction starts with a simple separation the author insists on: a reported loss is a fact; calling the business model unsustainable is an interpretation. High cash burn is a fact; assuming every additional rupee invested will destroy shareholder value is an assumption. Revenue growth is a fact; whether that growth is improving the underlying economics requires a much deeper investigation. The companies he names — Eternal, Meesho, CarTrade — have demonstrated that businesses difficult to understand through conventional lenses can still create substantial shareholder value. “Sometimes, the opportunity is not necessarily difficult to identify because the business is complicated; it is difficult to identify because we are trying to understand it using the wrong framework.”

Network density is the framework. Take a food-delivery business in one city: 500 customers, 100 restaurants, 50 delivery partners spread across neighbourhoods. On paper, a reasonably large network. In practice, the nearest available partner might be three kilometres away while another sits idle elsewhere, restaurants get irregular orders, and partners spend their time travelling without earning. Now concentrate the same resources in a few neighbourhoods: more customers ordering from nearby restaurants, more regular orders, more partners operating in the same area — and suddenly a partner who completed five orders an hour can complete ten, with no new hiring, no new hours, no new infrastructure. The network has not grown much. The economic activity inside it has. That is density: how closely connected and economically active the participants are, not how many of them are registered. One million registered customers means little if few of them transact; what matters is how many return, how frequently, and how efficiently the platform fulfils them.

Sparse network vs dense network — same size, different economics

The piece then walks the concept across four businesses, and the differences are the lesson. For Zomato and Swiggy, geography is the variable: transactions concentrated in a delivery zone are worth more than the same number scattered nationally, because partners complete more deliveries per hour, travel less, and orders can be batched along similar routes. For Urban Company, density shows up in a professional’s working day: fewer hours travelling between appointments means more monetised hours, and the company’s own annual report tracks it — monetised active hours per professional rose from 59 a month in FY22 to 83 in FY25 and 89 in the first half of FY26, attributed largely to densification. For Rapido, the same captain network can serve bike rides, autos, cabs and hyperlocal deliveries, spreading supply infrastructure across demand types — though the author is careful that supply growing faster than demand hurts captain earnings just as surely as the reverse hurts waiting times. For Delhivery, density is about shipment concentration across routes, sorting centres and gateways: fuller vehicles, better-utilised facilities, fixed costs spread over more shipments. Different models, one principle: make existing infrastructure more productive.

The framework comes with its own guardrails, and the author states them plainly. Density and network effects are related but not interchangeable: a denser network improves efficiency even when adding a participant does not make the platform more valuable to every other participant. Density is no guarantee — a crowded network can congest, too many professionals chasing too little demand hurts their earnings, and transactions bought with unsustainable discounts destroy economics rather than improve them. And the investor’s questions follow from the framework rather than the P&L: are customers returning more frequently? Is transaction density improving? Are delivery partners or professionals completing more productive hours? Is the cost of fulfilling an additional transaction declining? Are mature markets becoming contribution-margin positive? Can the economics be replicated in a new market? A company operating in 100 cities with weak local economics may be a worse business than one in 20 cities with high repeat usage and improving margins — geographical expansion looks impressive in a presentation, but the quality of the underlying market economics tells you much more. The closing line is the one to keep: “Revenue growth tells us that the business is getting bigger. Network density helps us understand whether the business is getting better.”

The takeawayDo not judge a network business by its registered users or its city count. Judge it by what happens inside a single neighbourhood: how fast a partner is matched, how many productive hours a professional bills, how much cheaper the next transaction gets. That is where the value is created — usually long before it shows up in the profit and loss statement.

The Hardest Project Management Job in the World, Done With ChatGPT

Peter Yang, Behind the Craft • 4 November 2026 • Read the original

The most human piece in today’s paper is also the most practical demonstration of what AI is actually for. Peter Yang’s mother has had cancer three times before, and it is back. His response was not to use AI to research the disease — though he does that too — but to do what a product manager does with any chaotic, multi-stakeholder problem: impose structure on it. He calls it PMing his mom’s care, and the essay lays out five steps any patient or caregiver can copy, built around a free skill he published on GitHub. Two in five people in the US will face cancer in their lifetime, he notes, and when you include caregivers, almost no family is left untouched.

Step one is the one that changes everything else: a one-page brief. Yang built a skill — install it by pasting a line into ChatGPT or Claude — that produces a concise, fact-based brief with five sections: patient information and essential contacts; what to do next, the top three actions or questions for the doctor; what we know, confirmed facts only; medical terms in plain English; and a care log, a dated history of updates. Having AI update the brief before every appointment has been critical, he writes, in asking the doctors the right questions. A reader’s line in the piece says it best: “You have to be a huge patient advocate. Push for answers. Push for biopsies. Push for proper testing. Ask questions.” Step two is the unglamorous prerequisite: organise the records early, in a shared drive, because a brief is only as good as the data behind it. Written records, imaging files (CT and PET scans, usually delivered as DICOM files that hospitals can take two or three weeks to hand over on a USB drive or even a CD-ROM), and pathology blocks or slides of the actual tumour — the most precious material, because it is what sequencing and second opinions need, and the longest to obtain. His mother is still waiting on a pathology block from a prior cancer.

Step three is research, done in the long wait that is typical of a cancer diagnosis. It has been three months since his mother’s first CT scan revealed a tumour, and she still does not know whether it is a breast cancer recurrence or something else. The path is standard — suspicious scan, follow-up scans, biopsy, a tumour board review over Zoom with the oncologist, radiologist, surgeon and other specialists, then treatment under NCCN guidelines, usually some combination of chemotherapy, radiation, surgery or immunotherapy — but the waiting is where a caregiver can act. Understand standard care and get a second opinion: at a major centre like MD Anderson, MSK, Dana-Farber, Stanford or UCSF. Yang is seeking one from UCSF, his mother being treated in Canada, and says that without ChatGPT it would have taken him forever to build the 90-page records packet the request required. Sequence the tumour: cancer is more than a hundred different diseases, and sequencing — around $4,000 through BostonGene in his case, needing the pathology block, taking weeks — looks for DNA and RNA markers that can point to better treatments. “The more data you have on the tumor, the better.” And research clinical trials on ClinicalTrials.gov, re-running the search weekly or monthly, while remembering that early-phase trials are unproven and can make things worse.

Steps four and five are about people. Sharing his mother’s story publicly brought offers of help he could not have bought: Ari from OpenCancer, who made introductions for testing and pathology review; Bayli from the Sijbrandij Foundation, who helped prepare questions about diagnosis, tissue testing and treatment options; oncologists including Dr. Odabashian and Dr. Bernstein, who offered their perspectives. He keeps them in the loop with short updates drafted with ChatGPT after every appointment. “Many families facing cancer keep it to themselves. I’ve found that sharing the journey online or even just with friends empowers other people to help.” And step five is the one he is most honest about: protect quality time. He took a week to stay with his mother when the cancer came back — walks, meals, chats he will cherish — and calls his parents every day so they can talk to his daughters. The essay’s sharpest sentence is its simplest: “Time with your loved ones is easy to postpone because there’s rarely a deadline attached to it.” You know when your next performance review is; you can measure your salary and your follower count. It is harder to see what you lose when work takes another night from your kids, or when you keep putting off a trip with your parents.

“Time with your loved ones is easy to postpone because there’s rarely a deadline attached to it.”Peter Yang, Behind the Craft

The piece is not a medical guide, and it does not pretend to be — it is a systems answer to a human problem, from a man who builds AI tools for a living and discovered that the most useful thing his tools could do was keep the paperwork straight, the questions sharp and the family close. For a reader who thinks in systems, the transferable lesson is the brief: a single page, five sections, updated before every meeting, turning scattered records into questions a doctor can answer. That is a template worth stealing for any high-stakes, multi-party problem — and a reminder that the best use of AI is often not the cleverest one, but the one that gives a family its evenings back.

The takeawayWhen the stakes are highest, AI’s job is not to be clever — it is to be organised. A one-page brief, records requested early, a second opinion started a month before you need it, and time with family protected like a deadline. If you are caring for someone, start with the brief. Everything else follows from it.
