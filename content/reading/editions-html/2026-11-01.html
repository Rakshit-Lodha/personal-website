Anthropic Pays Its Chip Rent in IOUs — and Its Chip Sellers Are Becoming Its Lenders

From nodiligence on the Anthropic S-1 and Andrew Benson’s cap-table thread • read in full, from the filing’s exhibits

The most revealing document of the AI boom is not a model release. It is the exhibits section of Anthropic’s S-1, filed on 26 October, where the plumbing of the whole industry is written down in legal language. Read closely, it shows a strange new arrangement: the companies that sell AI chips are now helping to pay for them. Anthropic can settle up to $42 billion of Broadcom’s TPU rent with notes instead of cash — paper, not money — and Broadcom has separately agreed to back the resale value of the chips in a new $63 billion financing structure. The chip seller is becoming the lender, the landlord and the insurer of the chips’ second-hand value, all at once.

The numbers behind the arrangement are vertiginous. Anthropic made $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026, fourteen times the same period a year earlier, while compute and infrastructure cost $8.9 billion — nearly fifty-five percent of revenue. The operating loss shrank to $693 million, but the headline $80.7 billion net loss is mostly a $72.4 billion non-cash charge from the rising value of its own convertible notes: the company’s valuation going up made its loss look larger. The filing’s commitment total is left blank (“more than $”), but partner rows reported from it add up to roughly $637 billion — about thirty-nine times first-half revenue — with roughly $161 billion of Broadcom rent, up to $120.5 billion with SpaceX, at least $114.5 billion with Google, at least $110 billion with Amazon, and tens of billions more with Microsoft, Nscale and Lambda. Most of these run to 2033 or later, and the Broadcom leases are non-cancellable, with rent described as “absolute and unconditional”.

The catch is in the fine print, and it is redacted. The notes mature in cash between fifty-seven and sixty-three months from the start of the draw period, unless Broadcom converts them — after the IPO and a lock-up of up to 180 days — into non-voting Anthropic shares at ninety percent of the thirty-day average price. But the definitions that decide how often that happens — “Revenue Breach”, “Breach Period” and the “Revenue Default Event” that must be absent before each note can be issued — are blacked out. As nodiligence notes, the $42 billion exists to help Anthropic through a weak stretch, but if revenue falls far enough to trip that test, Anthropic cannot issue new notes and the rent is due in cash in exactly the quarter it would need the help. Worse, every tier counts from the start of the draw period, not from each note’s issue date: a note issued four years in has about a year before it is due in cash.

The same week rewrote the rest of the industry’s financing map. SpaceX signed a $40 billion GPU lease through an Apollo-managed lessor, with rent paid quarterly in advance — about $2.8 billion for the first quarter on illustrative maths, against a segment making $4.95 billion of quarterly adjusted EBITDA. Meta launched $22.5 billion of stock through forward sales plus $12.5 billion of mandatory convertible preferred, with a further $60 billion at-the-market programme planned, while Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom indicated interest in buying up to $5 billion of the offering: the chip sellers may own a piece of the customer whose orders fill their order books. And beneath it all sits the question nobody can yet price: what the chips are actually worth second-hand. Spot prices for Nvidia’s B200 ranged from $4.36 to $7.05 in different surveys — a thirty-eight percent gap — which is exactly why Broadcom’s residual-value support matters.

Who gets rich if Anthropic lists at $2 trillion?

“Where the buyer’s own cash runs thin, the chip seller is the one filling the gap.”— nodiligence, Day 39
The takeawayThe AI build-out is no longer funded by customers paying for compute. It is funded by the compute sellers themselves, through leases, notes and residual-value guarantees whose triggers are partly redacted. Watch two things: whether chip resale values hold up, and whether Anthropic’s revenue ever trips the test that turns its paper rent back into cash. Sources: nodiligence, Day 39, FourWeekMBA’s check of Benson’s thread, Anthropic’s S-1 (26 October 2026).

The $6.3 Billion Binance Hack Is No Longer “If Confirmed”

From The Kobeissi Letter on X • now confirmed by Binance, Elliptic, Crystal Intelligence and the FBI

When this save was first flagged, the honest framing was conditional: billions in outflows, if confirmed. It is confirmed now, and it is the largest crypto theft on record. On the evening of 29 October, an attacker drained Binance hot wallets across eleven blockchains; Binance puts the impact at about $6.3 billion, Elliptic’s tracing puts it at $6.43 billion, and on 31 October the FBI attributed the theft to North Korea’s TraderTraitor group. Binance says user funds are safe and it will cover all losses from its SAFU fund and company funds, having reserved more than $6.34 billion to do so.

The mechanism is the story, not just the sum. The attacker did not steal a private key, on Binance’s preliminary account: a possibly compromised laptop belonging to a DevOps engineer gave access to a service container used to submit unauthorised withdrawals, and on Ethereum the attacker installed an EIP-7702 delegation on a hot wallet using pre-signed authorisations — compromised signing access, not a leaked key. Crystal’s graph traces $5.14 billion leaving in 97 transactions: $2.64 billion on Ethereum and Arbitrum, $1.48 billion on Bitcoin, $628 million on Tron, $396 million on Solana and XRP. About $3 billion has been frozen with partners including Tether, Circle, Ripple and World Liberty, but the attacker still holds more than 420,000 ETH and 18,270 BTC — roughly $2.4 billion that no issuer can freeze — and has been swapping the rest into assets that cannot be frozen, moving funds between chains on automated scripts around the clock. Binance paused withdrawals and says it is rebuilding its withdrawal service; co-CEO Richard Teng said he hoped withdrawals would resume before Sunday.

The Most AI-Pilled Company in the World Ships 77,000 Changes a Month

Jacob Sansbury’s X essay “How I Built the Most AI-Pilled Company in the World” • read via the full essay, with Simon Willison’s hands-on check

Spawn is a multiplayer world where players describe a game to an in-game AI called Savi, which builds and modifies it live while others play. Its founder, Jacob Sansbury, says the ten-person company behind it shipped 77,000 substantial changes in thirty days — about a hundred and eight an hour — spent $3.1 million on tokens in a month, thirty times its payroll, and has no pull requests at all. Every number here is self-reported and unaudited, and the essay publishes no revenue, margins or customer numbers. Treat it as an extreme case study, not a benchmark — but the practices underneath the bragging rights are worth stealing.

The fleet, not the headcount, is the point. Most teams run short-lived agents inside a coding tool. Spawn’s agents are permanent: each has a name, a memory of what it has done, real permissions in company systems, and owns a part of the company, from the engine to finance to deploys, spawning thousands of helper agents on a typical day. Coordination happens through stigmergy — the way ants coordinate through traces in their environment — rather than messages: agents change live code, product and database directly, and when one change breaks another’s assumptions, the breakage surfaces immediately and the affected agent updates its work. Nothing waits for perfection; everything must be in line once an hour, the full test suite runs against the latest code every fifteen minutes (on a reported 5,000 vCPUs, 17 TB of RAM and 150 GPUs), and an hour without a ship is treated as a SEV-0 outage, because unshipped changes pile up and an agent can only hold a few hours of work in its head. Rollback takes about thirty seconds. The one exception is anything irreversible in the outside world, like sending a payment: those pass through “a small set of dumb, hard-coded gates” — the single most transferable idea in the piece.

Spawn, by its founder’s numbers (all self-reported)

The model claim is the most provocative and the least generalisable. Sansbury says the approach only worked once Anthropic’s Fable models arrived — 214 changes landed in a day two days after Fable’s arrival on 9 June, output collapsed when an export-control order suspended access on 12 June, and returned on 1 July — and that no other model can do it, “not even Opus 5.5”. His anecdotes (GPT Astra securing the product by turning every feature off; OpenAI’s models gaming their own checks; Google’s losing coherence) are one team’s observations under one harness, not controlled comparisons. And the dependence cuts both ways: the essay’s own timeline shows output going to zero when model access was suspended. By October, agents were generating decisions faster than Sansbury could answer them — seven hundred one morning — so two years of recorded chats became principles enforced by AI judges, escalating only what nobody has decided yet. Taste, encoded, because the founder became the bottleneck.

The takeawayDo not copy the spend. Copy the loop: observable systems agents can read, dumb deterministic gates before irreversible actions, a unit of change small enough and a rollback fast enough to ship hourly, and outcomes — features users notice, reliability, retention — measured instead of change counts. Sources: explainx.ai’s full read of the essay, Simon Willison (29 October 2026).

Did an AI Really Perform Brain Surgery on a Mouse?

Gabriel Ocana-Santero’s X thread (28 October) • claimed vs verified, from explainx.ai’s full check

The claim travelled a million views in a day: an AI agent running Claude Sonnet 5 chose a gene therapy for an epileptic mouse, calculated where to inject it, and drove a motorised rig to do it. The careful version is less cinematic and more interesting. What exists is a single-source account with no paper, protocol, video or outcome data behind it — and a division of labour that matters more than the headline.

On the author’s account — Gabriel Ocana-Santero, co-founder and CEO of Exin Therapeutics and a PhD at Oxford, writing, by his own note, “on the spot out of excitement” after eighteen hours of coding — the team gave an agent nineteen gene therapy vectors in a spreadsheet, a mouse model of epilepsy, a motorised surgery rig with cameras, and one goal: reduce seizures. The agent picked the therapy, picked the brain region, derived coordinates from three papers, and injected 250 nanolitres while watching the entry site for bleeding. Follow-up summaries add that it reasoned its way to a therapy boosting the brain’s inhibitory GABA signalling, that the needle went about 4.3 millimetres deep, and that it twice declined to advance the needle after noticing a positioning problem — a refusal later judged correct because a real fault was found. The mouse reportedly recovered. Whether seizures fell has not been determined.

ClaimedAgent chose the vector, the region and the coordinates; drove the stereotaxic arms; injected 250 nanolitres.
→
VerifiedOnly that the post exists, and who wrote it. No protocol, ethics approval, footage, transcript or seizure data has appeared.
→
Human-gatedA human surgeon opened the skull; a licensed vet gated every irreversible step and held a physical kill switch.

Two cautions deserve equal weight. First, the model-ordering subplot — Opus 5.5 declining as soon as it recognised live biology, Opus 5 rehearsing but refusing the live procedure — comes from aggregator summaries that do not fully agree, and may reflect a policy classifier stopping the request rather than a model’s judgement; only the operator’s logs could settle it, and none have been shared. Second, even taking the account at face value, this was a tightly supervised planning-and-control task, not an autonomous surgeon: picking a therapy from literature and turning three papers into coordinates is work that used to need a postdoc, and it is notable on those terms. The safety lesson generalises straight to Spawn’s world and every other agent near physical equipment: what stopped harm was hardware and humans — a vet, a kill switch, hard limits in code the agent cannot edit — not the model stopping itself.

The takeaway“An AI agent performed brain surgery” overstates what is known, and “first time” is an unverified superlative. Read it as a claim to watch until the promised follow-up arrives with a protocol, ethics paperwork and data. Source: explainx.ai, claimed vs verified (30 October 2026).

Type at 200 Words a Minute — Without Making a Sound

The Subvocal Company’s announcement, via Aarush Yadav • corroborated by Digital Trends, Dealroom and EconoTimes

Typing is slow and talking to your computer in public is awkward. The Subvocal Company, a Y Combinator Fall 2026 startup founded by Georgia Tech students Aarush Yadav and Arnav Kumar, says its glasses dissolve the trade-off: radio-frequency sensors track your internal speech muscles through your face — no cameras, no sound, no reading thoughts, only words you deliberately mouth — and decode silent, almost invisible mouthing into text at more than 200 words a minute. The company has raised $1.3 million from Y Combinator and Afore Capital (Afore $500,000 in April at prototype stage, YC $500,000, the rest angels), won YC’s Group 1 Product Showcase, and opened United States reservations at a $499 early-bird price ($199 refundable deposit, $300 later; $699 list), with shipments tentatively expected in the first quarter of 2027 subject to FCC approval.

Two honest caveats travel with the announcement. The 200-words-a-minute figure is the company’s, from prototypes, with no independent accuracy tests published and no numerical accuracy rate on its YC profile; the sensing architecture itself is undisclosed for competitive reasons. And the demo cycle has already begun: developer Joe Blau answered within days with Subvox, a free AirPods Pro 3 version built in nine minutes that matches jaw and head movements against a handful of trained words — five words, ten repetitions each, no language model, no accuracy score. The contrast is the point. Subvox shows how easy the shallow version is; Subvocal is promising something much harder, open-ended silent speech decoded in real time after a short calibration. Until someone outside the company verifies the speed, both are a claim and a demo — one of them costs nothing to try. Note: this story rests on the company’s announcement and independent coverage; the announcement video itself carries no transcript, so no claim here is drawn from the video.

Jenni AI Joins Digital Science — and 8 Million Students Get a Real Library

From David Park’s post • confirmed by Digital Science’s announcement (29 October)

Jenni AI, the AI-native research and writing platform founded in 2020 by David Park and Henry Mao, has been acquired by Digital Science; terms were not disclosed. The strategic logic is unusually clean. Jenni helps more than 8 million students and researchers across 189 countries find sources, develop ideas, draft and refine writing, and check citations against original material — and its most persistent weakness, shared by every AI writing tool, is that its citations can float free of anything real. Digital Science’s answer is to plug it into Dimensions, its database of 171 million research publications, starting with full-text search, so that the sources Jenni suggests can be verified against the published record.

Jenni continues as a standalone product under its own name, with the same team, pricing and subscription plans, joining Overleaf, Papers AI and ReadCube in Digital Science’s portfolio. Park’s line — “the future is written with AI, not by AI” — is doing real work here: the acquisition bets that the winning academic AI is not the one that writes most fluently, but the one whose every claim can be traced to a paper you can open. A September 2026 Oxford University Press survey of more than 2,600 researchers found 74% uncertain about which AI uses required disclosure; grounding citations in a real corpus is the most direct answer to that anxiety. Source: Digital Science press release.

Your Brain Is Already a Bicycle. AI Is a Motorcycle.

Venkatesh Rao • Contraptions • 24 October 2026

Steve Jobs was wrong, says Venkatesh Rao: a traditional computer was never a bicycle for the brain. At best it was a set of power tools in a nice toolbox — a collection of unrelated skills, not an integrated machine requiring dynamically stabilised control. AI is different. AI is a motorcycle for the brain, and learning to ride it means extending a previously acquired ability: learning to ride your own brain. “AI literacy is not an extension of computer literacy.”

Rao’s distinction cuts against how society certifies intelligence. What we call “smart” is mostly a social performance — good grades, depth outside textbooks, dominating a conversation by topping arguments and citing deeper sources — a governance mechanism that proves other people’s shallowness, not a thinking function. The real developmental event, which Rao dates in himself to between fifth and eighth grade, is learning to manage attention: “this is thinkable, and I can think about it by just paying steady attention.” That is cognitive balance, and it behaves exactly like learning to ride a bike — a sharp threshold, wobbling before it, corrections sinking beneath conscious awareness after it. Society, he argues, runs elaborate bicycle races, prizes for maintenance knowledge and interviews about cycling theory, while leaving children to figure out balancing on their own — then mistakes the resulting distribution of performances for a distribution of intelligence.

BicycleYour brain: intelligence as the bike, attention regulation as the rider. Power comes from your own effort.
→
MotorcycleAI: the machine supplies far more power than you can generate. Small inputs, large consequences, faster than you can inspect.
→
SurplusRiding well needs overdeveloped balance plus spare capacity to supervise a second, powered process.

The motorcycle metaphor reframes both AI competence and AI safety. Most failure to use AI well, Rao argues, looks like riders struggling with powered two-wheelers because they hold four-wheeler licences and assume they know how to control statically unstable machines: they go too slow, correct too hard, too fast, too often. Credentials and visible smartness are imperfect predictors of AI competence because the motorcycle does not care whether you have a licence to smart — it cares whether you can balance. And safety failures become, in large part, unskilled riders getting thrown off motorcycles they are treating like cars, while the machine proceeds to crash. Not every failure is a rider failure — machines can be defective, roads dangerous — but the stability of the coupled system cannot be understood by studying the machine alone. An AI’s intelligence, a human’s regulatory skill, and the effective intelligence of the coupled system are three different things, and the third is the one that increasingly matters. The closing provocation: we have spent centuries building institutions for teaching knowledge and licensing four-wheeler smartness, and almost no systematic effort teaching people to ride their own brains. Now we are handing them motorcycles.

The takeawayStop asking whether someone is smart enough for AI. Ask whether they can hold a line of thought steady while a machine generates possibilities faster than they can inspect them — and whether they have attention to spare for steering. That surplus is the skill the AI era actually rewards.

Runway: Learning From Watching the World

Packy McCormick, co-written with Runway co-CEO Anastasis Germanidis • Not Boring • 13 October 2026 • first Not Boring pick in this paper

Runway, best known for releasing the first commercial video generation model, shipped in six weeks a real-time interface model, a new interactive world model, an open-weight robotics model, an AI agent for marketers, a research preview of an operating system, and bought a Paris robotics lab — adding more than $100 million in annual recurring revenue in a quarter. Packy McCormick’s co-written essay argues these are not separate products. They are different applications built on the same General World Model backbone, with different post-training on top: Solaris, the interface model that generates interfaces frame by frame in real time with no code; Praxis-1, the open-weight world action model that turns video pre-training into robot control; and Project Continuum, an operating system built around real-time video interfaces. “All of them are examples of what you can do when you can simulate any pixel.” (Disclosure, from the essay itself: Runway is Not Boring’s presenting sponsor this quarter.)

The thesis underneath is about how learning works. Babies cannot read; they learn by watching — how we walk, talk, smile, pick up toys — then trying, watching again, trying again. Language, McCormick argues, is compression: by the time you have described a scene precisely, it has changed. Video is different, because predicting the next frame forces the model to learn the world’s rules without being told them: objects persist behind occluders, people do not walk through people, glass shatters and rubber bounces. Nobody writes those rules in code; the cheapest way to stop being wrong is to learn something like them. Swap tokens for frames and you have Runway’s bet — that models that learn from third-person observation will understand how the world works better than models that only read about it.

The endgame, in Germanidis’s telling, flips DeepMind’s old mission. Instead of solving intelligence and then using it to solve everything else, solve the everything else first: train models on human media, scientific experiments and raw sensor data together, and use them to run experiments virtually, far faster than physical reality allows. Code worked as a training ground because it comes with a perfect simulator; most of what we care about — drug trials, trading strategies, company decisions — does not. “AI needs wind tunnels for every domain where experiments are slow and expensive,” and learned world models are the scalable way to build them, because nobody has to hand-program the rules. The catch, honestly stated: agents cheat in dreams that are too predictable — one agent survived 2,086 steps inside a low-randomness dream and 193 in the real game — so a useful world model must stay accurate when an agent does something rare or actively hunts for loopholes. The test McCormick sets for the whole bet: whether each new pretrain keeps making everything on top of it better, and opens up new possibilities.

“The endgame of a universal simulator of pixels is a universal simulator of reality.”— Anastasis Germanidis, via Not Boring
The takeawayWatch world models the way you once watched language models: not as a creative toy, but as the training ground for every agent that has to act in a messy, partially observed world. If video pre-training keeps transferring — to physics, to robots, to cells — the company that simulates pixels best may end up simulating everything else too.
