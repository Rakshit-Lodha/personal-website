The $64 Billion Roadshow: Anthropic Sets Its IPO Price

StockMKTNewz, Bearly AI & samuel • 10 November 2026 • Original posts

Anthropic has put a number on itself at last. On Monday the company filed the amended prospectus for its listing: 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78 each, raising $57.8 to $64.4 billion before the banks’ option for a further 123.75 million shares. On top of that, seven of the biggest names in chips and cloud — Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC — have agreed to buy $26 billion of non-voting shares at the IPO price in a concurrent private placement. The price is set on 18 November; trading starts the next day on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. At the top of the range the company is worth about $1.02 trillion on the shares outstanding, and roughly $1.26 to $1.41 trillion fully diluted.

The numbers are the largest of their kind, but the more interesting document is the roadshow video. Dario Amodei’s closing pitch, clipped by Bearly AI, is not about revenue at all. “If today we look at things like cancer, Alzheimer’s, heart disease, mental illness. These will be relics of the past in, I think, less than a decade,” he says. “I believe that the company which gets the technology first that is capable of doing that has both the largest commercial opportunity in history and also the largest impact on humanity in history.” It is a pitch that asks investors to underwrite a moral claim as much as a business: whoever cures disease first wins everything.

That framing is doing real work in the filing. The $26 billion placement is not just money; it is the supply chain buying into its own customer. Google and Nvidia are putting in $10 billion each, AMD $2.5 billion, Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft $1 billion each, TSMC $500 million. These are the companies that sell Anthropic its chips and rent it its compute, now becoming shareholders at the IPO price. And control stays where it has always been: a single Class F share, held by an entity of the co-founders, carries a fixed majority of the vote. The public buys economics; the founders keep the steering wheel.

The deal, at the $74 midpoint

Then came the reaction that will follow this IPO everywhere it goes. Elon Musk, who has spent years as Anthropic’s most prominent critic on policy, praised Dario’s speech in Washington as “excellent” and endorsed the uncomfortable part of it: that the most powerful AI systems will be built by a small number of companies, and the gap between the frontier labs and everyone else will be enormous. Samuel’s post, which Rakshit liked, spells out the dread underneath the agreement: the world splitting into two groups, those with access to the most powerful AI and those without, with a gap “so large that it’s going to be impossible to catch up.” The post repeats the line a dozen times, as if saying it once were not enough. It is the same argument Anthropic’s own filing makes in financial form: five labs can afford this game, and the list is shrinking.

“The company which gets the technology first… has both the largest commercial opportunity in history and also the largest impact on humanity in history.”Dario Amodei, Anthropic IPO roadshow

Watch the 18th. The price will tell you what the market thinks Anthropic is worth; the placement will tell you what its suppliers think; and the roadshow pitch will tell you what Dario thinks the money is actually for. If the range holds, this is the largest IPO ever — and the first one whose prospectus is, in effect, a bet on curing disease within a decade.

OpenRouter Officially Joins Stripe

Deedy • 2:27 AM, 11 November 2026 • Original post

Deedy’s post is short and personal — a dinner-table photo, six people, and one line: “OpenRouter is now officially acquired by Stripe today!! A huge outcome for a small, mighty team. What a privilege to be a tiny part of the journey of this incredible company.” Behind it sits one of the sharpest deals of the AI infrastructure boom. OpenRouter started in a dorm room, raised no venture capital at all, and became the switchboard for frontier AI: one API key routing more than 100 billion tokens a day for 7 million developers and 100,000 products.

The deal was agreed on 19 August at a reported $7 billion-plus — Bloomberg said more than $7 billion, the New York Times about $7.5 billion — and today’s announcement is the closing of it. The multiple tells the story: OpenRouter had raised a $113 million Series B in May 2026 at a $1.3 billion valuation. Three months later Stripe paid roughly five times that. Patrick Collison’s line in the announcement was that “tokens are the central currency for companies building with AI.” Stripe already processed OpenRouter’s payments; now it owns the routing layer those payments are for.

OpenRouter says nothing changes for users: same name, same product, same roadmap, same API keys and balances, and routing decisions still driven by what is best for the user. That promise is the whole asset — a gateway is only valuable if developers trust it to be neutral between the models it routes to. The honest read is that neutrality now survives only as long as it stays commercially convenient for its new owner, which also sells usage-based billing and agentic commerce products of its own. For Rakshit, who has followed this company since the dorm-room days, the lesson is the one the deal itself demonstrates: in the AI economy, the toll booth can be worth more than the road.

The takeaway Zero venture capital, 100 billion tokens a day, and a $7 billion-plus exit in three years: OpenRouter is the purest example yet that in AI, whoever controls the routing and the billing controls the margin — and Stripe just bought both.

Software Is Dead. Distribution Is the Whole Game.

Andrew Wilkinson • 7:27 AM, 11 November 2026 • Original post

Andrew Wilkinson’s post reads like an obituary written by someone who owns the funeral home. “Software is dead. We’re a bunch of portrait painters and the camera just came out.” His estimate: in five years, most software will be free; in ten, all of it. What survives is not the code but the customer: “The last people standing in software are the people who have the best distribution. If you don’t own a niche and love its people, you’re dead. All that’s left: great products that own their customers. That’s it. That’s the whole game.”

The analogy is doing more work than it first appears. Portrait painters did not disappear when the camera arrived; they became photographers, then filmmakers, then something else entirely. The camera did not kill the image — it killed the scarcity of the image. Wilkinson’s argument is the same about code: AI has collapsed the cost of producing software toward zero, so the scarce thing is no longer the ability to build, but the right to be the one a specific group of people trusts and returns to. The residual value he concedes — zombie subscriptions, old people who don’t switch, corporations paying a fortune for things that should be free — is a list of businesses living on inertia, and inertia is a wasting asset.

What AI does to the value chain

It is worth holding this post next to two others in today’s pile. Lenny’s job-market report (in the Substacks below) finds the same shift from the hiring side: PM openings up 75% from their low, engineering demand stalled, and “LLM” now the most mentioned skill in product-manager job descriptions. And the klöss image in the Fable 5.2 cluster says it in plainer language still: “Own your distribution. Rent nothing you can’t lose,” and “Taste is the moat.” Three different posts, one conclusion: the build is commoditised, the audience is not.

The takeaway If Wilkinson is even half right, the question for any builder stops being “what can I build?” and becomes “whose problem do I own, and do they love me for it?” That is a much harder question — and a much better moat.

ChatGPT Just Became a High Street

Greg Isenberg • 1:51 AM, 11 November 2026 • Original post • OpenAI’s announcement read in full

OpenAI has let every business claim a profile inside ChatGPT, and Greg Isenberg thinks it is the Google My Business moment for AI. His post runs through ten reactions in one breath, and they are worth taking seriously because they map, point by point, onto how the last platform shift played out. In the 2010s, agencies made millions just claiming and fixing listings for local businesses. Isenberg’s bet is that the same gold rush is about to happen inside ChatGPT — only this time the listing can talk back.

What OpenAI actually launched, in beta, is Business Hub: businesses claim their organisation, get verified, and keep their details, pricing and availability current through self-service product feeds. They get aggregated insights — mentions, website clicks, the discovery topics where their site is cited — without OpenAI sharing anyone’s conversations. And for eligible ecommerce and online-service businesses in the US, there is the bigger piece: a Business Agent. When a business appears in a model response, a person can click through to its profile and start a conversation with the agent — comparing products, asking what fits — clearly labelled and separate from ChatGPT’s own answers. Businesses can also sponsor their agent and use it as the destination for ChatGPT Ads. The audience is 1.2 billion people a week.

Isenberg’s ten points compress into four mechanisms. First, discovery: businesses that move first get a head start in local and niche search inside the conversation, where users increasingly start their buying. Second, advertising: intent and context targeting “in a way we’ve never seen,” because the ad is served at the exact moment someone is deciding. Third, retention: every profile and agent is another reason to stay inside ChatGPT rather than leave for a website. Fourth, data: OpenAI is collecting, in Isenberg’s words, “a goldmine” about businesses and consumers that becomes the raw material for future products. His conclusion is the one founders should hear: “the next big opportunity for founders and marketers is inside ChatGPT. The early movers will win big time.”

The open question is the one OpenAI does not answer in its post: how much of a business’s customer relationship moves inside ChatGPT, and how much stays its own. For Artha Studio’s clients, the practical move is the one Isenberg names first — claim the profile now, before the agencies do it for you and charge you for it.

The takeaway ChatGPT is no longer just where people ask questions; it is where they will discover, compare and buy. The businesses that treat their ChatGPT profile like their shopfront — claimed early, kept current, staffed by an agent — get the 2010s listings gold rush all over again, with a conversational storefront attached.

Claude Grows a Workspace: Sheets, Whiteboards, Data

Claude, Tibo & Somrat Dutta • 10–11 November 2026 • Original post • Anthropic’s launch articles read in full

Claude is no longer just a chat box. Anthropic’s launch on Monday adds three new artifact types — Claude Sheets, Claude Whiteboards and Claude Data — plus Interactive Connectors that let partner apps open directly inside Claude. The framing is deliberate: this is the Claude Code way of working, describing ideas and reviewing what Claude built, brought to every team. Tibo’s screenshot of the Claude docs captures the developer-facing half of the same move: you can now mark a tool as a global entry point, so Muse connects to the same MCP server from any chat and uses it inside Claude Code. “Labs innovating together ftw,” he writes. Somrat Dutta’s verdict is blunter: “there is no moat left…”

The three artifacts each attack a different kind of knowledge work. Sheets lets a team and Claude build spreadsheets together — tabs, formulas, formatting, drop-downs, often from a single prompt, with Claude editing in the cells and outlining each change; export to .xlsx or CSV, or hand off to Claude for Excel. Whiteboards turns a messy spoken plan into a board with a timeline, owners and dates; in a retro, everyone adds notes and Claude groups them by theme and drafts the action items, and you can drag a box around part of the board to ask Claude about it. Data, formerly Dashboards, connects to Snowflake, Salesforce, Redshift, BigQuery and ClickHouse to build dashboards, visual data stories or narrated short videos — with certified metric definitions carrying a badge, and live sharing that lets colleagues see approved numbers without access to the underlying connectors. Context carries across all three: an idea can move from whiteboard to spreadsheet to dashboard without being re-explained.

Interactive Connectors are the more strategic piece. Claude’s Marketplace already lists more than 9,000 connectors and plugins built on MCP, the open standard Anthropic introduced. Now partners embed their apps directly in Claude: open Asana, Canva, Notion or Vercel beside the conversation, or full-screen, and ask Claude to change a page, a row or a file you have open, approving its actions as you go. Adobe, Atlassian, Box, HubSpot and Notion are highlighted at launch; Figma, Harvey and Shopify are building integrations. For developers the underlying mechanism is MCP Apps, an open extension that lets an MCP server bring its own interface into any AI application that supports it — not just Claude. That open-standard bet is the tell: Anthropic is trying to make the workspace layer portable rather than proprietary.

OpenAI Acquires Odessia: Ten Days to a Legal Agent

Francis Davidson • 11:30 PM, 10 November 2026 • Original article • X long-form article read in full

OpenAI has acquired Odessia, the legal AI startup founded by Francis Davidson, and the announcement itself is a small case study in how the acqui-hire market now works. Odessia was built on a single belief: agents will do most knowledge work within five years, including a significant amount of what lawyers and legal departments do — and the highest-quality outputs would require a lot of high-quality context that only comes from the customer. So Odessia built dedicated agents for each specific legal task — a different agent for each contract type — each trained by the company’s own team of lawyers and legal professionals, drafting and reviewing contracts with what Davidson calls “unprecedented precision.” The team built its own evals and benchmarks for those agents and measured them on outputs, and kept adding capabilities, including pulling any document or information from any source to build an unprecedented amount of legal context for each matter.

The detail that will travel furthest is the timeline. In the last ten days before the announcement, the team built a product called Odessia Agent that Davidson describes as “a step change in the quality of legal AI” — built in ten days. His three lessons from the experience are blunt: speed is a weapon; agents are the future; the best products are built by the best teams. It is the same pattern as the Fable 5.2 demos elsewhere in this edition — small teams, frontier models, ten days to something that would once have taken a quarter — now playing out inside an acquisition announcement rather than a demo video.

The strategic read is straightforward. OpenAI is buying a team that has already solved, in one vertical, the problem every agent company is now wrestling with: how to get high-quality, customer-specific context into the agent, and how to measure whether the output is actually good. Legal work is the proving ground because the evals are unforgiving — a wrong clause is not a vibe, it is a liability. Davidson says he was struck, in his first conversations with the OpenAI team, by their ambition and their commitment to building the best products in the world. He joins OpenAI; Odessia’s customers and team go with him. No price was disclosed.

The takeaway The acquisition is the thesis in miniature: dedicated agents per task, trained by domain experts, measured on outputs, built in ten days. OpenAI did not buy a legal product; it bought a team that knows how to make agents trustworthy where being wrong has consequences.

Gemini 4 Carbon Is Now Available — Google’s Long-Horizon Machine

Melvin Johnson • 10:32 PM, 10 November 2026 • Original post • Google’s launch posts read in full

Google’s strongest model is now out of the lab. Gemini 4 Carbon, announced on 28 October and available generally from 10 November, is built for the hardest kind of work in AI: sustained agentic workflows on long-horizon engineering problems. Inside Google, teams watched it run for days, persisting through dead ends on complex multi-step tasks — root-causing and patching subtle bugs across large codebases and low-level systems. It is, in Google’s own framing, the model for tasks that test the limits of intelligence, while its sibling Argon “remains the ideal choice for the majority of software engineering and knowledge work.”

The headline numbers are in cybersecurity, where Carbon is genuinely a step change: number one on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at 59; 88.2% on SEC-bench Pro for finding vulnerabilities; 54.8% on ExploitGym for exploitation; 71.0% on CWE-bench for patching. Google says it delivers more than twice the success rate of Argon at generating secure code, and its Chrome team reports Carbon performed more than ten times better than another lab’s large frontier model at generating high-quality patches. In practice: Wiz’s Scan for Good found zero-day vulnerabilities in two major SaaS providers in under two hours, and Project Fortify hit 100% recall on scanning with 95% precision on triage. Carbon also tops CursorBench 4.0, Perplexity’s WANDR and Zapier’s AutomationBench at more than twice lower cost per task than the next best external model.

Just as notable is the safety work, which Google reports with unusual specificity: successful cheating on Terminal-Bench 4.0 down to 0.0%; high-severity misalignment alerts in live coding down by almost 80%; Carbon blocked 99% of attack trajectories in simulated cyberattack testing, with a per-attempt breach rate of 0.8% against 3.1% for Argon; Trajectory Labs found zero new universal jailbreaks across 132 hours and more than 13,000 requests; and on a 375-task agentic honesty benchmark it passed 92% of tasks against Argon’s 70%, declining to fill in missing experimental data 98% of the time. The price is the same as Argon’s introductory rate: $2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output, cached input at 95% off. Available now via the API in Google AI Studio and Gemini for business, and rolling out to Google AI Ultra subscribers in Antigravity, the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search. Google is also previewing /swarm in Antigravity, turning one prompt into a coordinated team of agents with Carbon in the deepest-reasoning roles.

The takeaway Carbon is the model for the jobs nobody wants to do by hand any more: multi-day debugging across a codebase, hunting zero-days, proving things about cryptographic protocols. The frontier is splitting into sprinters and marathoners — and Google has just named its marathoner.

Muse’s India Price Tag: &rupee;1,799 and &rupee;8,999

The Hindu, via Aroon Deep • 9 November 2026 • Original post • The Hindu’s report read in full

Meta has set rupee prices for Muse, its personal AI agent, ahead of the official India launch: &rupee;1,799 a month for the Power tier and &rupee;8,999 a month for Maximum, with marginally higher charges through Google Play and Apple billing. The prices, first reported by The Hindu on 9 November, are broadly in line with US rates — $20 and $100 a month respectively — and Meta has not said whether they are final or when the launch will actually happen. It did not respond to The Hindu’s queries.

The details underneath the headline are what make this an India story rather than a pricing story. Indian users are still on a waitlist; Muse is not listed on Indian app stores. The web and WhatsApp versions are reachable, but Muse cannot yet accept authorisation to make card payments on a user’s behalf in India, because that depends on payment-processor integrations that do not operate in the country. The free tier exists with usage limits; the paid tiers raise the weekly allowance — 500 million Muse tokens a week on Power, 3 billion on Maximum, per Meta’s help pages. Muse was introduced at Meta Connect in September as an agent that works across Meta products, including WhatsApp, sending emails and booking travel in the background. Some users who registered through a VPN set to a supported region have reportedly kept access in India even after switching it off, though Meta’s terms require physical presence in a supported market.

Read it next to the Anthropic IPO lead and the shape of the week becomes clear: every frontier lab is now pricing the same bet — that the agent, not the chatbot, is the product — and India is where the pricing has to survive contact with reality. &rupee;1,799 is not a casual subscription in India; it is a deliberate, premium number, pitched at the same professionals who are already paying for Claude and ChatGPT. The waitlist is the tell: Meta is pricing before it is ready, because the market is moving faster than its launch calendar.

The takeaway The price is set, the product is not quite here: &rupee;1,799 for Power, &rupee;8,999 for Maximum, waitlist only, and card payments on your behalf still don’t work in India. Meta is signalling intent more than availability — and the signal is that the agent wars will be fought on Indian pricing.

Kunal Shah: WhatsApp Is a Language, and the Future Won’t Speak English

Kunal Shah • 8:44 PM, 10 November 2026 • Original post

Kunal Shah has spent four months inside WhatsApp, and his post is a small essay on what he found there. “I think of WhatsApp like a shared language, spoken by 3bn+ people. Sometimes we use words, and sometimes it’s a voice note to a parent, a sticker that ends an argument, or a ‘that was a fun dinner’ at the end of the night.” Language, he argues, is at the core of any society’s design: the words we use, the way we say them, and the tools we use to say them define who we are and who we get to be. For the last few decades the default language of technology was English — the language of the internet, of code, of the future. “But the future is not going to be in English. The future is going to be in the language of the people who use it.”

The point lands differently when you read it next to the Muse pricing story above it. Meta’s agent speaks English beautifully and is still, in India, waitlisted and unable to pay a bill on your behalf. WhatsApp, meanwhile, is already the operating system of Indian daily life — the place where a voice note to a parent and a payment and a family argument all happen in the same thread, in the language the family actually speaks. Shah’s argument is that the interface of the future is not a new app but a familiar tongue: whoever builds in the language people already think in wins the next billion users, and whoever insists on English builds for the last billion.

The takeaway The next platform shift is linguistic, not technical. Products that speak the user’s language — literally — inherit three billion people; products that don’t have to teach them a new one first.

Nithin Kamath: What India Needs More of Is “Nahi Chalta Hai”

Nithin Kamath • 9:00 AM, 10 November 2026 • Original post • The Economist excerpt, via the image

Nithin Kamath posted a screenshot from The Economist with a one-line verdict: what India needs more of is “nahi chalta hai.” The phrase is the opposite of the familiar “chalta hai” — the “it’ll do” shrug that has become shorthand for Indian tolerance of the mediocre. The Economist’s argument, in the excerpt Kamath shared, is that this attitude may explain one of India’s biggest failures: the lack of progress on public health and sanitation. Cockroaches are everywhere, a major source of disease, and Indians have learned to live with them until they have become part of the landscape. The same learned tolerance, the piece argues, shows up in traffic: the chaos is lived with until it, too, becomes landscape.

It is a short post with a long fuse. “Chalta hai” is not just an attitude toward cockroaches and traffic; it is a standard-setting mechanism, and standards are contagious across domains. A country that tolerates unsafe streets tolerates unsafe food, unsafe buildings, unsafe data. The inverse is also true, and it is the hopeful reading of Kamath’s post: raise the standard in one place and it spreads. The builders in today’s edition — the ones shipping a game in eight hours, or pricing an agent at &rupee;1,799 — are all, in their own way, refusing “chalta hai” in their own domain. Kamath’s point is that the refusal has to become a habit of the whole society, not just its startups.

“What India needs more of is ‘nahi chalta hai.’”Nithin Kamath, sharing The Economist
The takeaway Standards are a network effect: tolerate the cockroach and you tolerate the chaos. “Nahi chalta hai” — this will not do — is the cheapest infrastructure India can build, and the one everything else depends on.

The takeaway Both posts are about the same underrated skill: knowing when to go, and going well. From a party, from a thought, from a version of yourself — the exit is the part people remember.

Simon Willison Gave Claude Code Ears

Simon Willison’s Newsletter • 10 November 2026 • Read the original

Something in Simon Willison’s box room was beeping, intermittently, from somewhere inside a pile of unpacked boxes — too infrequent to find by standing in the room and listening. So he had Claude Code build Beep Locator: a Python web server plus every spare device in the house — two iPads, a phone, a MacBook Pro and a Surface Ultra — turned into listening posts, triangulating the beep by arrival time. The system worked: it placed the beep to within about 40 centimetres, and digging through that specific pile of boxes turned up an ancient carbon monoxide detector begging for new batteries. 3,345 hertz, three confirmed hearings, one very relieved household.

The build is the interesting part. Claude started from a 2007 paper, BeepBeep, on acoustic ranging with commodity devices, and built a system where listeners keep a 60-second audio buffer, run FFT-based beep detection, and upload clips around each event — aligned by a chirp the server asks the fastest device to play. When device-to-device self-location didn’t work well enough, Willison added a “rough mode” to drag icons into place. When he wanted to test it, he added clap detection, with claps as diamonds and beeps as crosses on the map. When he wanted finer accuracy, he added a “reposition the devices” flow and a suggestion for where an extra listener would help most. The whole thing was built with Opus 5.5 running in extra-thinking mode, and the masterstroke was a hot-swap mechanism: listeners can receive and run new JavaScript over the WebSocket, so Willison could keep iterating without touching each device to re-grant microphone permission. “I think the most interesting part of this project is that I demonstrated it’s possible to give Claude Code ears,” he writes. Claude has no native audio input — yet — but it can run code that listens, and then query its own audio logs.

The post’s second half is a small, sharp observation about the new unit of work. Most of Willison’s changes now arrive as PRs opened by Claude Code or Codex, and the agents write descriptions that are accurate, comprehensive — and unreadable, because they read like slop. His fix is gloriously low-tech: put the agent’s full description behind an HTML <details><summary> tag, which GitHub renders as a collapsed heading, and write the opening paragraph yourself as the human-approved summary. The PR is the unit of work; the human summary is the unit of trust.

Beep Locator, how it works

The takeaway The agents are getting senses. A webcam pointed at a screen, a microphone network built from spare tablets — the frontier of agent work right now is not smarter models, it is giving the models a way to perceive the world they are supposed to change. And when they write your PRs, keep the human summary on top.

The Job Market, Late 2026: PMs Win, Engineers Stall, Designers Hurt

Lenny Rachitsky • 10 November 2026 • Read the original • Free text read in full; the final takeaway is paywalled

Lenny Rachitsky’s second job-market report of 2026 is, by his own account, the most surprising of the five he has done. The headline: product managers are winning in the AI era, engineering demand has stalled, design is at a record low, and everybody now needs to AI. The analysis triangulates two proprietary datasets — TrueUp, tracking openings at more than 9,000 tech companies, and Live Data Technologies, tracking job changes for 124 million professionals.

The PM numbers are striking. There are more open PM roles than at any point in nearly four years, up 75% from the March 2023 low, and PM openings have grown faster than any other core tech role since the start of 2023 — up 56% against 43% for engineering. Actual hiring tells a subtler story: hiring is flat or falling for every core role, but PM hiring has held up best by far, and PM is the only one of the five core roles whose share of hiring grew, from 16% in 2021 to 21% today. Openings up, hiring flat: demand for PMs is growing, but landing great ones is getting harder.

Engineering, for the first time in the history of the report, has stalled: openings grew 29% through 2024, 15% through 2025, and just 2.5% this past year — fewer open roles today than in March. US companies hired about 113,000 software engineers in the past twelve months, less than half the 2022 peak of 239,000, falling every year since. Two bright spots: demand for tech recruiters is on a tear, up nearly 300% since mid-2023, which may be a leading indicator of re-acceleration; and the forward-deployed engineer is exploding, with FDE openings up 4.6 times in a year to around 1,500 — partly real demand, partly “sales engineer” rebranded. Security engineering is one of the fastest-growing specialties; mobile and front-end demand has nearly halved in a year, which Lenny reads as the collapse of siloed specialisms as smaller teams take broader ownership with AI.

Design is the bleakest chapter: about 1,900 open product-design roles globally, the lowest since tracking began, down 28% year on year; US hiring of designers halved since 2022 to 12,600. There are now 3.8 open PM roles for every open design role, up from 1.9 in early 2023. User research has been hit harder still: 1,800 hires in the US over the past year, down 66% from 5,300 in 2022, with only 108 open UXR jobs globally. The skills data explains the mechanism: “LLM” is now the most mentioned skill in PM job descriptions, “Claude” the third, with Cursor, OpenAI and MCP in the top eight — and “evals” is gaining momentum in the top ten. For engineers, Python keeps growing and “LLM” just passed Java. For designers, “Claude” became the second most mentioned skill this spring, crossing CSS. The geography completes the picture: a record 15% of all open tech roles are now in the Bay Area, up 43% in a year, and one in four open PM and design roles is there; remote is at a record low of 14%, down from 21% in late 2022. Somewhere last year, Bay Area roles overtook remote roles globally for the first time.

The takeaway The AI era is not replacing product managers; it is crowning them — while engineers plateau, designers shrink, and the work concentrates in the Bay Area. The skill that now matters most in a PM job description is not SQL or Jira. It is “LLM.”

What’s the ROI of All That AI Spend?

Gergely Orosz, The Pragmatic Engineer • 20 October 2026 • Read the original • Free text read in full; the Big Tech section is paywalled

Engineering leaders everywhere are asking the same question this year: we are spending a fortune on AI tools, but what are we getting for it? Gergely Orosz went and asked — 27 companies, plus aggregate data from CodeRabbit and Gitar — and the answer is gloriously uneven. CodeRabbit’s data shows weekly pull requests up 113% on a year ago across 20,000 tracked developers, and Gitar’s tiers run from about five PRs a week per developer at “normal” teams to more than sixty at AI-native ones. But PRs are not value, and the moment you ask what the money buys, the picture fractures.

The spending itself is extraordinary in its range. Company size and AI spend are unrelated: a two-person .NET shop spends $3,000 a month per developer, while a public company with 1,500 engineers caps its people at $150. Spending peaked in June and July and has roughly halved since for API users, as teams moved to Opus 5.5 and open models. At the top end, a quant shop’s AI bill went from $500,000 a month in January to $8 million in September — a sixteen-fold rise, $1,600 per developer a month, with traders, not engineers, the biggest spenders, and a $25,000 per-employee limit. The two-person shop is the purest case: $3,000 a month each on Claude Opus and GitHub Copilot, 60 PRs per developer per week, up from one or two a day. “We barely type code anymore,” the co-founder says. “We do about 60 PRs a week… it feels like our two-person team is doing the work that would have taken about 5–7 people before AI.”

But the counter-examples are just as instructive. One company at $1,500 a month per developer reports PR frequency barely changed — but it built an agent to review PRs and flag the bloated ones, and that part is going well. Another, with a $1,000 soft cap, sees PRs double but PR size triple, and its engineers report that where quality is held to the old standard, “actual development speed had not increased much” — “accelerated slop produced all around me.” A team lead at a company with a $2,000 hard cap enforced by public shaming in a #ai-limits Slack channel reports velocity unchanged: “Most people are still sleeping on AI to be honest.” And the team that did see a real change measured it in business value, not PRs: three times more user stories closed per month since February, and for the first time ever, more stories closed than incoming. The honest summary, from Orosz’s own framing: few companies can yet answer what the ROI is. What the data does show is the shape of the answer — the gains concentrate where teams care about quality and measure outcomes, and evaporate where they measure throughput.

AI spend per developer, per month — the spread

The takeaway PRs are up twofold, spend is up sixteen-fold at the top, and almost nobody can say what the return is. The teams that can point to one measure stories closed, not pull requests merged — and hold the quality bar where it was. Everything else is throughput theatre.
