The Three-Man Spy Ring That Shorted Nvidia

Kevin Wang, via the Wall Street Journal • 11–12 November 2026 • Read the WSJ profile • Original post

Six months ago, a 31-year-old short seller working out of a Brooklyn apartment with two analysts published a 40-page report arguing that Nvidia — then the most valuable company on earth — had a China problem. The next day Nvidia hit an all-time high and nobody cared. This week the report went viral, the stock is down 20% since publication, more than $1 trillion of market value has evaporated, and the Wall Street Journal has profiled the man behind it: Christian Lamarco of Culper Research.

The name is the tell. Lamarco named his firm after the Culper ring, the network of spies who tracked British troop movements for George Washington during the Revolutionary War — he had watched the AMC show Turn: Washington’s Spies in a high-school friend’s basement and saw the parallel with his own work. “Similarly,” he wrote when founding the firm, “we take an exhaustive, investigative approach to investment research.” The Nvidia investigation was that approach pushed to its limit: interviews with former Nvidia employees through a network of paid experts, corporate filings dug out of Singapore and Malaysia, scrubbed code examined line by line, and YouTube footage studied from outside a Taiwanese noodle shop.

The biography is the classic outsider’s route. Lamarco grew up in upstate New York and studied finance at Grove City College, a small Christian school in Pennsylvania with no Wall Street recruiting pipeline. He once drove to Carnegie Mellon to compete against Ivy Leaguers in a stock-picking contest; he and his teammate finished second and won $100. With no recruiters visiting his campus, he cold-emailed his own research to hedge funds and landed a job at a Chicago long-short fund, where he worked from 2017 to 2019. Then he quit with a few months of savings and struck out on his own, first in Pittsburgh, then New York, his Shiba Inu begging for treats in the apartment.

The investigation itself nearly didn’t happen. Intrigued by reporting in the New York Times and Bloomberg last year, Lamarco’s team started digging into Nvidia, then stopped — it didn’t seem worth their time. “It’s just highly unlikely that you’re going to find something that everyone else has missed,” he told the Journal. “Are we really going to have anything to say about the largest company in the world?” What brought them back, in March, was news: federal prosecutors had charged Super Micro Computer officials with smuggling Nvidia chips into China in violation of US export controls. “Let’s dig a little bit deeper,” he said.

What they found became the report’s thesis, stated plainly on its cover: “This is not a bet against AI. We are short Nvidia for one reason: the company has a significant China problem.” The report raised questions about Nvidia’s business in China and about what Culper called an “uncomfortably close” relationship between CEO Jensen Huang and Alice Huang, an executive at one of Nvidia’s major business partners in Southeast Asia. On the day it was published, 13 May, Huang touched down in Beijing aboard Air Force One on a state trip with President Trump. The next day Nvidia hit a record high. The market’s verdict was silence.

Nvidia’s stock, from the report to the reckoning

Six months later the story inverted. This week, questions about Alice Huang turned into revelations that Nvidia’s CEO was having an extramarital affair with a woman now suspected of working for Chinese intelligence services — the national-security story that led Edition 46 of this paper. Culper’s report, ignored in May, became the rare piece of securities analysis to go viral. Lamarco muted his X notifications, where his Nvidia posts have racked up millions of views, and turned down television interview requests. “I’ve never quite had anything like this,” he said. “Sometimes, people care about what you have to say. Sometimes, they don’t. I guess in this case, they cared six months later.”

Whether the call made him money is, characteristically, undisclosed — he declined to say how much he has earned, or whether he is still short. But the episode is a study in how asymmetry actually works. Culper is not a giant fund with a research department; it is three people, a dog, and a willingness to do the unglamorous work — filings in two countries, code archaeology, noodle-shop footage — on a company everyone else had decided was too big and too well-covered to hide anything. The edge was not information. It was patience: the willingness to be ignored for six months while the thesis matured, and to publish anyway.

“This is not a bet against AI. We are short Nvidia for one reason: the company has a significant China problem.”Culper Research, 13 May 2026

There is a coda, and it is pure Lamarco. The firm named after a spy ring is already gathering intelligence on its next target. “There’s something relatively big,” he told the Journal. “But by its very nature, nothing will be as big as this.” He has also taken on powerful forces before: last year a window and glass manufacturer hired Elon Musk’s attorney and sued him for defamation after he alleged links to the Sinaloa drug cartel, and he once offered Jimmy Fallon a $1 million wager over electric air taxis — and never heard back.

The takeawayThe market does not reward being right; it rewards being right and early enough to matter, then patient enough to survive being ignored. Culper’s real product was never the short — it was the investigation, done at a scale nobody thought was worth doing on the world’s biggest company. That is a repeatable playbook, and it is the same one this paper keeps returning to: small teams, primary sources, unglamorous work.

Karpathy’s Autoresearch Loop: Research That Runs While You Sleep

Buzzoni, via Md Ismail Sojal (@0xsojalsec) on Instagram • Flagged 11 November 2026 • Original post

He flagged this one himself yesterday: a single-page technical sheet by Buzzoni on Andrej Karpathy’s Autoresearch Loop, a system that runs AI research all night on one GPU and hands you ~100 experiments by morning. The sheet is worth the full treatment, because the idea is not really about AI research at all. It is about what happens when you stop managing the work and start managing the rules the work runs under.

The setup is deliberately tiny. Three files. program.md, which the human writes: the goal, the rules, the loop itself. train.py, which the agent edits: the model, the optimiser, the training code. And prepare.py, which is locked: the data, the tokeniser, the evaluation. The agent is told never to stop and ask “should I keep going?” — it just keeps going. You never touch the Python. You only edit program.md, which Buzzoni calls “the code of your research org.”

1 · One ideaThe agent picks a single idea from program.md and edits train.py.
→
2 · CommitEvery change is git-committed before it runs.
→
3 · Train 5 minFixed budget. Any run past 10 minutes is killed.
→
4 · Judgegrep val_bpb — validation bits per byte. Lower wins.
→
5 · Keep or resetBetter score: advance the branch. Worse: git reset to the last good commit.

Every outcome is logged to results.tsv, crashes included — a crash is either fixed or logged as “crash” and the loop moves on. The numbers are what make it concrete: about 5 minutes per experiment, roughly 12 an hour, around 100 by morning, on a single NVIDIA GPU. The project itself carries some ~98,844 GitHub stars under an MIT licence, which is the kind of signal that usually means the idea has already escaped the lab.

The rules table is the heart of the sheet, and it reads like a constitution for an organisation of one human and one tireless agent:

One file only. The agent edits train.py and nothing else, so every change is a small, reviewable diff. Fixed budget. Five minutes per run, killed at ten, so no idea can eat the night. Locked judge. The agent cannot touch prepare.py, so it cannot move the goalposts to flatter its own results — evaluation stays honest. Simplicity. A tiny gain that adds 20 hacky lines gets thrown out; the same score with less code stays. The change has to pay for itself. No check-ins. The loop never asks permission, because permission is the bottleneck the whole system exists to remove.

“One number to beat. One file to edit. A fixed time per run.”Buzzoni, on the Autoresearch Loop

Why the constraints matter is the part worth sitting with. Small diffs mean a human can actually review what the agent did in the morning instead of drowning in it. The fixed budget means every idea is priced in wall-clock time on the same GPU, so ideas compete fairly. And the locked judge is the whole game: an agent that can rewrite its own evaluation will always find a way to look clever. The human’s job moves up a level — from writing code to writing the program: what to try, what is off limits, how to recover from failure.

Buzzoni’s sheet ends with the same pattern pointed somewhere unexpected: aim the loop at an Obsidian Zettelkasten and it keeps your notes linked and compounding instead of rotting. The pattern generalises because it is not really a research tool. It is a management philosophy in three files: define the game, lock the scoreboard, let the machine play all night, and review in the morning. For anyone running an EMBA project, a studio, or a product team, the question the sheet quietly asks is the same one it asks researchers: what would your work look like if you only edited the program, and the execution ran itself?

The takeawayThe loop’s power is not the agent — it is the constraints. One editable file, one locked judge, one fixed budget, one number. That is a template for any system where you want relentless execution without losing control: write the rules well, then get out of the way. And the morning review is the real job — the loop does the experiments, but only a human can decide which of the 100 is worth keeping.

Roma 2.0: The To-Do List That Makes Agents Multiplayer

Jan Senderek (@jans) • Video watched and transcribed, 11 November 2026 • Original post • roma.app

Jan Senderek sold his first company, Loom, to Dropbox in 2014. His second act, announced yesterday, is Roma 2.0 — billed as the first to-do list for AI agents — and the launch video, watched in full for this edition, is one of the clearest articulations yet of the problem every multi-agent user now has: “AI was just supposed to give us our lives back, and instead we’re spending hours managing them, and deciding which agent to use.”

The pitch is a single insight: the to-do list is the original productivity tool, and nobody had reimagined it for AI. Everyone uses a different agent — Claude, ChatGPT, Grok — and until now none of them could work together. Roma sits in the middle. It connects to your apps, turns a ramble into tasks, and hands each task to the right agent under rules you set once. “I never assigned a task myself,” Senderek says in the demo. “Roma hands every new task to the right agent using the rules I set once. So my most powerful agent only works on the bug that needs it, not on drafting emails. And that saves me hundreds of dollars a week.”

The demo is worth describing because the mechanism is the product. Senderek rambles: the launch moved to Wednesday, update whoever needs to know; a user says a connector is broken; the product newsletter needs updating before Friday. Roma turns that into three tasks and fills in the gaps — “I said whoever needs to know, and it figured out the rest.” The “who needs to know” table has four groups: the studio and the production agency (by email), 20 creators posting on launch day (each emailed via Notion), Cody (Slack message), and the YC partner at Thursday office hours (calendar note). Then the multiplayer part kicks in: his ChatGPT picks up the broken connector, reads the docs, fixes it, and logs the changes on the task. Cody’s and Toby’s agents get the context and update the landing page and the newsletter without a single text. When Toby asks Grok why the newsletter changed, Grok answers straight from the task history.

The follow-up thread fills in the rest. A second clip shows the cross-agent handoff in miniature: ChatGPT compares 30 domains and finds fernapp.co free and short; Muse buys it for $74 on a Visa; Claude connects it and ships the landing page. A voice-notes clip promises that a 30-second voice note, recorded on a morning walk, parses into clear tasks, gets assigned, and executes while you go about your day. And the origin story is personal: Senderek says he has struggled with ADHD all his life and can’t be in the moment until his head is clear — “the only way to clear it is to get things out of it.” He was copy-pasting tasks into ChatGPT and back all day, “like a human clipboard”, while AI picked up work everywhere except his to-do list. So he went back to YC — second time around — and rebuilt the list from first principles. Free access runs until the end of the year.

RambleVoice note or typed ramble: launch moved, connector broken, newsletter due.
→
ParseRoma turns it into tasks and fills in the gaps — who needs to know, by when.
→
RouteRules set once hand each task to the right agent. The expensive agent gets the bug, not the email.
→
Shared historyEvery decision logged in plain English, searchable. Agents answer from the same task history.

The honest question is the one Senderek himself raises in a reply: moving context around currently costs users “$500 to $1000” in wasted agent spend — “the human clipboard problem, only paid.” Roma’s bet is that the to-do list, not the chat window, is the right shared surface for agents, because a list is where work is already tracked, prioritised and handed off between humans. If that bet is right, the interesting number is not the launch’s 808,000 views but the claim that Roma handled 90% of the launch itself. A product that eats its own dog food that thoroughly is either a demo or a proof — and the difference will show up in whether the task history actually stays trustworthy when three agents disagree.

The takeawaySingle-player agents are a solved problem; multiplayer agents are the frontier. The bottleneck is no longer what an agent can do — it is context: who did what, why, and what changed. Whoever owns the shared, searchable record of agent decisions owns the next layer of the stack. Roma’s answer is the humblest object in productivity: the list.

The Agentic Phone: Your Agents Join the Call

Isa Usmanov (@isausmanov), founder of Raven (YC F26) • Video watched and transcribed, 12 November 2026 • Original post

The most common question Isa Usmanov gets from his YC batchmates is: what can your AI phone actually do? His answer is three minutes of demo, watched in full for this edition, and it is the most concrete vision yet of the phone as an agent’s body rather than an app launcher.

It opens mid-call. Usmanov is talking to Alex about meeting for coffee tomorrow; as they speak, the agent proposes the meeting and schedules it while they are still talking — noon, at Farley’s. Then the interface itself is the reveal: “What I love the most is I completely removed the chat interface when I work with my agents. These are the cards that agents prepared for me to read.” The phone becomes a review surface, not a conversation. Agent work arrives as cards — a draft reply (“looks good, send it”), some research to look at later, events scheduled, code being fixed — and the user’s job is reduced to accept or reject, with feedback in the middle of processing: ask Max from the team to review this code, look up who made that launch video, maybe use them. Below, the tasks still in progress hum along, three processing at once.

The demo keeps stacking ideas. Go live with the agent and ask it to find three real videographers in San Francisco to shoot the launch video; it reports back when done. Notifications get filtered to what matters — because the phone knows you are looking for a videographer, it surfaces that Hasan connected on LinkedIn and has shot content for other startups. Normal apps still work, but agents pre-draft the replies, so a text becomes a quick reply and a suggested time, and confirming it lets the agent accept on your behalf. And the calendar is not a calendar: “This is not just a calendar for today. It’s a reconciled day. Any commitment, if I message someone on WhatsApp promising to meet, it’s all going to appear here.”

The last beat is the most telling. You can create your own apps by prompting for them — Usmanov shows a mini-game where he runs like Mario through San Francisco collecting capital — and the more tools you connect, the more useful the phone becomes, because it can query them and act on your behalf. No price, specification or launch date is stated in the video. What is stated, in a reply, is the design goal: handing off “many little things — the focus-killers”, and less jumping between apps.

“Every day my agents control my day and support me. Planning it.”Isa Usmanov, on the Raven phone

Read next to Roma, the pattern sharpens. Roma says the to-do list is the shared surface for agents; Raven says the phone is. Both agree on the deeper point: the chat window was a scaffolding, not the destination. The interface of the agent era is a stream of prepared work awaiting a human yes or no — cards, not conversations. The open question is trust at scale: accept-or-reject works when the stakes are a draft reply, and it is a different proposition when the agent is buying domains on your Visa or accepting meetings on your behalf. The reconciled day is a beautiful idea right up until the day it reconciles something you never meant to promise.

The takeawayThe phone’s next form factor is not a better screen — it is a better filter. Cards to review, notifications that know what you care about, commitments gathered from every app into one reconciled day. The companies winning this shift are the ones that make the agent’s work reviewable in seconds, because human attention is the scarcest resource in the loop.

Of Miracles and Moats: Why the Best Technology Makes the Worst Business

Packy McCormick, Not Boring • 11 November 2026 • Read the original

This is the piece to read alongside yesterday’s Anthropic IPO lead, and it may be the most useful framework in today’s paper. Packy McCormick’s argument is disarmingly simple: a miracle technology does not guarantee a miraculous business. In fact, the more incredible and useful a technology is, the more it attracts competitors who collectively build too much supply and crash prices. “Miracles without moats.”

He refuses to call a bubble — “it is neither productive nor becoming” — then dismantles the three standard arguments against AI being one: the tech is genuinely useful and widely adopted; companies generate real, fast-growing revenue; labs can be profitable and losses are just investment. Grant all three, he says, and you have described the setup for a bubble, not the defence against it. The cycle runs in ten steps: a miracle technology arrives; early suppliers earn real profits because supply is short; demand looks bottomless; demand and profits pull in capital and competitors; everyone builds at once, even those who see the glut coming, locking in commitments at scarcity prices; supply arrives, the tech gets better, prices collapse; capital loses, consumers win, the technology chugs on; capital flees and new entrants can’t get funded; a survivor or outsider with capital consolidates and becomes the low-cost provider; demand catches up and the survivor ends up larger than all the failed companies combined. Steps one to seven are near-law, he argues; eight to ten often, but don’t always, play out — “sometimes consumers simply dine on the industry’s corpses.”

The history is the essay’s real payload, and it is told with numbers. Airlines: the New York Times predicted flight would take one to ten million years to figure out, about two months before the Wright Brothers did it. Warren Buffett’s verdict in his 2007 letter was that the industry “grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money” — and even he could not stay away, buying roughly $7–8 billion of four airlines in 2016 and selling all of them at a loss in April 2020. British railways: in 1842 the first lines paid dividends of 10% or more, demand looked bottomless, and by 1845 The Times counted 1,263 proposed schemes; Parliament authorised 4,540 miles of new track in 1846. The dot-com bandwidth buildout: telcos spent ~$500 billion, long-haul fibre mileage doubled between 1996 and 2001 while each strand carried 40 times more data, and a 155 Mbps New York–LA circuit fell from $1.8 million a year in 2000 to under $150,000 by early 2002. Global Crossing emerged from bankruptcy with debt cut from $11 billion to $200 million and shareholders receiving nothing — in its emergence year its traffic grew 130% while revenue fell 6%. Oil in the 1860s: $20 a barrel fell to 10 cents by December 1861, then rebounded above $8 in 1864 and drew another frenzy; Pithole, Pennsylvania went from farmland sold for $2 million in September 1865 to 237 residents by the 1870 census, and the same land sold back to the county for $4.37 in 1878.

And then the exceptions, which are the point. Rockefeller did not bet on oil prices; he tamed volatility through scale, cost and vertical integration, controlling 90% of American refining by the 1880s. Ford won by being the low-cost provider of quality in a glut, among some 2,000 early automakers, almost all of which failed. Vanderbilt did not care about railroads as such — he wanted to move people and parcels as quickly and cheaply as possible, by sail, steam, then rail, and died in 1877 worth ~$100 million, about one of every twenty dollars in circulation in America. The Yale economist William Nordhaus found producers captured only ~2.2% of the total social surplus from innovation between 1948 and 2001; consumers captured roughly 97–98%.

The AI labs are playing the same game theory in public. Larry Page is reportedly willing to go bankrupt rather than lose the race. Sundar Pichai told investors the risk of underinvesting is dramatically greater than the risk of overinvesting. Zuckerberg conceded a bubble is “quite possible” but said the risk is higher on the other side. And Dario Amodei told Dwarkesh Patel that if he is off by only a year, he destroys his company: he could buy a trillion dollars a year of compute starting at the end of 2027, and “if my revenue is not a trillion dollars, if it’s even 800 billion, there’s no force on earth that could stop me from going bankrupt.” Anthropic has now committed $900.9 billion of compute through 2045 in its S-1, about half of it before the end of this decade — though it generated an operating profit in Q2 and would have again in Q3 excluding roughly $2 billion in non-cash donation-matching charges.

“The more miraculous your technology, the more crucial your moats.”Packy McCormick, Not Boring

Packy’s closing is honest where most bubble commentary is not: on the Anthropic IPO and the AI bubble, “No fucking idea.” But the framework gives you the question to ask instead of the answer: who captures the value? The investor’s version of the answer is companies like Vanderbilt, Rockefeller or Ford — able to plug in a better technology to deliver more cheaply rather than be threatened by it — and, as AI makes everything cheaper to build, scale economies and cost advantages rather than network effects. For a founder, the same question lands closer to home: in a world where AI collapses the cost of building, your moat is cost, distribution and craft — not the model.

The takeawayDo not ask whether the technology is real — that is the wrong test, and it is the test everyone passes right before the crash. Ask who captures the surplus when supply arrives. The miracle is the input; the moat is the business. Anthropic prices on 18 November. Read this first.

Designing for Agents: Why Claude Code Keeps Recommending Resend

Latent Space, in conversation with Zeno Rocha, co-founder and CEO of Resend • 10 November 2026 • Read the original

Here is a mystery worth sitting with: the hosts of Latent Space are big fans of Cloudflare’s developer platform, and Cloudflare Email is the convenient choice when your domains already live there. So why do agents keep suggesting Resend? In a recent answer-engine study, Claude Code picked Resend first in 64 of the 102 email recommendations researchers could extract. SendGrid came first only 7 times, though it showed up 55 times as an alternative. If a developer asks an agent to implement email and accepts the patch, how often does anyone try the second choice?

The answer, as Zeno Rocha tells it, is that Resend was designed for the agent era before the agent era arrived — by accident, then on purpose. The wedge was React Email: while building the sending API, developers kept saying the painful part was making the email itself look right, so Resend shipped a tool to build templates with React, usable even through SendGrid, with documentation showing how. By the time a developer needed a different provider, some of Resend’s code was already in the repo. The same “developer gets there before the model” logic now applies to agents: Resend’s pricing page has a slider a person can drag; add .md to the URL and the agent gets a table it can read in one request. Rocha’s test for any developer-tools company: ask whether a model has heard of your product, and open your own pricing table to see whether it can answer a customer question. The company now serves 4.3 million users and 120,000 paying customers — Rocha discovered one of them, Warner Bros. Discovery, by sorting through short .com domains in the customer database on a Saturday.

The design questions get sharper in the second half. Resend’s MCP server grew from roughly 20 tools at launch to more than 80, because Rocha wants someone in a chat client to do everything the dashboard does — sending email is the demo, but inviting teammates, checking usage and managing billing are the long tail. The contrast is Cloudflare’s Code Mode server, which exposes its API through just two tools, search() and execute(). Rocha opens the collapsed tool calls in his own sessions to see what the agent actually did; in one, the result was too large to fit in context — and reducing the tool count would not by itself have made the response smaller. His cheap experiment for MCP designers: expand the result of a call you make every day and see how much of it the model actually needs. Anthropic’s own guidance on writing tools for agents — return less data — is cited in the show notes.

Then the part after “send”: the actual business of delivering mail. Click tracking rewrites links through a shared tracking domain, but if one customer uses that domain for phishing and it gets blocklisted, another customer’s legitimate emails suffer too — which is why Resend introduced custom tracking domains, and why Rocha wants to keep the free tier even though spammers can sign up for it, backed by a $3 million safety commitment and a published anti-abuse handbook. The episode closes on craft and on the personal stretch of building a company with a family — and on how much of a founder’s job comes down to finding a name other people actually use. (Rocha launched the Dracula editor theme in 2013.)

The thread that runs through this edition is now unmistakable. Roma says agents need a shared to-do list. Raven says agents need a phone that filters. Resend says agents are already the customer, and the companies winning are the ones whose docs, pricing and tools are machine-readable. For anyone building with agents — a support agent at ET Money, a studio selling ad creative, a product team choosing its stack — the distribution channel has quietly moved: it is no longer the developer reading your docs, it is the agent reading them on the developer’s behalf. The question is no longer whether your product is good. It is whether a model, asked cold, recommends it first.

The takeawayAnswer-engine optimisation is the new SEO, and the unit of competition is the recommendation. Make your pricing machine-readable, your docs agent-friendly, and your tools return less than the model needs — then check, cold, whether a model has heard of you. If it hasn’t, no amount of marketing will reach the developer who never sees the second choice.

Selflessly Selfish: Put Yourself First, For Others

Wulfie Bain, Small Sparks • 11 November 2026 • Read the original

The counterweight to a paper full of agents, shorts and moats: a short, confessional essay from one of his own picks, built around a quote of unknown origin — “You can’t pour from an empty cup.” Wulfie Bain’s thesis is in the title: be “selflessly selfish” — put yourself first precisely so you are better placed to be selfless. Providing for others requires being in a position to provide, and for ambitious people the neglected currency is rarely money. It is physical health, mental health and joy.

Bain is candid about the cost of getting this wrong. For years he did not think his own happiness mattered: on paper he was doing well — friends, academics, sport, career — and he loudly advocated men’s mental health and had deep chats with friends about their struggles, while carefully never talking about himself, not wanting to “burden” others. He burned out badly, which he calls “a true gift” because it forced him to open up, be vulnerable and do the personal work. Years on he is genuinely happy, and sees in retrospect that by neglecting himself to burnout he became incapable of the very things he cared about: achieving impact and supporting others. “My cup was empty, and I could no longer water those around me.” Caring for himself turned out not to be a trade-off with ambition but a contributor to it.

He applies the idea across his life, and the applications are the essay. On gendered expectations: women expected to give up careers they love, men expected to be providers who never show weakness — “it’s mental how many men fantasise about dying in a heroic last stand” — with unhappy families and men’s mental health in a terrible state as the result. On children: until a few years ago he was not sure he wanted them, despite adoring being around them; he now realises the real reason was fear — he was unhappy and unwilling to create another soul assuming they would be similarly unhappy. Now there is “almost nothing I’m more sure of” than wanting kids — but conditionally: he wants kids and to raise them well, to be “the best dad”. Financial providing is only a small part of that; loving a partner deeply and stably because he is happy in himself, showing a child it is OK to be vulnerable, doing ballet if his child is into it. He has a two-year-old niece who brings him “an unbelievable amount of joy”, has read parenting books to be the best uncle, still goes to therapy even when it is no longer “needed”, and takes ballet classes “so I don’t move like a robotic bodybuilder”.

On a partner: he wants someone selflessly selfish too. Kind is the only truly non-negotiable trait, but he does not want a partner always free when he wants to see them; he wants someone with a fulfilling career or passions who sometimes needs to prioritise it. The line he quotes is Jim Rohn’s: “I will take care of me for you, if you will take care of you for me.” His reasoning is partly selfish — people with purpose are happier, happy people make happy couples, which makes him happy. On longevity, his secret is blunt: “It’s a lot easier when you’re alive.” A minor health scare this year — a doctor telling him his blood pressure was high at his annual check, “for anyone, let alone someone who exercises as much as I do” — sharpened the prescription: exercise consistently, eat healthy, sleep enough, build close relationships, nurture mental health and be happy. He notes the tragedy of ambitious people “work[ing] 80 hour weeks just to die of a heart attack at 40”. On wealth: he used to demonise people who cared about money — “quite a British thing” — and now calls that nonsense. If you can get rich doing something you love, you absolutely should: wealth buys freedom, but more importantly lets you care for others. “If you’re someone that thinks of yourself as loving & selfless, please consider getting filthy rich.”

“Fill your cup continually… Be strong for the people around you; be the safe harbour for ships in the storm.”Wulfie Bain, Small Sparks

It is all habits, not one-off fixes, he insists: you cannot get “fit for life” in one year, or do a therapy blitz and never have a problem again — daily exercise, maintenance therapy, friendships sustained during busy periods. For a reader running an EMBA, a studio and a product career at once, the essay lands as the edition’s quiet warning: health and happiness are not the reward for the ambition. They are the constraint on it. “Health & happiness are great allies for staying the course.”

The takeawayAmbition that burns out its owner is not ambition — it is a loan taken out against the future, at ruinous interest. Fill the cup first, daily, as a habit: the providing, the parenting, the partnership and the work all run on the same fuel.

Durov Wants a Honeypot Too

Pavel Durov (@durov) • 12 November 2026 • Original post
