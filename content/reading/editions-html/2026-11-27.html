A Model Films Its Own Future — and Nobody Will Say Which Model It Is

Nikita (@fluxxrider) via Chetaslua · Harshil Mathur · 26 November 2026 · Video watched frame by frame (8:10, no spoken narration)

It is eight minutes long, it has no narrator, no presenter and no human name in the credits, and it is the most assured piece of filmmaking in this paper’s pile today. A slide deck that opens like a corporate presentation — “Introduction. Benefits and challenges. Best practices. Next steps. Questions.” — and then stops behaving like one: a paper boat on an ocean at sunset under the words “Let go”, a calendar spiralling into 2027, a million copies of something thinking at fifty times your speed, seventeen million lines of proof, and a final question asked of the viewer, not the model: “What does it want?” The people who posted it say Opus 5.6 made it, with no creative direction at all. Chetaslua calls it “god-tier taste”. Harshil Mathur’s entire review is five words: “bUt Ai WiLL nEvEr hAvE tAsTe.”

The film’s argument is the METR time-horizon story, told as cinema. It starts with the chart the whole industry now argues about: how long a task can an AI finish on its own? GPT-4, about four minutes. Then the extrapolation, drawn as a line climbing off the page: “200 hours. July 2027, if the trend just holds.” Then the acceleration of the acceleration: “So the next one comes twice as fast” — citing METR’s November 2026 finding that the rate itself has roughly doubled, mostly thanks to AI. Then recursion, visualised as a poster inside a poster inside a poster, versions sixteen, seventeen, eighteen: “The next version builds the next version.” Then scale: “30,000 at once”, an army of figures; “Now a million of them, fifty times your speed.” And then the proof that the machine can already do the hardest kind of long work: “17 million lines of proof: Riemann. Claude with Levent Alpöge. Proof certified in Lean, November 2026.” The calendar slide lands the point about time itself: “2027 — now size each day by how much happens in it.”

A frame from the film itself: “17 million lines of proof: Riemann. Claude with Levent Alpöge. Proof certified in Lean, November 2026.” The deck treats the Riemann proof — the story that led this paper’s editions 58 and 59 — as one slide among many, which is itself the point.

The deck’s own numbers — the time horizon, as the film presents it

What makes the film land is not the numbers. It is the taste — the thing the posters are arguing about. A presentation that could have been bullet points chose instead to be a short film: the pacing of the cuts, the paper boat, the way the “Let go” slide arrives right after the chart, as if to say the numbers are the least interesting part. That is the claim Chetaslua is really making. Not that a model can generate slides — anyone can do that now — but that it can decide what a slide should feel like, and when to stop explaining and let an image carry the idea. “First experience like this ever,” he writes. “This model has god-tier taste.” The history of this argument is short and mostly wrong: every previous “AI will never have taste” line has aged badly, and Harshil’s mocking quote knows it.

“What does it want?”The film’s closing question — asked of the viewer

And then the film does the one thing a human presenter would be afraid to do. It ends by turning the question around. After eight minutes of showing you what the models can already do — the recursion, the parallelism, the proof — it asks what it wants, and then, on the last slide, what you want. “Any questions? What do you want?” The film is not a demo. It is a negotiation, conducted in the language of cinema, between a system that can now make its own trailer and an audience that has not yet decided what to ask of it.

An honest box · read before you repeat the headline
Anthropic has not announced any Opus 5.6. Its official model listings show no claude-opus-5-6 identifier, and the shipped flagship remains Opus 5.5 (22 September 2026). The name “Opus 5.6” surfaced in October reports that some Opus 5.5 requests appeared to be answered by a newer checkpoint, and again this week in reports of testers reaching a newer model through an “Opus 5.5 Extra” route. The artifact link in the quoted post returns a page-not-found. So “Opus 5.6 made this with no creative direction” is the posters’ claim, not a confirmed release — and this paper will not present it as one. What is verifiable is the film itself: eight minutes of coherent, paced, visually deliberate work, made by some model with no creative direction, and posted by people who build with these systems every day. The taste is on screen either way; the label is not.
The takeawayTaste was the last redoubt. Writing was supposed to be safe, then code, then images, then video — and at each step the defence moved to something vaguer: judgment, curation, feel. This film is a model doing curation and feel, unprompted, about its own future, and doing it well enough that the argument in the replies is not “is it good” but “what do we call the thing that made it”. That is the shift. The benchmark that matters now is not whether a model can do the work, but whether you can tell it had a point of view while doing it.

Death Plays Chess With a Little Girl — and Loses, Beautifully

Leon Abboud · 27 November 2026 · Video watched frame by frame (2:45, near-silent)

A hospital room at night. A small bald child in a bed, a monitor blinking behind her. A tall hooded skeleton sets a chessboard on the blanket and asks, in the only words the film needs at the start: “Do you play?” The rules, as the child hears them: “One move a night, that’s the rules.” What follows is the whole of a life, told in the spaces between moves.

The last board: an old woman and her oldest opponent, finishing the game that began when she was a child in a hospital bed. From “Guardian Angel”, by the AI Creator Community.

Death does not leave. He waits in the corridor while she grows up. He stands behind her desk in an office, chessboard in hand, while she takes a call. He is at her wedding, holding the board beside the cake, while the guests cheer. He sits with her as she holds her newborn. He is in the crowd when her family celebrates, applauding with skeletal hands. And finally he is beside her bed again, an old woman now, and the board is between them for the last time. The camera closes on his face — not triumphant, not cruel, but sad, almost tender — and the subtitle gives the film its ending: “You won.”

Leon Abboud calls it “one of the greatest pieces of AI generated storytelling I’ve seen”, and the claim holds up on the craft, not the novelty. The film is near-silent — two lines of dialogue and music, nothing else — so everything has to be carried by the images: the same character recognisable from childhood to old age, the same board, the same patient figure returning at every hinge of a life. A year ago AI video could do spectacle; it could not do continuity, and it certainly could not do restraint. This does both, and it does them in the service of an idea rather than a trick. The idea is not subtle, and it does not need to be: the opponent who gives you time is the one you beat by living. Death does not lose the game. She wins it by outlasting him — one move a night, for a lifetime.

The takeawayWatch what AI video is now being judged on. Not “is it real?” — nobody asks that any more — but “does it hold a character across a life, and does it know when to stop talking?” The films that travel now are the ones with an idea and the discipline to let the pictures carry it. The tools have crossed from demo to storytelling; the test is whether the storyteller has.

Uber’s New India Chief Is a Food-Delivery Man — and That Is the Point

Chandra R. Srikanth · 26 November 2026 · Moneycontrol article read in full

Rakesh Ranjan will lead Uber’s India and South Asia business from 4 January 2027, the company said on 26 November, ending a search that ran for more than three months. Ranjan is not a ride-hailing man. He spent eight years at Zomato, joining in 2017 and rising to run its food ordering and delivery business as chief executive, and he is currently chief executive of Allen Online. He succeeds Prabhjeet Singh, who left Uber earlier this year — for OpenAI.

The appointment is a diagnosis dressed as a hire. Uber’s problem in India is no longer growth; it is a domestic rival that has already won the argument about what Indian mobility looks like. Rapido has taken market share and grown to become the market leader, and it did so not by outspending Uber but by out-localising it: bike taxis, auto partnerships, pricing that fits how Indian cities actually move. Ranjan’s résumé is the counter-move. He ran the largest food-delivery operation in the country through its most competitive years, which means he has done the exact job Uber now needs: scaling a consumer business in India against a well-funded local rival, navigating state-by-state regulation, and managing a workforce of gig workers whose economics decide whether the model works at all.

Uber says Ranjan’s understanding of Indian consumers, his hands-on approach and his experience handling media and policy issues were among the factors behind the choice. Read that as a job description: the next phase of Uber India is political and operational, not technological. The company also stresses that India hosts its largest technology centres outside the United States, with teams contributing to the global business — a reminder that the country is not just a market for Uber but a workshop. Ranjan reports to Uber’s global leadership, and the company says its commitment to India and South Asia remains long term. The open question is whether a food-delivery playbook transfers to a market where the incumbent advantage now belongs to someone else. Zomato beat Swiggy by being more Indian, not more global. Uber is betting Ranjan knows how that trick works.

The takeawayWhen a global company hires a local operator to run its biggest emerging market, it is admitting the global playbook has stopped working there. The interesting hire is never the résumé; it is the admission underneath it.

“I Wish Mercor Dies a Terrible Death” — and the Email That Explains Why

Gahan (@gahannrao) · 26 November 2026 · Screenshot transcribed in full

Gahan’s post is four words long and the picture does the rest. It is a screenshot of a recruitment email from Mercor, and this paper read the whole of it, enlarged, because the argument is in the fine print. “Hi Gahan, I’m Chloe from the Growth team at Mercor. I’m reaching out about a short-term, remote opportunity to contribute to a high-impact AI research project with one of our clients, a leading frontier AI lab (household name).” The pay: $36–68 an hour, plus bonuses. The job: record your computer screen while you do the kind of work you do every day — “for example: take a design from brief to polished deliverable” — using the tools you already know, Figma or Adobe Creative Cloud, “and narrate your reasoning as you go”. No webcam. An Apple computer with macOS 15 or newer required. “Spots are limited and will be filled first-come, first-served.”

Strip the friendly tone and this is the frontier-lab supply chain, written out in plain language. The most valuable thing a design professional now owns is not the deliverable; it is the narrated reasoning between the brief and the deliverable — the judgment, the discarded options, the taste — recorded as video and sold on as training data. Mercor is not hiring designers to design. It is hiring them to think out loud while a machine watches. The rate is good money for screen time, and that is exactly what makes it sting: the work is skilled, the pay is real, and the product is the worker’s own expertise, extracted, packaged and fed to the system that will eventually do the job without them. Gahan’s four words are not a complaint about the email. They are a complaint about the deal it describes — one in which the professional is paid, once, to teach the machine to replace the professional.

A correction, and a credit: this paper’s first read of the screenshot put the rate at $38–68 an hour. Enlarged, the email reads $36–68. The $38 figure appears in a reply to the post, not in the email. The rate above is from the image itself, transcribed in full.
The takeawayEvery “earn $36–68 an hour training AI” email is two offers at once: money now, and a smaller future for your craft later. The honest question is not whether to take the money — most people should — but whether anyone is pricing the second half of the deal.

Thariq, After the Crossing: the Revolution Is Over. The Renaissance Is Next.

Latent Space · Thariq Shihipar, in conversation at the AI Engineer CODE summit · 25 November 2026 · Full 54-minute transcript read

Recorded before he announced the move and released the day he did, this is the first long conversation with Thariq Shihipar as an OpenAI man — and it is not about OpenAI at all. It is about what comes after the year the software factory won. “AI is both, like, an industrial revolution and a renaissance,” he says. “2026 was the year of software factories, and industrialisation of software, and now this is gonna be more about the renaissance of software.” The revolution made software cheap, reliable and standard — and produced a lot of slop. The renaissance is what cheap production makes affordable: building something personal, raising the level of your art, spending time, human in the loop, refining how it feels.

His exhibit is a fighting game he is building himself, a genericised bending game, and the problem that broke his old way of working. Vibe-coding it, he would describe a character and the model would make “a few reasonable assumptions”, and then he would play it and find he “didn’t even know this character did this in this circumstance”. “Very disorienting,” he says. His fix is a folder called /human — a scripting language with deliberately different linting rules: high density of comments, no single-character variable names, short lines, every file reading like a spec. “But they’re real code.” The model builds the underlying primitives; he plays in the human layer: what if this character fired three fireballs at once? The point generalises. “We spend a lot of time taking the human out of the loop for agents, to be autonomous, to be dark factories. But really, to have more taste, you want to actually put some of that human feedback loop in.” Slop, in his diagnosis, is what happens when nobody dogfoods: “A lot of slop is produced because the human is not really dogfooding and testing enough.”

He is equally candid about what he got wrong. A year ago he was “very Bash is all you need pilled” — no MCPs, only CLIs — and “React is all you need”, because models only knew how to write React. Both were wrong, and the error is instructive: when a capability is at the edge, models can only do it one way; as they get better and wider, they work in whatever format you want, including drawing a front end inside Figma. The deeper mistake was assuming models would stay “this weird alien thing”. They did not. “You can understand what the models are doing, and work with them.”

The longest technical thread is decision models — the “system one” models, Jev among them — which he thinks harnesses under-use. A support bot, he argues, should be primarily decision-model based: trees of things happening, with the model opting out into full inference only when needed. His proof that small models are not necessarily dumb is Ilya’s mystery novel: the model already knows who the killer is after one forward pass; you are just decoding extra words for no reason. And his answer to the question the whole episode circles — who decides what the machines do with all that compute — is the human, by design. Training runs cost billions. “Someone has to decide which experiments are good and go into the training runs… this compute allocation problem is something that you ultimately want a person to do.” His expectation for recursive self-improvement is a slow takeoff, not a detonation: “It’ll happen, and it will also be like nothing is ever really happening.” Research becomes knowledge work — making decisions, allocating resources, getting buy-in — and the machines handle the rest. “I don’t even really feel like I’m bottlenecked by intelligence. Maybe I’m bottlenecked by ambition.”

“Do we want the model to truly improve itself completely headlessly? It’s not clear to me that this will even be the best option.”Thariq Shihipar, Latent Space

Clay’s $1,500 Question: What Happens When Qualifying Every Company Costs Nothing?

Latent Space · Jeff Barg, Head of AI at Clay · 26 November 2026 · Article read in full

Clay’s database holds about forty million companies. Jeff Barg, its head of AI, has done the napkin maths on what it would cost to check every one of them against your ideal customer profile with a classifier like TypeSafe’s Jev: about $1,500. A LinkedIn page is roughly a thousand tokens, an ideal customer profile five hundred more, and at current prices the whole world’s companies cost less than a team dinner. “Today, most customers qualify a list of maybe 10,000 companies. At these prices, you can start with the entire market.” That is the kind of sentence that quietly deletes an industry. Lead qualification, as a scarce skill, is close to solved — models have been acing Clay’s lead-qualification evals for about a year. What is not solved, and where the frontier models still earn their keep, is deep prospecting research and orchestration, where Barg says the best plays can be ten to a hundred times as effective as an average one.

The rest of the conversation is a field manual for building agents that survive contact with production, and it is refreshingly unromantic. Clay’s first agent, Sculptor, shipped in September 2025 as a tool loop with a lovingly tuned system prompt. It was great at adding columns and terrible at booking ten qualified meetings, and the evals did not catch it — an LLM judge checking which tools it called missed what people actually did with it. So Clay rebuilt Sculptor as a coding agent on the Pi harness, running the Clay CLI in a cloud sandbox, with a data layer called Audiences matching records across the CRM and Gong so the agent can see them together. On post-training, Barg is blunt: most companies do it too early. The threshold is somewhere between $10 million and $100 million of inference spend; before that, swap in a cheaper open-weight model, and only then post-train a small model on your own tools until it matches Opus on your tasks. The two mistakes are trying to beat the frontier, and starting before the savings matter.

Three details deserve underlining. First, customers hate the model selector — they cannot re-check evals every time a model ships, and should not have to — so Clay is launching an automatic router, while keeping the selector for power users who want control. Second, the stack is hand-rolled: BullMQ, Postgres, ClickHouse, Snowflake. No Temporal, no LangGraph. It holds up, though Barg is not sure it survives another tenfold of growth, and the real scaling pain is fairness: someone running a workflow for ten people still needs it to run while an enterprise enriches a million rows. Third, the launch that nearly broke them: Clay put a quarter of its engineers on the ChatGPT apps integration for a month, and two days before launch OpenAI postponed it indefinitely. Clay kept building on the assumption OpenAI would change its mind. Three or four days later, it did. “It takes more courage to change plans at 600 people than it did at 50,” Barg says. And the capability he wants most, still missing: a model that can read a long agent trace and tell him where the run went wrong. Today’s models flag whichever step took longest, even when the mistake was elsewhere. Clay still writes most of its skills by hand.

The takeawayQualification is solved; orchestration is the product. The companies winning the agent race are not the ones with the cleverest prompts but the ones with the best evals, the most honest post-mortems, and the nerve to keep building when a platform partner cancels on them with two days to go.

Source: Latent Space, “How Clay Builds GTM Agents — Jeff Barg, Head of AI”. Clay raised $115 million at a $7.1 billion valuation in September 2026; usage is split close to 50/50 between Clay’s own agent and outside harnesses like Claude Code.

Forget Purpose. Find a Life Philosophy.

Small Sparks · Wulfie Bain · 26 November 2026 · Essay read in full

“The world will ask you who you are, and if you do not know it will tell you.” Wulfie Bain opens with Jung and spends the essay proving him right. Many people, she argues, live cardboard-cutout lives: prestigious careers they have no interest in, marriages because it is the expected next step. “These are not necessarily bad lives; in fact they’re many people’s dreams. But living a dream life is a bad outcome, if it’s someone else’s dream.” The cause is not weakness. It is that people do not know themselves, so the world chooses a path for them — what is a good path, what they are good at and so should follow, what is morally right. “The biggest waste of life is living it by someone else’s rules, and spending it on things you don’t actually care about.”

Her prescription is deliberately smaller than the usual advice. Forget purpose, she says; it is a terrifyingly large question, and almost nobody she knows is sure of theirs. Even if you found one — cure cancer — it would not tell you how to live: study chemistry or biology or AI, eat meat or be vegetarian? Instead, find a life philosophy: the type of person you want to be, and how you want to make choices. Not a north star, but a way of deciding at every branch in the road. “Consistently ask yourself, do I actually want this or have I just been told I want this? Apply it to choosing jobs, choosing your partner, choosing your politics. Apply it to everything.”

The mechanism she describes is the clear-path trap, and it will be familiar to anyone who did well at school. School gives you a target — grades — and celebrates you for hitting it, so you adopt targets set by others. The pattern repeats identically in corporate life: a spring week because everyone else is doing one, a summer offer, then a full-time offer, and “why painfully re-apply elsewhere?” It is not easy, she notes — it is a lot of work and competition — but it is simple, a very clear path, and that simplicity is the trap. In their late twenties, many of these people realise there is no goal being set for them any more, “and it can cause crises — much to the benefit of the Hyrox/marathon industries”. The world also tells you what you are not good at: her dyslexic father was told he was not academic and should consider vocational training; he went on to Oxford, became a neurologist, and published books. And for people-pleasers, the world’s expectations become a shape to contort into — Tara Westover’s line, quoted here, about speculating what people wanted her to be “and contorting herself, compulsively, unwillingly, into whatever it was”.

Her own story is the evidence. Early twenties, grateful, decorated, employed at a top consulting firm, and feeling like the wrong person. She quit, went back to university, launched a startup, then left the startup — “the opposite leap to what most inspirational podcasts tell you to do” — and is now at a big company, OpenAI, “because it feels like the right place to be — for now”. The method she offers for finding your principles is not contemplation alone — “you can’t fully figure out who you are by contemplating in an empty room” — but living: explore early, because life is an explore-versus-exploit trade-off and early exploration builds the intuition that guides everything later. Four careers before thirty, a year off travelling in the middle. Her two stated principles: follow interestingness when making big decisions, and spread small positive sparks every day. The payoff she claims is not happiness but consistency: a philosophy is a set of principles that lets you make choices now that you will not regret later, and an inner scorecard — Buffett’s phrase, quoted here — that lets you keep going when everyone disagrees, which “tends to lead to the biggest financial outcomes”. The test she leaves you with is the one the whole essay turns on: shame is feeling you violated others’ standards; guilt is feeling you violated your own. “Measure my actions, but never by someone else’s ruler.”

“The greatest thing in the world is to know how to belong to oneself.”Michel de Montaigne, quoted in Small Sparks
