The Fingerprint in the Weights: Six Chinese AI Models Traced to Banned Blackwell Chips

Will Depue (@willdepue), via X • Full report read at Unemployment Labs, 13 November 2026 • Read the full report • Original post

Every AI model carries a hidden signature of the machine that created it, and this week someone finally read it. Will Depue, writing at Unemployment Labs, reports forensic evidence that the top four Chinese AI labs — DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group — have built their newest models on Nvidia’s Blackwell chips, the hardware barred from export to China without a US licence. Six models released since April 2026 carry the fingerprint. No leak, no insider, no satellite photo of a smuggled server rack: the evidence comes entirely from the models’ own published weights, and from a search across more than six trillion combinations of chip layout and seed number. The matches run from 49 to 1,385 standard deviations above chance. On a rented Blackwell GB200, Depue’s team reproduced Kimi K3’s untouched starting weights to a correlation of 0.999995. This is the biggest export-control story of the year, and it was proved with arithmetic.

The method is the story, and it is elegant enough to explain in one paragraph. Before training begins, a model’s billions of weights need starting values, and in PyTorch those values come from a pseudorandom generator called Philox, seeded with a single number. PyTorch splits the work across parallel threads on the GPU — 2,048 threads per streaming multiprocessor, or SM — so the number of SMs on the chip decides where each generated number lands. A Hopper H100 has 132 SMs; a Blackwell B200 or B300 has 148; a Grace Blackwell GB200 or GB300 has 152. Same seed, different chip, and the numbers land in different positions: only about 6% of the arrangement survives the move. Training then scrambles most of it — but not all of it. Weights attached to words or symbols the model never saw in training keep their starting values almost exactly, and even trained weights retain a faint trace of their origins, like a watermark bleeding through paint. Find the seed and the thread count that regenerate the preserved values, and you have identified the chip that created the model.

Four labs, one switch — Hopper to Blackwell, model by model

The verification is what lifts this from a clever inference to something close to proof. Depue’s team ran unmodified PyTorch on real hardware: a Hopper H100 reproduced all 57,344 preserved values in DeepSeek-V3 bit for bit, and none of V4-Flash; a Blackwell B300 reproduced V4-Flash’s preserved rows — 445 of 469 rows bit for bit, 461 of them at correlations above 0.99 — and none of V3. Change the seed by one and every match collapses. DeepSeek’s seed, it turns out, is 953049583, reused across generations; Moonshot’s Kimi K3 seed is 20256586. The method was also checked against ground truth: for 24 models whose developers have publicly named their training hardware, the forensic reading agrees in every case — Meta’s Llama 3.1 reads as 132 SMs, EleutherAI’s Pythia models as 108, Nvidia’s own Nemotron-3 Ultra as 152, matching its technical report. And the scale, once you accept the fingerprint, is industrial: the two largest runs, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro, each needed an estimated 3,000 to 4,500 Blackwell GPUs for two to three months — roughly 7,500 to 11,500 H100-equivalents — with the largest clusters costing $200–300 million each and the five runs together more than half a billion dollars, before counting experiments, earlier runs or serving. The report is candid that these are estimates, revised upward on 12 November after the first version assumed benchmark-level efficiency that real Blackwell runs have not achieved.

The limits matter as much as the findings, and the report states them plainly. The fingerprint identifies the hardware that initialised the weights; it supports, but does not by itself prove, that Blackwell also did the training — a lab could in principle generate weights on one system and train on another, though nothing suggests these labs did. One alternative survives scrutiny: AMD’s MI300X can be configured to use 148 or 152 of its processing units, and its random-number software can generate the same underlying sequences. The team tested it on a physical MI300X and found that for DeepSeek-V4-Flash, the preserved weights point to Nvidia — in the 377 values where the two vendors’ arithmetic differs, the published weight matches Nvidia’s in 363 and AMD’s in none. For the other five models the AMD test could not settle the question, but the report calls it very unlikely, and notes corroborating evidence: US officials and press reports had already alleged Blackwell use at DeepSeek and Moonshot, and Ant Group’s own public code includes benchmark results generated on a GB300. What the weights cannot say is where the chips were, who owned them, or how they were obtained — Blackwell systems can be rented from data centres outside China, and the evidence alone does not establish that chips were smuggled or that export controls were violated. The report’s conclusion is calibrated accordingly: strong evidence for six models, conclusive for DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and an urgent question about access that it leaves to the public.

“It is exceedingly unlikely that these results are a coincidence. Across six trillion combinations, the best accidental match should reach only about 8 sigma. The real matches range from 49 to 1,385 sigma.”Will Depue, Unemployment Labs, 13 November 2026

Why this lands so hard in this paper is the mechanism. Export controls have always been enforced at the border — at the shipment, the licence, the customs declaration. Checkpoint forensics enforces them at the artefact: the model itself testifies about the machine that made it, in numbers anyone can re-derive from the published weights. The code and the preserved weights needed to repeat the checks are public, and the comparison runs without a GPU. That changes the game for every lab publishing open weights: the initialisation seed is now a provenance record, and a lab that wants to hide its hardware must either stop publishing weights, change its initialisation software, or accept that the fingerprint will be read. Depue says there is more to come from other forms of checkpoint forensics, and that the findings were independently verified by senior researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic. The next question is not technical but political: what Washington, Beijing and Nvidia do with evidence that the world’s most advanced chips are training the models they were meant to be kept from. Trapit Bansal quote-posted the thread with a single character: “🤯”.

The takeawayWeights are not just a model — they are a receipt. A seed and a thread count, recoverable from public files, now say more about a lab’s hardware than any press release or export filing. If you build open models, your initialisation is your provenance. If you regulate chips, your enforcement just moved from the border to the checkpoint file.

The AI Traps, Episode 1: If AI Takes Our Jobs, Who’s Left to Buy the Stuff?

Avisha (@distilled_ai), Instagram reel, 12 November 2026 • 2m 56s, transcribed in full • Watch the reel • Paper: Hemenway Falk & Tsoukalas (2026), “The AI Layoff Trap”, arXiv:2603.20617

Rakshit flagged this one himself this morning, and it earns the flag: the sharpest three minutes on the AI-jobs question this paper has seen. Avisha — of DistilledScience, now applying the same debunking discipline to AI — opens the first episode of a new series, “The AI Traps”, with the paradox at the centre of the whole debate. If AI replaces us in all of our jobs, who is left spending money on the products the AI now builds? And if automating everyone hurts a company’s own total profits in the long run, why are they all racing to get there? The answer, she says, comes from a 2026 paper that treats the problem as game theory — and the paper’s model has three twists, each worse than the last.

The model is a toy economy called Cointown, and the toy is the point: strip away everything but the loop, and the trap becomes visible. Four shops, ten workers each, paid ten coins a day. Each worker spends eight coins in the four shops and saves two. The money flows in a nice loop — until a robot arrives that can do a worker’s job for four coins a day. Shop A replaces one worker and saves six coins. That worker stops spending anywhere, but the eight lost coins of spending are spread across all four shops, so Shop A loses only two while saving six. The savings go to the owner, who is already rich enough not to spend in Cointown at all. The town as a whole is two coins poorer. Now put yourself in the owner’s shoes, as Avisha does. Two choices: automate, or hold back. Automate all ten workers and you save forty coins a day if nobody else automates — pure profit. If the other shops automate too, you lose twenty a day. But if they all automate and you don’t, you lose sixty. No matter what the others do, automating is always the most economical decision for you. It is a prisoner’s dilemma with a balance sheet, and the rational move for every shop is the move that impoverishes the town.

Shop A automatesSaves 6 coins a day per replaced worker. Loses only 2 of the worker’s 8 coins of spending — the rest is spread across town.

The trapEconomists call it a demand externality: each shop keeps all of its savings but feels only a quarter of the damage.

Then the three twists. Twist one: competition makes it worse, not better. If one company owned all four shops, it would feel all eight lost coins and would never buy the robot. With two shops, each feels four — and the robot still wins. With a hundred shops, each feels almost nothing. That is more like the real world, Avisha notes, and it inverts the usual story: usually competition protects us; here it speeds up a bad situation. Twist two: better AI makes things worse. If robots can make more stuff, each shop hopes to outproduce its rivals and grab a greater share of sales — but every shop thinks this way, so the gains cancel out. Like the Red Queen in Through the Looking-Glass, everyone runs faster just to stay in place, and what is left is even more automation than before, and more stuff that people cannot afford. Twist three: even universal basic income does not solve it. Give each person two coins and they spend them, which raises the floor — but the shops still save the exact same amount by automating, so the incentive is untouched.

The prisoner’s dilemma, in coins per day — your shop’s outcome

The paper’s proposed way out is an automation tax, with the proceeds going to retraining workers — charge each shop for the damage it puts on its rivals, six coins in the Cointown example, in the same spirit as carbon credits. That would make hiring a robot worth it only if it saved nine coins, more than the eight it removes from the economy. Avisha is careful with the caveats, and they matter: the whole thing is a simplified model, and the authors themselves say there is so far no evidence of the trap actually in effect — no massive job loss yet. What has changed, she says, is the capability line. Until now, even the best models needed hand-holding to carry a task from start to finish, and wiring them into real-world systems was messy. She has spent the last few days with Fable 5.2, and she says plainly that she knows people whose entire jobs would probably be automatable by it. The layoff data is already moving — she promises a video with the full statistics soon, with the hiring impact concentrated on junior positions — and her worry is a tipping point: the models are now good enough that the trap, which has so far been theoretical, could start to bite. That is the thread to watch, and the reason this reel leads the flagged slot rather than sitting in the briefs.

“Usually competition protects us, but here it just speeds up a bad situation.”Avisha, “The AI Traps” Episode 1, 12 November 2026
The takeawayThe AI-jobs debate usually argues about whether the models are capable enough. This paper argues the capability question is the wrong one: once the models are good enough, the economics push every firm to automate whether or not it is good for the economy as a whole. The fix it proposes is not to slow the models down but to price the externality — an automation tax that makes a robot worth hiring only when it creates more value than it removes. Watch the junior-hiring numbers she promises next: that is where the trap would show up first.

Maguire Doubles Down: Nikesh Arora Is His Pick to Run Nvidia

Shaun Maguire (@shaunmmaguire) • 13 November 2026 • Original post

Two days after Jensen Huang’s resignation — the lead of Edition 50 — Shaun Maguire has restated his succession pick as a standalone post, and it is worth recording because the argument is now the debate. “Now that Jensen is out, my pick for his successor would be Nikesh Arora,” he writes, and the case is the same three attributes he set out in the eulogy: Huang mattered because he was technical, aggressive, and had the credibility required to be aggressive — and Arora, in his view, is one of only a handful of people who has all three on day one, plus being a world-class dealmaker with the relationships to keep Nvidia at the centre of the AI ecosystem. The evidence he cites is Palo Alto Networks: eight years under Arora, stock up roughly tenfold. “There’s no easy choice,” he concedes, “but he’d be my go to.” The board has said only that it will search inside and outside the company, and interim chief Colette Kress faces her first earnings call on Tuesday. The succession question is now the live one in this paper’s Nvidia thread, and Maguire’s is the most specific answer anyone has offered.

Two Eras of Shipping: One Engineer, 11x the Pull Requests, and the New Constraints

Neil Kakkar, The Pragmatic Engineer • November 2026 • Essay-challenge winner, 125 submissions • Read the original

The Pragmatic Engineer turned five in September and marked it with an essay challenge — a $10,000 prize for the best account of how tech workplaces have changed — and the winning entry, published this month, is the most data-driven thing this paper has read about the agent era. Neil Kakkar, a London software engineer with nine years behind him, kept records. At Bloomberg, a 20,000-person fintech with defined processes, he shipped slowly. At PostHog, a fast pre-AI startup, he shipped 2.4 times as much. At Tano, an AI-native startup where he was the first engineer, he now merges more than 200 pull requests a month — eleven times his PostHog rate, on roughly 100,000 lines of code a month he could never have written by hand. The headline number is the least interesting part. The essay is about what disappeared, what replaced it, and what it costs.

Pull requests merged per month — one engineer, three eras

What disappeared first was the machinery of coordination. At PostHog, building surveys took two engineers and a designer a month, with constant back-and-forth between backend and frontend; at Tano, one person owns a feature end to end — design, frontend, backend — because Claude Code makes that tractable, and the communication overhead that justified the team simply evaporates. Code reviews, the sacred cow, are optional at Tano once an engineer has a handle on the codebase: a review agent built with Claude Code GitHub Actions runs on every pull request, and in most cases it is the only review. Human review comments fall to near zero, the median time to merge drops under an hour, and the revert rate is six times lower — partly because the team fixes forward rather than reverting, and partly, Kakkar says, because agents are writing more bug-free code as the models improve. His working day is now a portfolio: three to five things in flight at once, agents running one to two hours per feature, a digestible HTML artefact generated with every PR for him to review, and the laptop left at the office overnight so the agents keep working while he switches off at home. Between 9 and 11 in the morning he opens 18% of his PRs, against 9% at PostHog — the morning is when the overnight runs land.

But the essay’s real contribution is the other side of the ledger: the new constraints. With writing cheap, the bottleneck moves to the engineer. Context switching becomes thrashing — the operating-system term for spending more time switching between tasks than doing them — and Kakkar finds himself firing up a Claude session for every small bug because each one takes under an hour, then realising he has spent the day reacting instead of working on sprint goals. Losing shared context is the second cost: nobody reads other people’s PRs any more, so nobody knows what anyone else is building, and the bus factor — the number of people who must disappear for a project to stall — collapses to one. His answer is AGENTS.md files, written context that lets anyone, human or model, pick up a product area cold. The third cost is pacing: front-loading the morning, running several workstreams at once, he was mentally exhausted by 3pm, and had to learn to prioritise the most important tasks rather than trying to do everything before lunch. Design, he notes, is being discounted at early-stage startups because AI produces good-enough design — and most AI design looks the same. And there is a hard limit: one 20x Claude Max plan per person, no infinite budget, and at API rates his usage would cost more than $8,000 a month. Hitting the limit, he says, forces focus — which may be a feature.

The mistakes are recorded with the same honesty as the numbers, and they are the most useful passages for anyone running agents. A reporting update needed minor currency units handled properly; the agent parsed them as floats and multiplied by 100, assuming every currency has 100 minor units — the Bahraini dinar has 1,000 fulūs — and a human reviewer would have caught that the code ignored an existing utility. A Postgres migration on a 25-gigabyte, six-million-row table blocked writes, stopped the ingestion pipeline, and timed out deploys; now every such migration runs concurrently, and the rule is baked into the review agent. Gergely Orosz, closing the piece, adds the industry context: since January, PR output is up roughly twofold on average across the industry, so Kakkar’s 11x is partly the move to an AI-native startup and partly the era — and he makes the point this paper has made before, that AI is necessary but not sufficient: give a pre-AI PostHog a time-traveller with future tools and you get at most a 1–2x gain, because the review queue becomes the bottleneck. Anthropic, he notes, automated code reviews away and then had to redesign its CI pipelines, then its source control, as each new constraint surfaced. The lesson is the essay’s closing line, and it is a management lesson, not a tooling one: culture — experimenting, open to being proven wrong, humble, curious — is a necessary and sufficient condition for AI adoption at every AI-era startup. The tools are the easy part.

“PRs are just one metric, but this contrast between an ‘AI-native’ and a ‘pre-AI’ startup is still a big one.”Gergely Orosz, The Pragmatic Engineer
The takeawayMeasure the agent era in constraints, not output. The PR count is a vanity metric until you ask what broke when it rose: reviews, shared context, pacing, design, token budgets. The teams winning are not the ones shipping fastest — they are the ones that notice each new bottleneck fastest and redesign around it, with culture doing the work the tooling cannot.

Airbound: The 21-Year-Old From Bangalore Who Wants to Move All Movement to the Sky

Packy McCormick, Not Boring • 28 September 2026 • Deep dive • Read the original

The second Substack in today’s paper is a founder story that reads like a companion piece to the first: same obsession with cost, same willingness to rebuild the stack, but in atoms instead of code. Naman Pushp is 21, from Bangalore, and taught himself physics, engineering and manufacturing on YouTube during COVID. At fifteen, with a $500 hackathon grant and no 3D printer, he printed drone plans on paper, glued them to foam and hand-sanded the parts — twenty hours a part, wearing a respirator — because he wanted a delivery drone and could not afford to buy one. That foam prototype became Airbound, which has since raised a $37 million Greenoaks-led Series A, hired engineers from SpaceX, Tesla and Android, and flown more than 13,000 autonomous flights, including over a thousand commercial runs carrying blood samples for Narayana Health — four kilometres in seven minutes, against thirty by road. Packy McCormick, who invested, calls him one of the most exciting founders he has backed. The reason is not the drone. It is the loss function.

Payload-to-empty-weight ratio — how much a drone carries vs how much it weighs

“There is one loss function that we care about: cost per kilogram-kilometre,” Naman told McCormick. Everything else is secondary. The logic runs through the whole piece like a proof. Today, everything that moves needs a human chaperone, and chaperones are expensive, so humanity built its movement networks around batching — buses, trucks, container ships, planes, all economical only when full. Autonomy removes the chaperone, so a vehicle can be sized around what it carries instead of who carries it. But a drone that weighs 83 pounds to deliver five pounds of cargo — Amazon’s Prime Air, by the piece’s numbers — is still paying the batching tax in dead weight. Airbound’s answer is to attack weight itself, with carbon fibre and a blended-wing body that borrows from birds not just in the wings but in the shape of the whole aircraft. The TRT, its first drone, is so light Naman can hold it with three fingers. The V2 carries five kilograms at a ratio of 1.67 — it carries more than it weighs. At that ratio, Naman’s claim stops sounding like a pitch: flight can dominate all existing transportation for 80% of all cargo and 90% of all human movement, at a cost per delivery heading for a dollar, and eventually a rupee.

McCormick’s essay earns its length by putting the claim in history, and the history is the argument. The Erie Canal cut the cost of moving a ton of grain from $100 and three weeks to $10 and six days, and in doing so made New York America’s financial capital — transportation technology decides where cities grow, what things cost, and who you spend time with. The car did the same thing in the other direction: batch machines — galleys, canals, railroads, container ships, jets — pull activity toward hubs, while individual vehicles let people spread out, and the map of the world’s biggest cities is the result of both. Then the piece makes its sharpest move: the automobile’s history. Steam carriages existed for a century before the car, but they were buses — economical only in batches. Benz’s compact engine made the individual vehicle possible in 1885; Ford’s maniacal focus on cost, betting on vanadium steel and a single model, took it from a rich man’s toy to half the cost of a horse and carriage within twelve years. Naman’s thesis is that aviation today is where the auto industry was in 1908: the eVTOL stack — electric, autonomous, no runway, small enough for a driveway — is 90% of the way to replacing cars, and cost is the last 10%. Human-grade eVTOLs cost $4–5 million today; the stack is there, the economics are not. Carbon fibre, he believes, is the Ford move. The piece is honest about what is not proven: regulation, above all beyond-visual-line-of-sight flight, is the binding constraint, and the vision — buildings you enter from the roof, billions of aircraft no more noticeable than birds — is a bet, not a forecast. But the flights are real, the hospitals have stopped using road deliveries for the routes Airbound flies, and Andhra Pradesh has signed on to build a network toward 10,000 flights a day. A 21-year-old who started by sanding foam in lockdown is, as McCormick puts it, in the early days of what will be a decades-long pursuit.

“If you move mass more cheaply than anyone else, you win.”Naman Pushp, founder, Airbound
The takeawayEvery logistics business is a batching business until someone removes the chaperone and then removes the weight. Airbound’s bet is that the winning variable in transport was never speed or software — it is kilograms per kilogram-kilometre, and the company that minimises it reshapes cities the way the canal and the car did. Watch the Andhra Pradesh network: if 10,000 flights a day works there, the model travels.
