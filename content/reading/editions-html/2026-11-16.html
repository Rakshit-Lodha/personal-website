Jensen’s Return Is One Tweet Away

Balaji Srinivasan • Nassim Nicholas Taleb • Financial Times, via zerohedge • 15–16 Nov 2026

Four days after Nvidia’s board pushed out its founder, the succession fight has a new front: the founder himself. The Financial Times, as quoted by zerohedge, reports that Jensen Huang is discussing a return as Nvidia chief executive with allies — and the timeline has already worked out the mechanics of how it happens.

Balaji Srinivasan lays out the lever. Under Nvidia’s bylaws, he writes, shareholders holding at least 15% of the votes for one year can demand a special meeting to remove sitting directors — a simple majority with cause, or a two-thirds supermajority without — and elect a new board. The anti-Jensen directors, in his telling, know they will be held responsible for a trillion dollars of market value erased if they hold out against a restoration. His summary is a binary: “If Jensen says no, the board wins. If Jensen says yes, the board loses.” And the ask, as he frames it, is almost comically small: “All Jensen Huang has to do is tweet that he wants to be reinstated as CEO and gears will start moving.” He adds a political reading — that Trump has no interest in removing his top AI chief executive, and that the spying allegation is, in his words, the same kind of manufactured story Trump himself was once subjected to.

Nassim Nicholas Taleb, who rarely weighs in on corporate governance, weighed in with both barrels: “The guy starts a company, invents a new technology, then is dethroned by a board composed of virtue faking empty suits who never did anything other than powerpoint presentations, while the Epstein crowd is enjoying champagne parties. His personal life is none of their business.” The Watcher Guru post he was quoting put it flatly: “Jensen Huang discusses returning as Nvidia CEO with allies.”

Step back and the shape of the last four days is now clear. On 12 November the board announced the transition, with Colette Kress as interim leader. On 13 November came the eulogies — Naval calling Jensen irreplaceable, Aravind Srinivas thanking him for 33 years. On 14 November, Gerstner and Baker’s letter demanded a slow, deliberate process that leaves every option open, including Jensen’s return; the standwithjensen petition crashed under sign-ups; Musk signed; Durov told founders to be the board. Now the story has moved from the board’s defenders to the founder’s own intentions — which is the only variable that was ever going to decide it.

The Talk That Explains Jensen: Perspective, Not Vision

Jensen Huang at Stanford, c. 2009 • Video transcribed in full by The Unread Pile

Seventeen years ago, Jensen Huang stood in front of Stanford students and explained, in an hour, everything that has happened in the last four days. This talk sat in the pile for a day while we transcribed all 59 minutes. It is the best thing in today’s paper.

It opens with a distinction he insists on. “Vision is an awfully big word,” he says. “I like to use the word perspective because it makes it possible for anyone to have one.” Everyone has a perspective; that is all vision means — you see the world in a way that is different from somebody else, and you see opportunities you believe are particularly important to address. Then he shows what a perspective is worth when it is right and everyone else thinks it is wrong.

The world of 1993, when three engineers started Nvidia: Windows 3.1, the CD-ROM about to be introduced, no PCs with networks, and the fastest microprocessor on earth a 66 MHz 486 DX2. “I don’t think any of you would even use it in your tennis shoes today.” Their perspective was that the PC could run 3D graphics programs — that you could explore new worlds and play games on it. The business plan, in his summary: take technology available only in the most expensive workstations, reinvent it, make it inexpensive, and the killer app was video games. Sand Hill Road told them there was no video game market: “People don’t start companies to play games.” He rang his mother to tell her he was starting the company. She asked what they did. “We build these things called 3D graphics chips and people would use them to play games.” Her reply: “Why don’t you go get a job?” Video games are now, he notes, the world’s largest digital media industry. “At the time, our common sense was unique.”

The talk is full of these near-misses and second-order effects. A struggling Silicon Valley company called Keyhole had built a 3D virtual world — start in space, see the Earth, zoom to any address — and couldn’t raise a penny. Nvidia put money in; Jensen showed the demo everywhere, telling people “this is the way we’re going to do search someday.” Google bought Keyhole and it became Google Earth, “the single largest downloaded, most frequently downloaded application in the history of mankind. Over 200 plus million downloads.” On the other side, Sequoia asked him about two Stanford kids with an internet thing — type in what you’re looking for and it shows up. “There’s no freaking way they’re going to make money. That stuff is free.” It became Yahoo. “Just because you’re a visionary doesn’t mean you’re a visionary [about] everything. Your perspective stems from your life experiences.” And the search engines that survived all made the same mistake in the same direction: they decided they were destinations, portals serving up content, and outsourced the search itself — “which made it possible for Google to start.” Two companies doing exactly the same thing, with the same core technology, ended up in radically different places because they had different perspectives.

By 1995 there were 50 to 70 startups doing exactly what Nvidia was doing; over the years they competed with about 200 companies — IBM, HP, Silicon Graphics, Sony, 3DFX, S3. “NVIDIA today is the only surviving computer graphics company in the world.” Why? His answer is the operating system of the whole talk: understand the essence of your business. Theirs was semiconductor technology, and Moore’s law was “not so much a physical law” as “a law of competition” — twice the performance every year or two, at half the cost. 3D graphics was insatiable: if they made something twice as good every year, customers would want it even if they said it was too expensive. So for the first five years they ignored their customers. Dell, HP, IBM and Gateway all told them the product spec was too much money. “Sometimes you have to ignore your customers… they don’t know the nature of your business.” He took “the last few million dollars of the company’s money and built a chip that is way, way, way too big” — and it was in allocation for its entire life, until the next generation, at twice the price, cannibalised it.

Then the reinvention. By the late nineties he concluded 3D graphics would become good enough as a fixed-function device, and the company had to make the GPU programmable — an artistic medium, so games could be stylistically different from one another. That crossing, the programmable shader, “almost killed the company.” The first chip of that generation, GeForce FX, was “a baby only a mother can love” and “almost broke our back. But if we didn’t build that chip, I am sure Nvidia would be dead today.” The accident that followed is the origin story of everything Nvidia later became: Stanford researchers bought graphics cards from Fry’s, discovered the Cg language let them program the GPU for other work, and got molecular dynamics and fluid dynamics running twenty times faster. A hundred times faster, he points out, is roughly ten years of Moore’s law — “to put ten years’ worth of computing resources in the hands of scientists, researchers, engineers, unbelievable benefits.” That became CUDA, and general-purpose GPU computing.

“Experimentation requires exploration. Exploration will result in failure. Unless you have a tolerance for failure, you would never experiment. And if you don’t ever experiment, you would never innovate. If you don’t innovate, you don’t succeed. That’s it.”— Jensen Huang, Stanford, c. 2009

The second half of the talk is about building the company rather than the product — culture, organisation, people. Innovation, he says, is impossible without a culture of risk-taking, and risk-taking has two parts: the skill of taking calculated risks, and the courage. “If you want to be successful, I would encourage you to grow a tolerance for failure.” Not failure as an identity — “if you fail often enough, you actually might become a failure” — but failure that is fast and cheap, governed by what he calls intellectual honesty: assess continuously whether something makes sense, and if it is the wrong decision, change your mind. “A lot of people say CEOs are always right and they never change their mind. That doesn’t make any sense at all to me.” Google, he notes, ships everything in beta so it can try things and take them out if they fail. That is the mechanism.

The founding details are the kind you only get from the founder. All three were paid the same $100,000 salary and held equal shares — “that’s just simply fairness” — and the CEO decision took about one sentence: “All right, Jen, so you’re the CEO, right? Okay. That was done.” He had never taken a business class, never used PowerPoint (it was Persuasion on the Mac), bought a Mac to use it, and never finished the business plan. He read the first three or four chapters of Gordon Bell’s How to Start a High-Tech Company and went to work. The first day of work was his 30th birthday, 17 February; the three of them met every day in a founder’s townhouse in Fremont, and for months the big event of the day was deciding where to go for lunch. The lawyer asked how much money he had in his pocket: $200. “For $200, I bought 15%, I think it was 20% of NVIDIA. So it was a good deal.” The other two did the same. VCs, he says, don’t invest in business plans, because business plans are easy to write. “They invest in great people… do they trust you? Your reputation matters.” And the market has to be big enough: if they put in $10 million and the market is only $20 million, they never get it back — but if it is a $200 billion market, it is a rather different thing.

Two answers in the Q&A land hard in 2026. On succession: picking three names for the board to consider if he gets run over by a bus is “a toxic, toxic process” — the better way is to develop so many leaders that the board has many choices, including outside. On why anyone should start a company: “Money is the only singular reason not to start the company… Ideas are a dime a dozen… You have to have a perspective that’s unique that you feel really strongly about.” And on the biggest challenge of all: reinvention. “Every successful thing needs to be torn down at some point and be rebuilt… reinventing the company every 10 years is almost a necessary thing… I love the process of reinvention.” His forecast for the company: it has the opportunity to become one of the most important technology companies in the world. His forecast for himself: “I am 80 years old and I’m here talking to students and I hope I’m still the CEO.” He was 46 when he said it. He is 63 now, and the board has just discovered how seriously he meant it.

The takeawayRead the talk as a manual for the current fight. The board fired a man whose entire operating system is reinvention, intellectual honesty, and comfort with ambiguity — and whose own succession philosophy was to grow many leaders, not anoint one. The talk also explains why the timeline is so sure he will be back: nobody else in the building has his perspective, and perspective, as he says, is the whole game. “As a startup, you’re always going out of business. That’s the definition of a startup. An enterprise that is nearly out of business all the time.” Nvidia, this week, is learning what that feels like from the other side.

Everyone wants zero catastrophic risk before shipping anything.” The personnel moves are offered as evidence: Tibo moving to safety, Andrew Ambrosino taking his spot, GPT-6.1 Astra “so locked down”. Mira Murati, Still Building

Rishabh Karnawat, via Autumn AI • 15 Nov 2026

Mira Murati’s life, as summarised by an AI research tool and saved by Rakshit, reads like a compressed history of the last decade of AI — and it ends, this month, at a $40 billion valuation.

The timeline, as the post lays it out: born in 1988 in Vlorë, Albania, then the most closed-off country on earth; graduates high school at 16 and leaves for Canada, then the US; 2013, joins Tesla as a senior product manager on the Model X, then the Model 3; 2018, joins OpenAI and leads ChatGPT’s development as chief technology officer; November 2023, interim chief executive of OpenAI for two days after Sam Altman’s firing; joins the attempt to bring Altman back a week later, then withdraws; 2024, launches Thinking Machines Lab, one of the biggest seed rounds in AI history; January 2026, parts ways with her chief technology officer over alleged misconduct, which he disputes — and 58 minutes later OpenAI announces he is coming back; brings in PyTorch co-creator Soumith Chintala as CTO; by April, Meta has hired five of her founding team; March 2026, Nvidia invests and she locks in a gigawatt of compute; July 2026, releases Inkling, her first open-weights model; November 2026, reportedly closing $5 billion at a $40 billion valuation. Only she and John Schulman remain from the original co-founders. She is 37.

The post’s closing line is doing the work: “A collapsing country, a boardroom coup, a co-founder fallout, and she’s still building.” It pairs neatly with Nathan Lambert’s Interconnects piece in today’s Substacks section, which asks whether Thinking Machines can become the frontier open-model player the US now needs — and answers, cautiously, that Inkling 2 Small is the best Western open-weight model in a while, but still far behind Kimi K3.1 Pro, and that a $40 billion round “won’t be enough to keep pace with the closed frontier for long.” The two stories together are the open-model dilemma in miniature: a founder who has survived everything the industry can throw at her, running a lab that still has to prove it can keep up with the closed giants.

The takeawayWatch the people, not just the models. Murati’s story is a reminder that the open-model ecosystem’s scarcest resource is not compute or capital — it is founders who keep building after the boardroom coup, the poaching raid, and the co-founder fallout.

Fable 5.2 Wrote a Transformer. It Runs on a Pentium.

Umair Shaikh (video verified frame by frame) • Deedy • 15 Nov 2026

The most charming video in the pile has no narration at all — just a floppy disk, a vintage laptop, and a 367,000-weight language model chatting at four letters a second. We watched the whole thing, frame by frame.

The claim, from the post: “Fable 5.2 wrote the transformer. Opus 5.5 generated the training data. The result: a 367K parameter language model that fits on a floppy disk and runs on a Pentium. AI is now building AI for hardware from 1995.” The video backs up the shape of it. It opens on a hand holding a 3.5-inch floppy in front of a battered old laptop, then cuts to the screen: a DOS-style interface titled “TINY — NEURAL INTERFACE 2.1 — 367,000 WEIGHTS,” with a conversation pane on the left and a neural monitor on the right showing confidence at 100%, speed at 7.5 letters a second, and the network as four layers, 96 wide.

The demo is honest about what 367,000 weights can and cannot do. Asked who it is, TINY replies: “A small quiz robot.” Asked for a joke: “Why did the PC go to the doctor? It had a virus.” It knows a triangle has three corners and that a circle “has no straight sides.” And then, asked for the capital of the United States, it answers with total confidence: “The capital is Havana.” The speed has dropped to 4.2 letters a second by then, and the model is still typing when the clip ends. That wrong answer, delivered at 100% confidence, is the whole story in miniature: the capability is real, the scale is real, and the jaggedness that Ethan Mollick wrote about in the Fable 5.2 piece two editions ago is still there at every scale.

The tiny model, by the numbers (from the video itself)

Deedy’s list, posted the same day, is the other side of the same coin — what Fable 5.2 does at full scale. His “most insane things” so far: a video explainer of the Anthropic S-1, a 3D rendering of a human face (he marks it a fail), a physics-accurate cricket game for mobile, an animated video story explaining the entire Bible from a PDF, a 40,000-queries-per-second turbo scraper in Rust “super optimized in every single way,” an anime character drawn, rigged and enacted in a scene (meh), algorithmic art, and a visualisation of money flows in the US healthcare system. He promises a megathread of results from each if people are interested. Put the two posts together and you get the honest shape of the frontier right now: at one end, a model that can build a working scraper at 40,000 queries a second; at the other, a model it built itself, running on a Pentium, confidently wrong about Havana.

The takeawayThe floppy-disk demo is the better benchmark. It shows what the new models can do when you strip away the datacentre — and what they still cannot do, which is know what they do not know. Confidence at 100%, capital at Havana: jagged AGI, in 367,000 weights.

The Great Hall Candles Were Real

PotterWorldwide (video transcribed in full, 0:33) • 15 Nov 2026

How they created the original Great Hall set for the films: not with CGI, but with hundreds of real candles, tiny strings, and a pulley system — lit by hand before every take.

The 33-second behind-the-scenes clip, narrated throughout, is a small masterclass in practical effects. “One of the coolest things about the Great Hall set was that those floating candles were real,” the narrator says. “We decided to do them practically as opposed to creating them in a CGI environment. So we placed hundreds of floating candles above tables with tiny strings, and all of those candles had to be lit before each take, and all of them were on a sort of pulley system where they moved up and down, so they felt like they were floating.” The payoff shot — the camera craning down through the candles, the strings visible — got applause in the screening room.

It lands in this paper for a reason. Yesterday’s edition covered the HBO Harry Potter series’ first Great Hall feast scene; today’s clip is the original, and the contrast is the story. Twenty-five years ago, the answer to “how do we make candles float?” was labour: hundreds of candles, lit by hand, on pulleys. The modern answer would be a render farm. The screening-room applause is the giveaway — the crew knew, watching the strings, that the impractical thing was the better thing. Craft, like perspective, is a choice about what to do the hard way on purpose.

The takeawayThe best effects are the ones somebody chose to do for real. Hundreds of candles, lit before each take, because floating felt better than rendered — that is a product decision, and it applies far beyond film sets.

Open-Source AI Without Jensen

Nathan Lambert • Interconnects • 14 Nov 2026 • Read in full

The resignation of Nvidia’s chief executive is a major setback for the American open-model ecosystem — and not only because of the money. Jensen was the person who used his power to keep the ecosystem alive, and nobody obvious replaces him.

Lambert’s argument starts with Nvidia’s interest, which is real but narrow: open models stop a few giant customers from building their own chips and cutting Nvidia out, so Nvidia has a financial reason to fund the ecosystem. But supporting it “is a tricky puzzle more than an engineering problem.” Jensen, he writes, “has been flooding the zone with funding in a way that will very likely dry up,” and the open-model economy is in its nascency — the crucial years for establishing use-cases, revenue flow, and motives to keep training the next generation. The upside case is that open models have correctly latched onto “ownership of intelligence” and enterprise post-training as their lane; the open question is how big the downstream market is. The downside is that truly commoditised intelligence is very far away while compute is this scarce: if open-weight models are served at giant inference margins, as DeepSeek’s DeepInfer release showed, they are far from a commodity, and “the closed labs with better economies of scale and integration from model to datacenter and chips will win.”

The sharper half of the piece is political. “Jensen was more than a cheerleader. He would stop a lot of shit from going wrong once early winds were stirring. He was willing to use his power on open models.” Lambert’s biggest near-term worry is sloppy regulation — state-level first, then national — driven by a swell of Democratic government funding towards candidates who want to regulate the industry in the name of safety. “We’ve been one, strong Chinese open-weight model away from a crisis since this summer. It still holds.” No major hack driven by a popular Chinese open-weight model yet, he writes, but it is a matter of when, not if — and when the open-weight hawks come hunting, the ecosystem needs someone to encourage caution and an ecosystem perspective. Knocking over the open-source domino is easy; unwinding the downstream concentration of power, the reduction in safety research, and the stifling of long-term innovation is far harder.

The prescription is to incubate a truly frontier open-model player in the US. Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis, quoted from a ChinaTalk emergency podcast, puts the problem plainly: “It’s not money, it’s talent, man.” The best people work at Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind and Meta; Thinking Machines has a cracked team but is not as good as Moonshot, which has a compute and deployment deficit rather than a talent one. Inkling 2 Small is the best Western open-weight model in a while — still far behind Kimi K3.1 Pro. Mira Murati’s next round, supposedly a done deal at a $40 billion valuation, “won’t be enough to keep pace with the closed frontier for long.” The likely allies, Lambert writes, are the hyperscalers — Zuckerberg, Jassy, Satya. But Jensen carried the mantle for about two years, and “times were messy before he stepped up clearly in 2025. We’re regressing in the operational capability of our movement, when the stakes and attacks are destined to rise.”

“Shoes have been emptied that will never fully be refilled.”— Nathan Lambert, Interconnects
The takeawayThe open-model ecosystem just lost its protector, its funder, and its phone call in a crisis — all in one person. The work now is coalition-building, not engineering: someone has to pick up the phone when the next crisis comes, and the job description starts with talent, not money.

Syngene After the Fall: A Turnaround Worth Tracking, Not Yet Worth Buying

The Loggical Investor • 14 Nov 2026 • Read in full

From around ₹940 in November 2024 to around ₹386 in July 2026, Syngene International lost more than 55–60% of its market value. The interesting question is not how far it fell — it is whether the business underneath is starting to change.

The decline has three causes, and the piece takes them in order. First, the Zoetis disruption: Syngene manufactured Librela, the animal-health giant’s monoclonal antibody for osteoarthritis pain in dogs; after building up inventory, Zoetis sharply cut orders, leaving a hole management puts at around US$50 million of annual revenue. Second, capacity: heavy investment in biologics manufacturing, including the Bayview facility in Baltimore and Stelis Unit 3, built a cost base for a level of activity that has not arrived — operating EBITDA margin fell from around 29% in FY25 to 25% in FY26 and then to 12% in Q1 FY27. Third, leadership churn: Jonathan Hunt stepped down as managing director and chief executive in January 2025, Peter Bains followed, Siddharth Mittal took over in July 2026, and a chief financial officer transition was announced in Q2 FY27. Guidance was revised down repeatedly — flat revenue for FY27 became a single-digit decline after Q1 — and, as the author notes, “a stock can fall 50% and still not be cheap if earnings expectations continue to decline.”

What makes the Q2 FY27 call worth reading is the candour. Kiran Mazumdar-Shaw, now active again as executive chairperson, said the company had “really taken the eye off the ball,” and described research services as a “race to the bottom” — commoditised chemistry work, priced by the hour, easy for clients to move between providers. The reset has four parts. Discovery Services moves from the full-time-equivalent model — selling scientists’ time — to fee-for-service, selling integrated projects and deliverables, with AI and automation tools like ChemVoyager and unified scientific leadership under chief scientific officer Dr Jayashree Aiyar. The contract development and manufacturing business becomes the long-term growth engine, with the first manufacturing agreement signed for Bayview: process development in India in FY27, technology transfer and drug-substance manufacturing at Bayview from FY28, commercial production subject to approval with the second half of calendar 2028 as a best case. Bengaluru Unit 3 has clinical and filing-supply contracts with multiple partners. And the Zoetis capacity at Biocon Park becomes available for other commercial clients from FY28 — two milestones to track, the author says: whether the planned Q4 FY27 Zoetis deliveries materialise, and whether the freed capacity gets filled.

Syngene’s margin slide — and the first step back

The numbers are moving in the right direction, slowly. Dose 219: Genomes, Welding Robots, a Sentient Sun, and Voyager’s Last Mile

Packy McCormick • Not Boring • 13 Nov 2026 • Read in full

The week’s dose opens with the news this paper leads with — Jensen’s resignation, with “heavier shoes” said to be coming — and then gets on with the progress, which is considerable: a genome model that doubles its hit rate, a $300 million welding-robot round, a space data-centre plan measured in gigawatts, and a spacecraft one light-day from home.

Start with Google’s AlphaGenome 2, released on Wednesday as Nvidia was in turmoil. The first AlphaGenome took a million letters of DNA and predicted what they do, averaged across whole tissues; averages hide things, like variants that cause disease in only one rare cell type. AlphaGenome 2 takes the same million letters plus a condition — a tissue, a cell type, a cell state, or data from a few dozen of your own cells it has never seen — and predicts what the DNA does there specifically. It trained on more than 107 million individual cell profiles from 68 human and mouse tissues, and on a benchmark of 28 immune cell types it found 30% of the variants scientists believe are causal, against 16% for the old model. Genome-wide association studies have handed science an enormous pile of disease-linked variants with no clear gene or cell attached; AlphaGenome 2 can pick up signals computationally, which means working through them far faster. It still misses most of them — 30% is 30% — but doubling in one generation is real progress. “Bad time to be a disease.”

Then the deal of the week in biotech: argenx is acquiring Unnatural Products, the macrocycle company, for $550 million in cash upfront and up to $595 million more on milestones. Macrocycles are ring-shaped peptides between small molecules and antibodies — the precision of a biologic in a pill that can slip inside cells to hit targets antibodies cannot reach, an oral GLP-1 or a cancer therapeutic among them. The team will become argenx’s home for peptide discovery, working on a pill aimed at FcRn, the same target as Vyvgart, which did $1.72 billion in sales last quarter.

The industrial item is Path Robotics, the Columbus, Ohio company that makes robots that weld. Normal welding robots repeat a programmed path, which fails when every part is slightly different — which is always. Path’s model, Obsidian, looks at the actual joint each time, plans the custom weld, and adjusts as it goes. On Tuesday it announced a $300 million Series E led by Georgian at a $2.45 billion valuation, with revenue up 17 times in 18 months and customers including Lockheed Martin, Saronic, and HII, the Navy shipbuilder, with which Path signed a $600 million deal earlier this year. There is also a robot dog that welds — Rove, a Boston Dynamics quadruped with Path’s tech, 50 pre-orders, headed to shipyards and construction sites next year. “If we want to build big things, we’re going to need a lot of welders, whether of the human, robot, or robot dog variety. Learn to weld.”

The most audacious item is SpaceX’s Starmind. On Tuesday, SINGULUS, a small German company that makes the machines that make solar cells, announced SpaceX had signed a contract for a turnkey heterojunction cell production line, to be delivered in the first half of 2027 to SpaceX’s satellite factory in Bastrop, Texas — the first line worth a little over €60 million, with more coming, and a plan to scale to “more than 100 GW of production equipment.” Elon Musk’s version, posted the same night: “SpaceX has ordered 100 GW/year of HJT solar manufacturing equipment… to create a sentient sun.” The arithmetic, as Packy notes, is preposterous: each Starmind is designed with a 75-metre wingspan and a 210 kW solar array, so 100 GW a year is enough for something like 475,000 satellites a year, against about 15,000 active satellites in orbit today. The commitment so far is €60 million; the announcement was probably worth much more than that to the supplier’s market cap. “A promise to build 475,000 space data centers a year is also exactly the kind of thing you’d expect to hear — and for people to take at least somewhat seriously — if we were in a bubble.” But if SpaceX builds the panels and does not send them all to space, consumers win in the form of more cheap, clean energy.

And the dose closes, beautifully, in deep space. Next Wednesday, 14 years after crossing the heliopause in 2012, Voyager 1 will pass one light-day away from home — 49 years after launch, the furthest anything we have ever made has travelled, by far, and still almost nothing: one 34-trillionth of the way across the observable universe, a journey that at this pace would take 1.67 quadrillion years. The dose also notes a study in Aging Cell in which LSD, in rats, pushed brain gene activity in the opposite direction of ageing — mapped across 3,086 human prefrontal cortex samples, from donors aged two days to 106 years — more strongly than exercise or an enriched environment. From outer space to inner space, as Packy puts it, in one dose.
