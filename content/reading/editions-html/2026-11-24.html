Jensen’s Back — and the Market Just Had Its Biggest Day Ever

NVIDIA Newsroom · 23 November 2026 · Release read in full · Market data: CNN, Investopedia, Reuters

Twelve days after Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia’s chief executive, the board voted unanimously to bring him back — and the stock answered with the largest single-day gain in market value in the history of any company. Nvidia closed Monday up 31.48%, adding roughly $952 billion in a session, back to just short of a $4 trillion valuation. The Nasdaq rose 6.51%, its biggest day since April 2025, and the S&P 500 rose 4.73%. Deedy’s version of it, saved by Rakshit hours after the news broke, is the cleanest summary in the pile: “King Jensen’s return to Nvidia is the single biggest change to market cap from a news event for any company in all of history: +$1T.” The number is, to the nearest round figure, right.

The reinstatement, effective immediately, restores Huang as president, chief executive and board director. The release is careful in a way that tells you how much negotiation sits behind it: the board says it is “satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang’s candor and ability to lead”, that it “received input from senior levels of the United States government”, and that it is “confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk”. Harvey C. Jones stays as board chair; Colette Kress, who served as interim chief executive, is thanked and returns to her role as chief financial officer. Huang’s own statement is humble to the point of penance: “I take this responsibility with the greatest humility and renewed commitment.”

What the release does not do is close the matter. The board’s committee of independent directors, formed after reporting that Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort, will proceed to its conclusion, and the board has formed a new National Security and Export Control Committee of independent directors, plus a search for a chief compliance officer. The company will, it says, continue to cooperate with ongoing government investigations. The return is real; the scrutiny is not over.

“I want to thank all of you. I’m terribly sorry for putting you through all of this. I’ve made some very terrible mistakes and I’m going to work on coming back. Thank you all for the second chance. I’m going to be a better CEO the second time around.”— Jensen Huang, first day back, to Nvidia staff (GeekPark clip)

That is from the eighty-second clip Siqi Chen saved, filmed inside an Nvidia office on Huang’s first day back: a circle of applauding employees, Huang in the black leather jacket, hands clasped, apologising twice over. Halfway through, a colleague steps forward and hugs him — the hug Siqi jokes he would have declined, “out of an abundance of caution”. The clip is the human half of the market story, and it matters: the board’s statement is about candour and national security, but the room’s reaction is about a founder the workforce plainly wanted back.

The politics are impossible to separate from the price. Semafor reported that President Trump dialled into the Nvidia board meeting to argue for Huang’s return; the White House told Semafor’s Shelby Talcott that Trump “was totally involved” and that “we have to protect our geniuses”. Trump himself said he secured a deal with the board. Bloomberg reported the board decided without consulting senior leadership, who were informed only at the last minute. And the market had, until minutes before the announcement, priced almost no chance of this: a Polymarket contract on Huang rejoining by 30 November sat at about 3%. The chair had said publicly days earlier that Huang would not be rehired, and the board had already rejected an earlier offer from Huang to return. Twelve days is a short exile, and the reversal is total.

Monday 23 November — the day in one chart

Pavel Durov’s gloss, also saved, is the sharpest one-liner in the pile: “Jensen’s back. Nvidia’s board finally accepted that a CEO has to evaluate Chinese models personally. You can’t delegate that.” It is a joke, but it points at the real governance question underneath the celebration: the board reinstated a chief executive while its own investigation into the affair allegations and the foreign-intelligence reporting is still running, and while Congress has hearings to come. The market has voted that the founder is worth more than the uncertainty. The next few weeks will test whether the board’s new committees and the compliance search are enough to make that vote stick.

The takeawayThe edition’s through-line, in one number: a single person was worth a trillion dollars of market value in a day. That is not a normal fact about a public company, and it is the reason this story leads the paper. Everything else in this edition — agents that ship code, models that run companies, founders who leave labs — is downstream of the same question: how much of the value now sits in one person’s judgement, and what happens when that judgement is doubted.

The Coin Flip: Sam Bankman-Fried, Interviewed from Prison by a 14-Year-Old

Eli Goldfine, SuperCycle · Video, 1 hour 22 minutes · Transcribed in full · Carried over from Edition 60

The most arresting item in this edition was also the longest-delayed: an 82-minute interview with Sam Bankman-Fried, conducted from federal prison in Brooklyn by Eli Goldfine, who is fourteen. It was carried over from yesterday because the transcription was still running at publication time; it is finished now, all 4,920 seconds of it, and the interview is stranger and more interesting than the premise. SBF is, for the first hour, mostly himself: the same rapid-fire expected-value reasoning, the same refusal to flinch at a number, applied now to Anthropic, to superintelligence, and to his own sentence.

The interview’s centrepiece is the coin flip. Goldfine asks SBF about a bet he once described: a coin with a 49% chance of destroying the world and a 51% chance of doubling it. Would he take it? SBF does not dodge. He says, in effect, that the framing is wrong — that in the real world the upside is not a permanent doubling, because someone else would get there eventually, while the destruction is permanent, and the uncertainty in every part of the bet makes destruction the worse side of it. “It’s much easier to destroy than it is to build,” he says. The man who once treated the world as an expected-value calculation now argues, from prison, that the calculation itself was the mistake.

“You told them you would take a bet that had a 49% chance of destroying the world.”— Eli Goldfine, to Sam Bankman-Fried

The second half of the interview is the one Rakshit’s pile will care about most, because it is about Anthropic. SBF put close to $500 million into Anthropic’s Series B — nearly all of what the company was raising at the time, he says, because Anthropic needed about half a billion dollars of compute to bootstrap its models and did not want to sell more of the company than that. Goldfine presses him: why not more, if he believed what he believed? SBF’s answer is a small masterclass in venture discipline — companies get damaged by their own cash, raising too much and blowing it on ads because it feels like they have to do something with the money — and it lands, in November 2026, in the same week Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 a share and raised $81.3 billion. SBF’s own prediction, in the interview, is that Anthropic will be “at over a trillion”. He is, on the IPO’s own numbers, early by an order of magnitude; the gap between his trillion and the market’s $81 billion is itself a story about how much of AI’s value is still a bet on the future rather than a price on the present.

His case for the valuation is the same case Wang made in yesterday’s edition, from the other side of the bars: AI is becoming one of the core sectors of the economy, worth tens to hundreds of trillions in cumulative market cap, and there are only a single-digit number of companies that could plausibly fill it — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google’s labs, xAI. “You’re talking at least trillions of dollars each in value for the top couple,” he says. Anthropic, on current model strength and product usefulness, is “one of, if not the top AI company”, with the best AI software-development tools in the world.

On timelines, he is strikingly un-dramatic. Superintelligence, he says, is probably not a binary event. Over the next five years, he expects piece after piece of society to be “basically subsumed by AI” — software development and maths research already, games long ago — with human oversight dropping off over time. The line from human to superhuman is, in his telling, blurry: “over a matter of months and years [it] goes from 80 IQ to 180 IQ”, with no single point at which it clearly snaps. When Goldfine asks him to put a number on p(doom) — the probability of a catastrophic AI incident killing a good portion of humanity — he gives one: “less than 50%, more than 10%… somewhere in the 25% range.”

And then the interview circles back to the coin flip, this time with his own life as the stake. Goldfine offers him a coin: 51% chance of release tomorrow, 49% chance of doubling his remaining time. SBF takes it instantly. His sentence, he says, is effectively a life sentence — his actual sentence is 25 years, but he frames it as life — and his goal is not to guarantee a few quiet years in a cabin at sixty. It is to have a positive impact on the world, and that requires being out. If the cost is losing non-productive years at the tail end of his life, “that’s absolutely [a] price I pay”. It is the same expected-value machine, pointed at himself, and it is the most honest moment in the interview: the philosophy did not change in prison. Only the stakes did.

SBF’s ASI, as he describes it — not a cliff, a blur

The takeawayRead this interview next to Wang’s superintelligence piece and the Anthropic IPO pricing in the same week, and the pile’s argument sharpens: the people closest to the frontier — including one in prison — now agree that intelligence is not the constraint and that the next five years are a gradual subsumption, not a single event. SBF’s trillion-dollar call for Anthropic is a reminder that the disagreement is not about the direction. It is about the price, and the price is still being argued in public.
The Agent Economy

“Every Step an Agent Takes Is a Search Problem”

Aravind Srinivas, chief executive of Perplexity, interviewed · Video, 33:27 · Transcribed in full · Via painy

Perplexity grew its headcount by ten to twenty per cent this year and its revenue by four to five times. That is the whole interview, in one sentence, and it is the number Aravind Srinivas keeps coming back to in a 33-minute conversation transcribed in full for this edition: “Our goal is to give that power to every other company on the planet, and that is Perplexity.” The product that did it, Perplexity Computer, launched in February, and the revenue is up 4.7 times year on year because of that one product. The company is past a billion dollars in annual recurring revenue, the interviewer notes, with roughly a fifth of Anthropic’s cash burn.

The argument for why is the cleanest statement of the application layer’s case in this edition. Towards the end of last year, Srinivas says, a lot of people thought Perplexity was dead: the world had moved on to coding agents, and search was old technology. What they missed is that every single step an agent takes is a search problem — which tool to pick from a repository of tools, which files to look at, which paragraphs in those files, which sources from the web, which connectors. A company that has spent four years building a search index of a trillion web pages — the best, Srinivas says, after Google — is exactly the company positioned to do that orchestration, and to do it without being married to any model. It could be Gemini, Claude Opus, GPT Astra or Sol, Grok, an open-weight model like DeepSeek or GLM: “Any time any part of the AI ecosystem improves, Perplexity improves.” The gross margin is 56%, there are no trillion-dollar compute commitments, and the company is not in the business of pre-training at all — which, Srinivas says, is precisely why it can be capital-efficient.

Perplexity’s year — headcount vs revenue

The most revealing detail is about the model, not the product. On the agent side, Opus 5.5 — Anthropic’s latest — now runs sixty to seventy per cent of Perplexity’s agent tokens, the highest share any one model has held there in the last year. What makes it the best, in Srinivas’s telling, is not the cost per token but trajectory efficiency: it makes the right tool calls, so it does not waste the user’s time, and the cost of the whole agent trace is what matters. That is the capability Perplexity’s new Frontier Research Lab is built to hill-climb on, with its own environments and evals and real reinforcement learning — post-training only, never pre-training. And the vanilla product, the fast answers, now goes mostly to a model Perplexity post-trained itself on DeepSeek and GLM, which Srinivas says saves about $100 million a year against paying a frontier model for quick answers.

Then the story every founder in the pile should read twice. When the team saw Opus 4.5 and the first real coding agents, twenty engineers went to work on what became Perplexity Computer, and the rest of the company froze the codebase and shipped nothing for three to four months. “A lot of people outside were wondering,” Srinivas says. “There were some well-meaning people who even texted me saying, hey, hope you’re all right.” They came back, shipped the computer, and “that changed our destiny forever”. The conviction was that models were going to be really good at code, and that code was going to be the way to do any kind of work — legal, insurance, accounting, not just software engineering — and that the job was to forecast where the frontier would be six months out and build products that are merely okay today and really good when that model lands.

He runs the company on the product, and the examples are the most concrete in the interview. Most of his questions now go to internal AI teammates: a bug he spots on X gets root-cause analysis from the tweet’s URL alone, and the teammate pages the right engineer. The speed at which bugs get fixed “has multiplied like ten to a hundred times”. He is honest about the limit: the human still has to look at the PR, because once they merged the wrong one and “it causes a lot of money”. The vision he is building towards is the one the whole edition keeps circling: point an AI at a retention metric and have it hill-climb on it for weeks, adjusting itself until it hits the target. Not yet, he says. Not yet.

Two more passages deserve filing. On Jensen Huang — asked before the reinstatement, when Huang was still out — Srinivas says the partnership was never a personal one: Huang made Perplexity work with the whole Nvidia team, Slack channels, engineers in each other’s meetings, local deployments of its harness on DGX and RTX, an open-sourced sandbox platform built with Nvidia’s feedback. “We earn our right to work with them by showing them some insightful results.” On Google, he is unsparing: AI Mode is zero-sum, search ads have taken a hit in the most recent earnings, and the innovator’s dilemma is real — “the best thing they could have done, honestly, is to just not ever roll it out”. The same will be true of agents, he says: if agents make purchases on the user’s behalf, how do you charge for humans clicking five links?

“Our job is to basically keep innovating, forecast where the frontier is going to be six months from now and build products that would actually be okay-ish today and would be really good when that model lands six months from now.”— Aravind Srinivas, Perplexity
The takeawayPerplexity’s year is the application layer’s best argument: you do not have to own the model to own the work. You have to own the index, the orchestration and the discipline to freeze everything for four months on a conviction about where the frontier is going. The number to watch is not the revenue multiple. It is the 60–70% — how much of the agent economy now runs on one model, and what happens to every orchestrator when that model changes.

Verification Engineering: The $400,000 Skill of Running 30 Fable 5.2 Agents

By Arcos · X Article, 18 November 2026 · Read in full · Quoted in the Srinivas post above

If Srinivas’s interview is about building the agent economy, Arcos’s long-form article — quoted in the same post, read here in full — is about what breaks inside it. The central claim: the scarce, well-paid skill is no longer writing code, but proving agent-written code works without reading it. Companies, he says, pay $400,000 or more a year for shipping agent code you never had to read. For months he ran three Claude Code agents, no more — not for token cost, but because he was the check: every “done” meant another diff to read, and three was as many as one human can babysit. Last month he ran thirty Fable 5.2 agents and read four reports, not four thousand diffs. Same model, same repo; what changed is who gets to say “done”.

The progression he sets out is the field’s in miniature: prompts, then agents, then harnesses, then loops, then graphs, then verification. Graphs let you run thirty agents at once; verification is why you stop reading them. Almost nobody builds this layer, he says; they add a second model that says the work looks good and call it a review. The evidence he cites is from Anthropic’s own Claude Code team, where Claude writes and reviews about 90% of the code, and where merged pull requests more than doubled from June to September while the share reverted fell by more than 40%. Addy Osmani’s reason, quoted in the article: when software is generated faster than it can be reviewed, the bottleneck moves to proving it works.

The one rule that makes a check impossible to cheat is the article’s core: a check the change can edit is a suggestion. On the Claude Code team, review rules live in a file called REVIEW.md, read from the base branch, never from the pull request — the change being judged cannot touch the rules that judge it. The setup Arcos gives, in full, is one skill, two hooks, one checklist and one evidence file: a verify skill that makes Claude start the app and drive it like a QA engineer before every commit; a stop hook that sends Claude back until .verify/evidence.md says PASS, three fails and then a human; a commit gate that starts in observe mode and logs what it would have blocked; and a stamp on every commit, Verified-by: e2e,unit,checks(4) — or, honestly, Verified-by: none, so unverified commits can be found later with one git command.

Two ideas in the article deserve to travel. First, the second reviewer is a group chat: same model, same blind spots, and the reviewer reads the author’s confident summary and nods; the Claude Code team tested it and found adding a second, more expensive reviewer is often worse than tuning the primary, which gets one REVIEW.md and is pointed at the failures from earlier rounds. A reviewer with no rubric gives vibes; a reviewer with a rubric gives a verdict; a command with an exit code gives a fact. Second, the only number that tells you it is working is not the test pass rate — “it is 100% on the day login breaks” — but reads per ship, the diffs you actually opened divided by the changes that shipped, with a goal under 0.1, and escapes, the bugs found after merge that a check should have caught. If the same class escapes twice, the ratchet is broken, not the agent. And the ratchet is the point: every miss becomes one line in the checklist, so the same bug cannot ship twice, and every check that has not fired in sixty days gets deleted.

Karpathy’s framing, cited at length, is the one to keep: coding agents basically did not work before last December, and basically work since — and the catch is they work best on tasks where you can verify and test what they did. His own example: a weekend project building a video-analysis dashboard for his home cameras, handed to an agent that logged into his DGX Spark, set up ssh keys and vLLM, benched Qwen3-VL, built the UI, tested everything, set it up with systemd, and came back with a report in thirty minutes. “Programming is becoming unrecognisable,” he says — “you are not typing code into an editor… you are spinning up AI agents, giving them tasks in English and managing and reviewing their work in parallel.” Arcos’s closing line is the coda: a senior engineer was never the one who wrote the most code; they were the one who knew how to tell if it worked.

The takeawayRead the two stories together and the edition’s argument is complete. Srinivas shows the agent economy scaling — revenue up five times on a fifth of the headcount. Arcos shows the cost of that scale: a human cannot read thirty agents’ diffs, so the definition of done has to move out of the human’s head and into a file the change cannot touch. The $400,000 skill is not prompting. It is knowing how to tell if it worked.
The Shipping Desk

Claude Ship: the Merge Button Now Belongs to the Model

By Boris Cherny · via ClaudeDevs · 23 November 2026 · Verified against TechCrunch and Anthropic’s announcement

A year ago, Boris Cherny wrote, Anthropic launched Opus 4.5, “the first model to write 100% of my code”. Shipping pull requests never caught up with writing them, so on Monday Anthropic launched Claude Ship in beta for Team and Enterprise plans: Claude reviews your pull requests, works on the fixes, and approves the ones your team’s policy allows. “I use it all day every day,” Cherny writes, “and can’t imagine shipping without it.” The shift is not capability but authority. Code Review, already generally available, finds the problems. Two new agents in beta do the rest: PR Steward fixes failing checks, merge conflicts and review feedback, commenting after every push on what it changed and how to roll it back; Claude Approvals applies a natural-language approval policy the team writes as a file in the repo, and approves routine changes on its own. Anything riskier goes to a human. A new /ship command in Claude Code pushes, opens the PR and triggers the whole loop from one command.

Anthropic’s own advice is the most honest part of the launch: run approvals in observe-only mode first, on one repository, and see what the model would have approved before letting it approve anything. The company reports its own Claude Code team more than doubled merged PRs from June to September, with PRs passing after one review-and-fix round rising from 65% to 78%. The guardrails matter as much as the headline: usage is billed on tokens, with a monthly cap; Zero Data Retention and HIPAA-configured organisations cannot use it at all. Read next to Arcos’s verification-engineering piece in this edition, Claude Ship is the vendor’s version of the same idea — the definition of done, written down, enforced by a machine — and the same warning applies: a policy file is only as honest as the team that writes it.

The takeawayFor a builder, the question is no longer whether the model can write the code — that was last year’s question. It is who holds the merge decision, and under what written policy. Claude Ship’s answer is: the model, under your policy, in observe mode first. That is a reasonable answer. It is also one that deserves a week of watching before a single approval goes live.

GPT-6.2 Sol Comes to the Chat Tab

Via Tibo, quoting ChatGPT · 23 November 2026 · Verified against OpenAI’s release notes

A week after GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol launched for Work and Codex, OpenAI has rolled a chat-tuned version of Sol into the Chat tab itself — the place most people actually talk to ChatGPT every day. The takeawayThe model that most people meet is now, quietly, a different model. Check the picker; if GPT-6.2 has arrived, the biggest visible change will be on uploaded images and on long, detailed instructions — the two places the old model most often fumbled.

Roze: Your Agent Should Prompt You

By Eric Appel, via Josh Elman · 23 November 2026 · Verified against launch coverage

Eric Appel — “one of the best iOS engineers I have ever worked with”, in Josh Elman’s words, from their Robinhood days — launched Roze on Monday, a personal agent “designed to be proactive from the ground up”. The pitch inverts the last three years of assistants: “you shouldn’t have to prompt your agent, your agent should prompt you.” Roze connects to email and calendar, works out what needs doing, and comes back with suggestions you accept with one tap. It is in private beta on iOS. The idea is not new — OpenAI’s Dots, Anthropic’s Claude Duet and Meta’s Muse are all chasing the same shape — but Roze is the purest version of it in the pile: an app whose entire premise is that the prompt is the bug. The question it will have to answer, like all of them, is the one LangChain’s engineers put best this month: a proactive agent still has to decide when to speak, and a wrong interruption costs more than a missed one.

The takeawayWatch the notification, not the chat box. The next interface war is over who gets to interrupt you — and the winner will be whoever interrupts least, and most usefully.

By Trapit Bansal · 24 November 2026

Trapit Bansal left Meta last week, after sixteen months building the TBD lab from scratch — from Mark Zuckerberg’s initial pitch in June last year to the release of the Muse models. His leaving note is the most quietly important post in the pile, because it is a diagnosis from inside the frontier. “It surprises me how much the frontier labs now agree on the path forward,” he writes. “Most of the effort goes into scaling the ideas we already have, and very little into new research bets, even though there is still so much we don’t understand about intelligence.” When he started the reasoning effort at OpenAI, it was a new research bet; now it feels obvious. “I wonder what else will feel that obvious a few years from now. I’m starting from scratch again to find out.” Read next to SBF’s interview in this edition — ASI as a gradual subsumption, not a breakthrough — Bansal’s note is the same observation from the builder’s side: the consensus is scaling, and consensus is exactly what a research bet is not.

The takeawayIf every lab agrees on the path, the path is priced in. The alpha, Bansal is saying, is in the bets nobody agrees on yet — and he has gone to go find one.
The Deal Desk · India

CrowdStrike Buys Zscaler for $43.9 Billion — Jay Chaudhry’s Fifth Company

By Madhav Chanchani, quoting CNBC · 23 November 2026 · Verified against Reuters, CNBC-TV18 and CRN

CrowdStrike announced on Monday that it will acquire Zscaler in an all-stock deal valued at about $43.9 billion — the largest pure-play cybersecurity acquisition on record, passing Google’s $32 billion purchase of Wiz. Zscaler shareholders receive 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each share they own, valuing Zscaler at $260.41 a share, a 34% premium to its last close. George Kurtz will lead the combined company; Jay Chaudhry, who founded Zscaler in 2007, will keep running the Zscaler business and join CrowdStrike’s board. The deal is expected to close in the second half of CrowdStrike’s fiscal 2028 — between August 2027 and January 2028 — subject to shareholder and regulatory approval.

The Indian thread is the one Madhav’s post pulls. Chaudhry, an IIT (BHU) Varanasi graduate, has now founded and sold five companies in the United States in thirty years — SecureIT, CoreHarbor, CipherTrust, AirDefense and Zscaler — and he and his family own about a third of Zscaler, worth close to $15 billion in the combined entity. The combined company starts with more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue and targets $1 billion of annualised revenue synergies. Kurtz’s framing is the AI-era one: “Security used to be about keeping the adversary out. Today, safety is required to let AI in.” Chaudhry’s is blunter: “In the AI era, Zero Trust security has become non-negotiable, and AI agents are rapidly becoming the new weakest link.” The deal pits the combined company directly against Palo Alto Networks, run by Nikesh Arora — an Indian-origin founder on one side of the largest cyber deal ever, an Indian-origin chief executive on the other.

The takeawayFive companies, thirty years, one playbook: sell the infrastructure that lets the new thing in safely. Chaudhry’s fifth exit is also the biggest — and it is, fittingly, a bet that the agent era needs a new kind of gatekeeper.
The India Desk

“No VC for Anyone Who Is Not in SF”

By Manas Saloi (mjs) · 23 November 2026 · Three chat screenshots, transcribed in full

Manas Saloi posted three screenshots of WhatsApp chats with founder friends in India, with the names redacted and a note that he will delete the post soon — “it is not as funny with all the names redacted”. It is, even redacted, the frankest conversation about venture capital in this edition, and it is worth reading whole, because the argument it makes is the one Indian founders have been making to each other for a year: the Valley’s software VCs are in an existential crisis, and India is where they have come to work it out.

The thread starts with a question — “bc what are these VCs doing in SF man… why would anyone take their money in the valley… isn’t it adverse selection” — and the answer, from the other side of the chat, is that it is the reverse: the VCs are the ones with the problem. “Its existential crisis time for all software vcs bro… in india,” one founder writes, and another adds the mechanism: the big firms have understood there is no point betting on companies in India, so they go there to figure out how to bet there — overpaying and squeezing into hot companies to build reputation, then praying for deal flow, “basically like any new VC starting out”. The punchline, delivered deadpan: “you need to be sarah guo with friends in high places like palo alto and nvidia to get founders an outcome”, and, of one unnamed investor’s India plans, “wtf is he going to do? take them to [redacted] so that they can learn how to chew pan? even for that rajeev mishra is there”.

The corollary, in the third screenshot, is the line this paper is filing away: “the corollary of this is no vc for anyone who is not in SF — if you are building AI/Software whatver you want to call it”. And: “Only thing that will get money in india is consumer stuff” — followed, in the same breath, by the joke that lands the whole thing: “do you want to start a Beautiful Indian Ladies skincare brand with me?”

“Only thing that will get money in india is consumer stuff.”— From the chats, via mjs

Strip out the jokes and the claim is serious: in AI and software, the capital, the distribution and the outcomes are concentrating in San Francisco, and an Indian founder building software is competing for money that increasingly only flows to companies already inside the Valley’s orbit. The consumer exception is the tell — India’s consumer market is big enough to fund on its own merits, while its software market is not, yet. It is the same concentration story as the Jensen lead, told from the other end of the telescope: the value is pooling where the judgement already is, and everyone else is being asked to come to it.

The takeawayFor a founder in India building AI or software, the chats’ advice is blunt: either get inside the Valley’s network or build for the consumer market that funds itself. Everything in between is, in their words, praying for deal flow.

Latent Space: How Linear Got to $100 Million in ARR — and Let Agents Do Half the Work

By Latent Space, with Cristina Cordova, COO of Linear · 19 November 2026 · Read in full

Linear was founded to build a better issue tracker. Seven years and $134 million in funding later, its chief executive published a post titled “Issue tracking is dead” — and the company has spent the eighteen months since rebuilding itself around the claim. In this episode, chief operating officer Cristina Cordova — seven and a half years at Stripe, platform and partnerships at Notion, a partner at First Round before joining Linear in 2023 — explains how the company navigated that, and how its go-to-market changed along the way. The numbers first: Linear passed $100 million in ARR this year, with more than 40,000 paying customers and 177% net revenue retention; in August, employees sold $99 million of stock at a $2.5 billion valuation.

The headline number is the one that belongs in this edition’s agent story: agents created 3% of Linear’s work a year ago. Now it is more than half. Linear shipped its MCP server in May 2025 — Cordova says it was basically the first in its category — and a year and a half later, more than half the issues created in Linear come from agents. That does not mean Linear wants to be a database with an MCP server on top; Cordova thinks SaaS companies settling for that are making a big mistake. If the agent workflows inside your product are not better than the ones outside it, nobody has a reason to open your app. So she watches two lines: agent usage and app usage. If agent usage climbs and app usage does not, you are becoming a commodity. At Linear, both are up and to the right, because people still come back to the app to see and coordinate the work.

The go-to-market story is a case study in sequencing. Three weeks after joining in 2023, Cordova crossed “move upmarket to enterprise” off the company goals in front of the whole company — 35 to 40 people, one salesperson. You do not go upmarket with one person. She went after growth-stage companies that would have to rip out an existing tool instead, the way Stripe won new businesses long before it won switchers, and only in 2025 did she stand up and say the company was going after enterprise. The sales team she built has never had a single SDR — she thinks the qualification call wastes the customer’s time; account executives do their own outbound — and no forward deployed engineers, because Linear should be easy enough to adopt that nobody needs access to your systems. Support stays in-house and is never outsourced, because for a long time the first human touchpoint a customer had was support, and support had better be good. AI drafts replies; a human decides what goes out. In three and a half years, she says, there has never been an escalation over a bad support experience.

Two more details deserve filing. First, the AI budget: companies carved out money for AI tools that did not exist before, and any product that fits inside it skips the usual fight for spend — but buyers are signing one-year deals and paid pilots, not three-year contracts, because nobody knows what they will want in two years, and some overnight-$100-million stories are a handful of $30–40 million defence or law-firm contracts. When those one-year deals come up for renewal, Cordova says, “we’ll find out whose ARR was real.” Second, pricing: Linear charges for what costs it money and keeps the rest free. Linear Agent chat is free even though it runs on frontier models; coding sessions pass through the published API price plus a small markup. “Linear doesn’t start charging for things that used to be free,” she says — and, from Stripe, she brought a 40% rule: at least 40% of the company should be engineering, product, design and data.

The takeawayLinear’s lesson is the same as the verification piece’s, from the commercial side: the agent era does not kill the product, it re-prices it. The companies that survive are the ones whose app is still worth opening after the agents arrive — and whose ARR is still there when the one-year AI budgets come up for renewal.

Simon Willison Gave Claude Code Ears — and Found the Beep

By Simon Willison · 9 November 2026 · Read in full

Something in Simon Willison’s box room had started beeping intermittently, far apart enough that standing in the room and listening was useless, and he did not want to dig through every box. So he had Claude Code build Beep Locator: a system for triangulating the source of a mysterious beep using several devices scattered around the room as microphones. He ran a Python web server for the main UI and joined two iPads, a phone, a MacBook Pro and a Surface Ultra as listeners over WebAudio and WebSockets. The devices play noises to each other to estimate their locations — that worked poorly, so he added a “rough mode” where he drags their icons to approximate positions — and then they all listen. A clapping mode let him test the system by clapping in different places. Beeps show up on the map as crosses; each can be replayed from every listener in turn, and confirmed as the mystery beep. The system worked: it placed the beep to within about forty centimetres, and in that pile of boxes was an ancient carbon monoxide detector that desperately wanted its batteries replaced.

The build is the interesting part, and it is the most useful account of agentic engineering in this edition. Willison started the project on his iPhone, using Claude Code Cloud with Opus 5.5 in extra-thinking mode, against a newly created empty repository. Claude found and read a 2007 paper, BeepBeep, on acoustic ranging with commodity devices, and wrote it up. Then Willison told it to build the system with a mechanism for sending new code to the listeners that they would run automatically — so he could set the devices up and leave Claude alone to keep hacking while he did something else. When Fable 5.2 was released mid-project, he switched to it as a test. The key mechanism is an installModule() method on the listeners, triggerable over the WebSocket, which means the admin server can execute arbitrary new code on its clients — and, crucially, deploy new code without touching each device, since web pages listening on the microphone ask for permission again on every reload. From then on, his involvement was mostly sitting by the laptop, chatting with Fable 5.2, and requesting features in plain language: drag things in rough mode, listen for claps, timestamp every symbol, add a play button, add a “reposition the devices” button, suggest where an extra listener would help most.

Willison’s own summary of what he demonstrated is the line to keep: it is possible to give Claude Code ears. Claude has no native audio input, but it can run JavaScript and Python that analyse audio captured by a microphone — so he left it alone, querying audio logs and reconfiguring its ears, while it solved the problem. He was reminded of a hack from GitHub Universe, where Martin Woodward debugged badge computers by plugging them in over USB-C and pointing a webcam at their screens, so his coding agent could see whether updates were working. “Figuring out ways to let coding agents perceive the world around them,” Willison writes, “is a whole lot of fun.” It is also, read next to the verification piece, the other half of the agent story: the agents are getting senses, and the question is no longer whether they can act, but whether we can check what they did.

The takeawayThe cheapest way to understand what agentic engineering can actually do is to give an agent a stupid, concrete, physical problem — a beep in a room full of boxes — and watch it invent the sensors it needs. The pattern generalises: the agent’s job is not to answer, it is to perceive, and the human’s job is to decide what counts as found.
