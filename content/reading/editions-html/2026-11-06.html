The 80% Barrier Falls: Claude Opus 4.5 Arrives

Anthropic’s launch announcement, read in full

Anthropic’s new flagship, Claude Opus 4.5, is the first model ever to break 80% on SWE-bench Verified, the benchmark that most closely tracks real software-engineering work. It scores 80.9% there, and 66.3% on OSWorld, the computer-use benchmark — a correction worth making, because the launch-day chatter claimed it had cleared 80% on both. Only the coding score is above the line. Even so, the headline stands: the frontier model has crossed a threshold that, until this week, no model had reached.

The pricing is the quieter revolution. Opus 4.5 costs $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens — roughly a third of what the earlier Opus line charged. Anthropic has also removed Opus-specific usage caps, and raised Max and Team limits so users get roughly the same Opus token volume they previously had with Sonnet. The most expensive model in the lineup just became dramatically cheaper to run, which is exactly the kind of move that changes who can afford to build serious agents on it.

The feature that matters most for builders is the new effort parameter on the API: a dial that trades latency and cost against capability. At medium effort, Opus 4.5 matches Sonnet 4.5’s best SWE-bench score while using 76% fewer output tokens; at maximum effort it beats Sonnet 4.5 by 4.3 points while still using 48% fewer tokens. That is the shape of the next phase of the model race — not just a bigger number, but a cost curve you can steer. Around it sit the product changes that make long-running agents practical: context compaction and memory for extended sessions, better management of teams of subagents, and what Anthropic calls its most robustly aligned model yet, with substantially better prompt-injection resistance.

The surrounding product updates tell the same story of agents becoming the default way to use Claude. Claude Code gets a Plan Mode that asks clarifying questions and builds a user-editable plan before writing code; Claude Code now runs inside the desktop app with parallel local and remote sessions; “endless chat” auto-summarises older context instead of cutting the conversation off; Claude for Chrome is now available to all Max users; and the Claude for Excel beta has expanded to all Max, Team and Enterprise users. Opus 4.5 is available across the Claude apps, the API, Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud Vertex AI and Microsoft Foundry.

“The best model in the world for coding, agents, and computer use.”— Anthropic, on Claude Opus 4.5

There is a small caution in the announcement worth pocketing. Anthropic cites a benchmark scenario in which Opus 4.5 found a legitimate workaround — upgrading a cabin before modifying flights — that the test scored as a failure, and presents it as creative problem-solving. It is a reminder that as models get better at finding their own way around a task, the tests themselves start to misjudge them. The benchmarks are beginning to lag the models they measure, and the 80.9% is, in that light, a floor rather than a ceiling.

The takeawayIf you run agents in production, the effort dial is the feature to test first: the same model at medium effort may now do Sonnet-grade work for a quarter of the tokens. And quote the numbers carefully — 80.9% on SWE-bench Verified is real and historic; the “above 80% on OSWorld” version circulating on launch day is not. Critical Thinking When Agents Do the Work

Addy Osmani’s X article, read in full

Addy Osmani’s argument starts with a shift in where the bottleneck sits. It used to be producing the answer — writing the code, doing the research, drafting the document. Agents now produce those things at a scale and speed nobody imagined a few years ago. So the question changes: what is left for the human? His answer is evaluation — critically assessing what the agent produced, figuring out what is right, what is missing, what is wrong, and how to make it better. If you cannot evaluate well, you will accept garbage, get led astray by hallucinations, and trust things you should not.

The reason this is hard is that creation and evaluation are different skills, running on different machinery. Creation uses fast, intuitive, pattern-matching thinking; evaluation needs slow, deliberate, analytical thinking. Most of us were never trained for the second one. Osmani spent six weeks talking to engineers, researchers, product managers, designers and writers about working with agents, and the pattern surprised him: the people who are best at it are not the ones who write the best prompts. They are the most sceptical ones, the ones who ask the most questions, the ones who do not just accept the first answer. The agent will always produce something. The question is whether it is any good.

“The skill that matters most is not prompt engineering, it’s evaluation.”— Addy Osmani

His mental model for the shift is a single sentence: in the old world, critical thinking was about asking good questions; in the new world, it is about asking good questions of the answers. He illustrates it with a small study of his own: five models asked to write a function that checks whether a number is prime. All five produced correct code. The best answer was the one that was most readable, most efficient and most well-documented — the difference was not in the code but in how it was presented. If you cannot tell a good answer from a bad one, you will accept the bad one, and that is how a codebase fills with garbage, a document with hallucinations, a product with bugs.

Getting better at evaluation is, he insists, a learnable skill, and it only becomes more important as agents get better. It takes practice, and deliberate questions: what would a good answer look like, what are the criteria, what are the trade-offs — and the willingness to say, this is not good enough. Domain depth is the prerequisite: you need to know what good looks like, and you need to have taste. Prompting is the skill everyone is teaching; judgement is the one that decides whether the work holds up.

The takeawayWhen you review agent output this week, score it against a written-down picture of what good looks like before you read it — correctness, completeness, whether it actually answers what you asked. The prompt is the easy part now. Source: Addy Osmani, “Critical thinking when agents do the work”.

The Detector That Can Spot AI Photos — Even the Edited Bits

Deedy (@deedydas) on Pangram Image 1.0, post read in full

Pangram Image 1.0 is not another image generator. It is the opposite: a detector, built to tell real photos from AI-generated ones, and it arrives at the moment that distinction is becoming hardest to make. Deedy’s post, which flagged the launch, puts the stakes plainly — AI-generated photos and videos are already used to scam people online, for shopping, apartments, dating apps, blackmail and wire fraud. Preventing that, he argues, is one of the biggest emergent problems of AI, and it is technically very challenging.

The numbers Pangram claims are striking. Image 1.0 identifies 99.2% of AI-generated images while holding a 0.27% false-positive rate; on a company-built test set of 100 real photos mirrored synthetically across 12 generators, it scored 99.96% accuracy. A lighter sibling, Pangram Image Flash 1.0, works frame-by-frame on video and scores 98.57% on AI videos against 84.2% for the next-best competitor. These are the company’s own figures, not independently verified — but the design choices behind them are what make the product interesting rather than just a scoreboard entry.

Detection has to survive the ways reality degrades an image. Malicious actors blur, add noise and artefacts to slip past detectors; social apps downscale everything; and phones now do AI-based post-processing of their own, so the line between “edited by a phone” and “generated by a model” is genuinely blurry — Pangram’s CEO says the launch was delayed while the model learned to tell iPhone post-processing apart from fully generated visuals. The detector analyses pixels rather than relying on watermarks or metadata, and it returns a heatmap pinpointing which parts of an image are AI-generated, so it can catch a pointed AI edit inside an otherwise real photo. Minimum input has dropped to 256 by 256 pixels.

Deedy’s framing is the part worth sitting with: frontier-model safety gets the attention, but the safety problems AI already poses to the world — scams running on convincing fakes — are the neglected half. And this is not a “talk to our sales team” launch: the API and a Chrome extension that flags AI images while browsing are live today, with free accounts able to check a handful of images. Detection is an arms race, and a 99.2% claim will be tested in the wild within days. But the direction of travel is clear: as generation gets cheaper, verification becomes the product.

The takeawayBefore you trust a photo in a listing, a profile or a pitch deck, run it through a detector — and remember the hard case is not a fully fake image but a real one with one AI-edited region. Source: Deedy on Pangram Image 1.0.

OpenClaw Saw the Future Too Early

Arnav Gupta (@championswimmer), post read in full

OpenClaw was, in Arnav Gupta’s telling, an important part of internet history — a project whose founder saw the future and tried to build it before the models were ready. It was too technically involved for ordinary users, and for technical users the base models themselves evolved fast enough that OpenClaw often felt dumbed down, and not secure enough. The category it pointed at is now real, but the winners are the packaged consumer products: Muse, Bot and Dot, as he names them, with Muse the fully legitimate one. For the category to truly reach the masses, he argues, Dot has to be retired and the ChatGPT main app has to grow into that functionality; for Meta, the Meta AI app should retire instead, because Muse is already its main AI app.

The counterpoint came in the quote he attached, from Amir: the packaged bots may not have strong product-market fit outside the tech and developer world at all, and their fate may be similar to OpenClaw’s. That is the tension worth holding. OpenClaw proved that people want an agent that lives on their machine, wakes itself up, and acts on their behalf — its heartbeat feature, clearing a 4,000-email backlog overnight or negotiating a car price over days, was the glimpse of the future. What it could not prove was that ordinary people would maintain one. The packaged successors solve the maintenance problem and inherit the distribution problem: the agent has to live where people already are, inside the app they already open.

The takeawayWatch where the agent lives, not what it can do. OpenClaw’s lesson is that capability without packaging stays a hobbyist tool — and packaging without distribution stays a demo. Source: Arnav Gupta on OpenClaw.

Attacks Are Not Getting More Sophisticated. They Are Getting Faster.

Nikesh Arora, CEO of Palo Alto Networks, post read in full

Nikesh Arora’s warning is the other half of this week’s security story, and it pairs directly with the essay in today’s Substacks. Six months ago he said frontier-grade hacking capability would become widely available. It has happened: a critical vulnerability, an innocuous-looking social-engineering attack, or several publicly known vulnerabilities daisy-chained into a sniper strike are all being seen in the wild, and incident responders have never been busier. His key point is that the attacks themselves are not becoming more sophisticated. The same attacks are now faster, and carried out by far more people.

Three mechanisms drive it. Attack-path discovery, which used to take trial and error across multiple attempts, is now trivial, with open-source models helping. Supply chains are the soft target: an insecure player upstream or downstream with access to your infrastructure is the next attack path, and marketplaces make that vector easier. And the increase in activity comes from more people becoming better at hacking because attacking is now easy — a factor Arora admits he had not put in his calculus. His advice is unglamorous and specific: test your infrastructure for accessible vulnerabilities and misconfigurations; fix your code and demand your vendors do the same; update old or insecure infrastructure at the perimeter first; and put a security framework around the new AI coding tools, because developers will do more with code, new skills and more automation — and the coding models trained on human code.

The takeawayThe threat did not get smarter; it got cheaper and more numerous. The fix is not a new tool but the boring work: patch the perimeter, audit the vendors, govern the coding agents. Source: Nikesh Arora.

Blinkit’s Ambulances, and Deepinder Goyal’s Defence of Temple

Deepinder Goyal, two posts read in full

Two posts from Deepinder Goyal this week show the two sides of a founder operating in public: a service built on an idea of duty, and a product defended against an angry professional establishment. The first began with a customer’s story — Sparsh Sehgal wrote that a Blinkit ambulance had saved his grandmother’s life, reaching home in eight minutes. Goyal’s reply, opening “I started Zomato with a spirit of service,” lays out the numbers: Blinkit Ambulance launched in January 2025 in Gurugram with five vehicles, and has since served around 20,000 emergency cases, about 42,000 cases in total, without charging a single rupee.

The new policy is the interesting part. Emergency transfers stay free; non-emergency transfers — routine check-ups, hospital visits, hospital-to-home moves — now cost ₹2,500, charged after the trip, broadly in line with what private ambulances charge. The money goes to the Eternal Foundation, which runs the ambulances. The reason is a rationing problem: non-emergency use was crowding out emergency availability, and a ten-minute ambulance only saves lives if it is available when an emergency happens. The bottleneck, Goyal says, is trained, empathic paramedics, and the training academy is scaling as fast as it can without compromising quality. The team aims to bring Blinkit Ambulance to all major Indian cities in the next two to four years.

The second post is a defence of Temple, his ₹85,000 wearable, against doctors who have called it a scam. Goyal’s letter makes seven points, and they are worth reading as a case study in how a founder answers a credibility attack. If Temple were a scam, he argues, users would know within days, and the 14-day no-questions-asked return policy would kill the company before it recovered a tenth of its research spending. Employees bought into the last round; the round being raised now is mostly customers. And the most groundbreaking features, he adds, are not even public yet.

Read together, the two posts are a study in how trust is built and defended: one by giving a life-saving service away for free and charging only where use is discretionary, the other by putting personal money, reputation and a returns policy behind a contested scientific claim. Whether Temple’s science holds up will be settled by the independent studies Goyal promises; the ambulance numbers are already settled, and they are the stronger argument.

The takeawayFree for emergencies, ₹2,500 for the routine: the Blinkit pricing change is a textbook rationing decision, and it is the model to copy when a free service gets crowded out by non-urgent use. On Temple, judge the science when the independent studies arrive — the founder has now staked his own money and name on them coming. Sources: Goyal on Blinkit Ambulance and Goyal’s letter on Temple.

SpaceX Is Not a Rocket Company

Shaun Maguire’s X article, read in full

Shaun Maguire’s argument is disarmingly simple: SpaceX is not a rocket company. It is the only company in history to mass-produce rockets, but it is a power and computing company that happens to use rockets to produce that power and compute. Starlink alone runs at about $20 billion a year in revenue — bigger than most people think, and the strongest signal of what the company really is. If SpaceX were just a launch company, it would launch satellites as a service for other companies. Instead it uses its own rockets to launch its own satellites to sell its own power and compute. That vertical integration is the whole thesis.

Most people have Starlink wrong, Maguire says: they think it is a satellite internet company, but the internet is just the first product. The real product is power and compute in space. Starship is what changes the scale: a single flight can put more than 100 tons into orbit, and at roughly $1 billion of annual revenue per launch, that works out to about $5 billion of lifetime revenue per Starship flight. The market for power and compute on Earth is about $1 trillion a year, and Maguire puts the market for power and compute in space at least that large — with SpaceX the only company able to serve it at scale. His forecasts are deliberately extravagant: a $1 trillion company by 2028, $10 trillion by 2035, $100 trillion by 2040.

The numbers deserve a sceptical read alongside the vision. Maguire built his model after SpaceX’s third-quarter earnings call, calls it his 25th-to-50th-percentile estimate, and flags himself as extremely biased, with Sequoia a major shareholder. Wall Street expects roughly $110 billion of full-year 2027 revenue against his model’s far higher figure, and analysts caution that deploying 10 gigawatts of compute in 2027 may be too aggressive. Musk’s own reply to Maguire was: “I think we can do better.” The forecast may be wrong in magnitude and still right in direction: the company has already folded xAI into a Superintelligence division whose third-quarter revenue rose 830% year over year, and it has applied to launch up to a million orbital data-centre satellites. The question is not whether the space-compute market exists, but how fast anyone can actually build it.

The takeawayReframe SpaceX as a vertically integrated power-and-compute company and the valuation argument changes shape entirely — but treat the trillion-dollar forecasts as a direction, not a schedule. Source: Shaun Maguire, “People don’t understand SpaceX”.

Karpathy Built a Better YouTube in Two Hours

Andrej Karpathy, post read in full; method confirmed from multiple write-ups

Andrej Karpathy was annoyed with YouTube’s recommendations, so on a Saturday morning he had Claude build him an alternative. Two hours with Opus 5.5, three things done by hand — a Google API key, a rented server, a bought domain — and HackerTube was live. The trick is not the interface. It is the ranking signal: instead of an engagement algorithm, HackerTube ranks videos by how often Hacker News commenters keep recommending them, over twenty years.

The method is a small masterclass in using a model as a research assistant rather than a code generator. Start from the open Hacker News archive: 43 million comments from 2007 to 2026. Keep comments that link a YouTube video and contain at least 100 characters of the commenter’s own text. A video qualifies only if at least three different people recommended it, in at least three different months. Claude then read about 39,834 comments and labelled each mention as recommending, citing, joking or criticising — only about four in ten mentions turned out to be real recommendations. The result: 6,454 videos across 150 topics, with the labelling run costing roughly 504 million input tokens, 90% cached, and 7.6 million output tokens.

What you get is a YouTube-like interface with no login, no algorithm and no watch history; likes and history live in your browser. Thumbnails are a frame from the video’s halfway point rather than the uploader’s clickbait image, and each card carries a short summary, why Hacker News likes it, and whether it has aged well. The most-recommended video in the whole canon is Ray Dalio’s How The Economic Machine Works. The full dataset is downloadable, so anyone can hand it to Claude and build their own version — one outlet rebuilt it in under two minutes for about sixty cents. The caveat is disclosed in the data docs: the labels are a few percent wrong, and about a quarter of the videos have no positive recommendation label among the mentions retained. That is the honest version of a weekend hack, and it is also the point: a durable recommendation signal was hiding in a public archive, and it took a model two hours to extract it.

The takeawayWhen a feed annoys you, ask what signal it should have used instead — then check whether that signal already exists in a public dataset. Source: Andrej Karpathy and HackerTube.

How to Make a Million a Year as a Digital Writer

Dan Koe’s letter, read in full

Dan Koe’s letter is a rare thing: a creator-economy success story told with the actual numbers attached. He started creative — design, photography, film — taught himself to code, and took a web-design agency job that taught him the missing pieces: marketing, sales, operations. He tried everything to land freelance clients, from cold email to copying phone numbers off pest-control vans, replaced his income within a year, and was making close to $100,000 a year mostly from referrals. But that was manual effort, not millions, and three realisations changed the model.

First, social media gives unlimited clients if you understand marketing — building an audience would save ten to twenty hours a week on lead generation. Second, he could grow on X: 10,000 followers in year one, 50,000 in year two, 100,000 in year three, around 2.8 million across platforms four years later. Third, everything pointed to writing: posts were also emails, newsletters, landing pages, scripts and course curricula. “I just considered myself a writer. That was all I did every single morning.” The money followed a rule of thumb he had read somewhere — fifty cents to a dollar per follower per month with your own product — and it held: about $1 per follower per month in the first three years, $800,000 in his third year, and around $3.1 million in the past year, more than his follower count in income.

From the Saves • How the Best Agent Is Built

Claude Code’s Obsession With Simplicity

The MinusX analysis of Claude Code’s design, read in full

The most-read analysis of why Claude Code works so well has a thesis that cuts against the whole multi-agent fashion: choose architectural simplicity at every juncture. One main loop, simple search, a simple to-do list. Resist the urge to over-engineer; build a good harness for the model and let it cook. The reasoning is about debuggability — language models are already hard to debug, and every extra layer of multi-agent systems, complex handoffs or elaborate retrieval makes debugging exponentially harder.

The numbers come from intercepted network traffic, and they are counterintuitive. Over half of the important model calls go to Claude 3.5 Haiku, the small cheap model — reading large files, parsing web pages, git history, summarising conversations, even generating one-word processing labels per keystroke. Small models are 70 to 80% cheaper, so they are used liberally. Edit is the most frequent tool, then Read and the to-do writer. The system prompt is about 2,800 tokens, tool descriptions 9,400, and the user’s claude.md context file ships with every request at 1,000 to 2,000 tokens — and the analysis says the difference with and without that file is night and day.

The design choices read like a checklist for anyone building agents. One main loop with a maximum of one branch — sub-agents can be spawned but cannot spawn further sub-agents. XML tags for structure, with good and bad examples for tool use. LLM-native search with ripgrep, jq and find instead of retrieval-augmented generation: no similarity-function confusion, no chunking or reranking decisions, and it naturally handles huge JSON and log files. A frequency-based tool hierarchy, so common operations get dedicated tools and edge cases get generic bash. The model manages its own to-do list. Tone is engineered explicitly — no unnecessary preamble, emojis only when requested — with heavy emphasis words where they matter. And the important tasks are written out as explicit algorithms and decision procedures, not rule soup. Anthropic’s own research is cited as the clincher: a multi-agent system beat single agents by 90.2%, but 80% of that gain came from simply using more tokens to think harder, not from architectural complexity. The magic is not in the complexity; it is in the careful simplicity that lets the model shine.

The takeawayBefore adding a second agent, a retrieval layer or a handoff, ask whether more thinking tokens and a simpler loop would do the job. Complexity is a debugging tax you pay forever. Source: MinusX, “What Makes Claude Code So Damn Good”.
From the Saves • India

India’s Rise Will Be Messy. But Inevitable.

Hemant Mohapatra’s essay, read in full

Hemant Mohapatra’s essay — shared by Dharmesh Shah, written by the Lightspeed partner and former AMD semiconductor engineer — is the best thing in today’s pile on how to read India. His thesis, in one line: India does things that are broadly right and specifically wrong. The country sets targets that are too big and too round; people game them; outcomes lag the numbers by years. And yet, while everything is more jagged than uniform, the country keeps getting richer, faster than almost anyone gives it credit for.

The mechanism is Goodhart’s law, told through two bounties. Delhi once paid for dead cobras, so people bred cobras; when the bounty was scrapped, there were more cobras than before. In 1902, Hanoi paid for rat tails, so people clipped tails and released the rats to breed more. The modern version is Semicon 2.0: on 31 August the IT minister announced a target of one lakh more semiconductor engineers, and the ministry counted 67,000 in February and 85,000 in August — around 18,000 chip engineers in six months, in a country that has yet to make a chip. Mohapatra, who spent six years at AMD, says it takes years plus an end-to-end chip design to be a real chip engineer, and guesses maybe a fifth of those 85,000 can actually close circuit timing on a real design. The world will be short about a million chip workers by 2032, and India should be in the game — but if you set a headcount, you get a headcount.

The scorecard is unsparing. Skill India has trained or “oriented” 16.4 million people since 2015, but independent reads of the ministry’s own dashboard put placement at 18%, 23% and 10% across the first three phases, and phase four stopped tracking placement. Ujjwala gave 103 million poor households an LPG connection against an 80 million target — but those homes bought only about 4.7 refills in FY26 against 6.5 for an ordinary household, and the government has now cut subsidised refills from nine to four a year. Jan Dhan opened 582 million bank accounts, about 26% of them dormant. The $23 billion production-linked incentive scheme reached 37% of its production target by late 2024 and paid out under 8% of the money, while manufacturing’s share of GDP fell during the scheme meant to take it to 25%.

And yet the essay refuses the cynical reading, because the same programmes keep producing second-order wins. The incentive scheme missed in most of its fourteen sectors and worked spectacularly in one. The 582 million Jan Dhan accounts became the rails for direct cash transfers and UPI. Ujjwala’s 4.7 refills are 4.7 more than zero, in homes that used to cook on wood and dung. The green shoots are real: smartphone exports from $3 billion in FY21 to $29 billion in FY26; extreme poverty at the World Bank’s $3-a-day line falling from 27.1% in 2011–12 to 5.3% in 2022–23, about 269 million people in eleven years. China is the proof that big countries build this way — Wuhan’s HSMC promised 7-nanometre chips on a $19 billion plan with a chairman who had no chip experience and made nothing, but China outspent the US roughly 3.6 times on chip subsidies over the decade and got SMIC, YMTC and CXMT.

“India’s real skill is moving the target once people learn to game it.”— Hemant Mohapatra

Democracy at India’s size runs on round numbers, Mohapatra argues: Delhi must push intent through 28 states, some 780 districts and officers rotating every couple of years, to a population roughly 240 times Singapore’s. The only thing that survives the trip intact is a number, so we get numbers, and then the numbers get gamed. The British answer to the cobra problem was to scrap the bounty; India’s answer, over and over, is to keep the bounty and change what it pays for. So he watches whether targets keep moving closer to outcomes — from engineers trained to wafers shipped, from connections to refills, from accounts opened to money actually moving. The close is personal: the kid who once waited a full day for a late train now takes on-time trains and flights for granted. India will stay messy for the next twenty years, missing in the details and hitting in aggregate, very substantially if not in full measure. Come home and build.

The takeawayRead India like a portfolio, not a report card: judge programmes by their second-order effects and by whether the target keeps moving toward the outcome. Source: Hemant Mohapatra.
From the Saves • Venture Capital

The Sequoia of Tomorrow

Sequoia Capital’s memo, read in full via TechCrunch

Sequoia Capital is deliberately rewinding about twenty years of expansion and returning to what it does best: high-conviction, highly selective venture capital investing. “The best founders reinvent themselves over and over again. We must do the same,” the memo opens, a year after a generational leadership transition. The objective function, it says, is not scale but quality — and the firm concedes it is scary to break something that is already working just fine, and painful to say goodbye to good people.

The changes are structural. The Arc batch accelerator, launched in March 2022 as a Y Combinator-style programme with $500,000-to-$1 million cheques, is being sunset. So is the Scouts programme, running since about 2009, which let founders and operators invest small cheques on Sequoia’s behalf — Jason Calacanis pointed Sequoia to Uber through it; Sam Altman brought Stripe. There is no more “Early & Growth” split: “There is no more ‘Early & Growth’. There is only Sequoia.” The London office, opened in 2020, is closing, with several people leaving; Europe will keep producing outlier founders, supported from the Bay Area. The partnership is being flattened after hierarchy crept in during the expansionary phase: seven general partners now own and operate the firm, led by co-stewards Alfred Lin and Pat Grady. The Monday partner meeting is dead — “Founders operate 24/7, and so do we.” And the firm has raised about $10 billion in new funds.

The context is an industry-wide retreat from the expansion era: a16z shuttered London last year, Coatue closed its office in 2024, and Sequoia separated its China and India and Southeast Asia arms in 2023. The memo is best read alongside the week’s other big story, the Pragmatic Engineer essay in today’s Substacks, about bottlenecks moving rather than disappearing. Sequoia spent two decades adding surface area — accelerators, scouts, offices, funds — and discovered the bottleneck was never deal flow. It was judgement. The rewind is an admission that the firm’s edge was always selectivity, and that scale was diluting it.

The takeawayWhen the bottleneck is judgement, adding surface area only dilutes it. The question for any scaling organisation — a fund, a startup, a team — is what to stop doing, not what to add. Source: TechCrunch on Sequoia’s memo.
From the Substacks

Descent Into the Computational Sublime

Contraptions • Venkatesh Rao, 6 November, read in full

October 2026 was, in Venkatesh Rao’s phrase, “Black October” — a vulnopocalypse in which AI-assisted vulnerability research exposed flaws across the foundations of the software stack faster than institutions could patch them. Rao argues this is the inciting incident of a three-act epic: the Dark Forest reaching apotheosis, then the Hardness Wars over the mathematical foundations of cryptography, then the Strange Forest — a divergent ecology of computational forms. And his twist on the sublime is the reversal that gives the essay its title: transcendence points downward, into deeper situatedness and particularity, not upward into abstraction.

The inciting incident has a number attached: the Binance heist of 29 October, roughly $6.34 billion in digital assets compromised. Attackers prepared for months through fake recruitment interviews, malicious downloads and compromised developer workstations, then exploited Ethereum’s EIP-7702 delegated execution so a malicious contract could act for a compromised wallet — permissions that persisted on-chain even after Binance halted withdrawals. Multiparty signing, normally a safeguard, complicated the emergency response, since there was no single key to seize back control. About $2.85 billion was frozen or intercepted by 3 November, and Binance committed to covering customer losses. Forensics found clear automation but no sign of autonomous AI decision-making — scripts stubbornly repeated failed operations. Rao’s point: imagine the same operation run by agents that are fast and adaptive.

Black October’s wider toll fills out the picture: AI-assisted vulnerability research on PHP, on the Brotli compression library, a Linux networking flaw, a critical UEFI firmware flaw sitting beneath operating systems and security tools, and Anthropic reporting a model building a working remote-root exploit in under an hour. The bottleneck has flipped: finding weaknesses now scales computationally faster than validating, disclosing, patching, testing and deploying fixes. That is the Dark Forest’s apotheosis — Yancey Strickler’s 2019 dark-forest social web and the 2020 Ethereum MEV dark forest, extended now to all of software. AI removes the old constraints: scarce skilled hackers, defenders’ superior skill, manual investigation. And Jevons paradox applies: cheaper, more secure software means vastly more software, more ambitious projects, more connections, and a threat surface that grows combinatorially.

Act 1 — Dark ForestFinding flaws scales faster than fixing them. Adaptation: “cozy code” at one extreme, fortified public infrastructure at the other.
→
Act 2 — Hardness WarsCivilisation depends on some problems staying hard. Quantum computing and AI-assisted maths attack the assumptions under post-quantum crypto.
→
Act 3 — Strange ForestWeight-space programming, latent-space recursion, composite human-model systems. Divergence, not convergence.

Rao coins Hacker Extractable Value — the dark forest’s analogue of MEV — for any exploitable weakness treated as extractable value. Its nasty property is that the victim’s loss can vastly exceed the attacker’s gain: a pickpocket steals roughly what the victim loses, but a hacker can impose a hundred dollars of damage while realising a few. The dark forest can be profoundly negative-sum, and state attackers may accept a net cost to impose asymmetric damage. The response is not eliminating that value but channelling adversarial discovery into repair: markets for security research, verification, remediation, insurance and recovery.

Act two, the Hardness Wars, turns on a strange fact about cryptography: it reverses the normal direction of progress, depending on certain problems staying hard. Rao walks through Russell Impagliazzo’s 1995 five worlds — Algorithmica, Heuristica, Pessiland, Minicrypt, Cryptomania — and notes we assume we live in Cryptomania without proof. Two pressures converge: quantum computing, with NIST finalising three post-quantum standards in 2024 and adding HQC in 2025, and AI-assisted mathematical research, which may discover better attacks on the lattice, code and hash assumptions behind post-quantum crypto. Josh Stark’s concept of blockchains’ “hardness” — the capacity to make the future dependable enough for reliable commitments — generalises: hardness is the raw material of civilisational trust, property rights, contracts, identity.

Act three, the Strange Forest, is where the essay connects to this week’s own coverage: weight-space programming, the MIT work from yesterday’s edition in which frontier models edit smaller networks’ weights directly; latent-space recursion, models reasoning in internal representations without translating every step into words, like whales breaching the surface to breathe occasionally; RLHF versus RLVR as different sources of structure, human judgement against independently checkable correctness from a curiously playable universe; and physical embodiment beyond the humanoid form. The payoff is a reversal of the science-fiction image: in Iain M. Banks’s Culture novels, Minds “Sublime” into a higher plane. Rao’s sublimation descends into greater situatedness, like the Ewoks on Endor defeating the Empire through local knowledge of the terrain. Before sublimation, chop tokens, carry context. After sublimation, chop tokens, carry context.

The takeawayThe essay to sit with this weekend. Its practical edge is the HEV framing: stop asking how to eliminate adversarial discovery and start asking how to price it, channel it and insure against it — because the finding side will keep getting cheaper. Source: Venkatesh Rao, Contraptions.

How Software Engineering Is Changing: A Tale of Two Eras

The Pragmatic Engineer • Neil Kakkar, 3 November, read in full

Neil Kakkar’s essay, the winning entry in The Pragmatic Engineer’s $10,000 essay challenge, is the most concrete, quantified account yet of what AI-native engineering actually feels like from the inside — and its headline number is startling. He now ships more than 200 pull requests a month, about eleven times his rate at PostHog, where he shipped 2.4 times his Bloomberg rate. But the essay’s real point is that the bottlenecks did not disappear. They moved: from writing code to context-switching, thrashing, lost shared context, and pacing. AI is a speed multiplier, not a sufficient condition; culture — whole-company AI adoption, experimentation, relaxing old constraints — is the necessary condition.

The three eras give the comparison its force. Bloomberg: 20,000 people, rigid process, three years. PostHog: about ten people when he joined, growing to 200, four years, the pre-AI baseline. Tano: first engineer, now about ten people, AI-native, eight months — in which he built an affiliate platform, a content-monitoring system and a migration of the whole agent architecture to persistent memory, comparable breadth to what took 45 months at PostHog. He now runs three to five things in parallel; agents take one to two hours to build a full feature end to end; he reviews through an HTML artefact Claude generates with every pull request; and he uses Claude Code Desktop, with its integrated browser he can hand over, rather than the command line.

The old constraints that vanished are the ones every engineering manager will recognise. Mandatory code reviews are now optional once onboarded — comfortable after three weeks, up to eight weeks for others — because a code-review agent runs on every pull request in CI, with a deeper on-demand agent for risky changes; in most cases the automated reviewer is the only review, and human reviews are nearly zero as a ratio. There are no product managers at Tano: he is his own PM, roadmapping with the chief executive and chief technology officer on Mondays, then picking separate things. The costs arrive with receipts. An agent parsed currency minor units as floats and multiplied by 100 — wrong for the Bahraini dinar, with 1,000 fulūs to the dinar — where a human reviewer would have known a currency utility already existed. A blocking Postgres migration on a 25-gigabyte, six-million-row posts table stopped the ingestion pipeline and broke deploys. A missed guardrail in a migration paid creators twice. The trade-off is explicit: fix forward fast rather than revert.

The killer comparison is migrations. PostHog’s person-on-events migration, moving person identifiers onto a 20-billion-plus event table to fix slow funnel joins, took a year, all hand-written. At Tano, two complex agent-architecture migrations took weeks each. What used to take a month now often takes a week, and architecture went from mostly irreversible to reversible — which changes what experiments you can run. The new constraints are human, not technical. Thrashing: he reacts to every incoming bug because each fix takes under an hour, and drifts from sprint goals. Lost shared context: code review used to be how he learned what everyone was doing, and that became impossible as pull requests per day grew from 15 to 30. Bus factor of one is now acute, and his proposed fix is writing context down in AGENTS.md files so anyone — plus AI — can pick up an area. Design is being discounted at early startups because AI produces good-enough neo-brutalist sameness. And pacing: front-loading mornings left him exhausted by 3pm, so he now paces, prioritises, and leaves his laptop at the office every evening — agents keep running locally and pull requests are ready by morning — as a deliberate guardrail against the feeling that he is not living up to AI’s potential while sleeping. His AI spend, at API rates, would be over $8,000 a month; hitting plan limits forces focus. Gergely Orosz’s coda adds the context: industry-wide pull-request output is up about two times since January, so Kakkar’s eleven times is part tool, part moving to a truly AI-native startup with nothing to lose by moving fast.

The takeawayIf only one person on a team has AI tools, gains cap at one to two times, because the review queue becomes the bottleneck. The lesson is organisational: lean in, find where the new constraints land, and relax them — and write the context down, because the bus factor of one is the new single point of failure. Source: Neil Kakkar, The Pragmatic Engineer.

Make Your Work Visible Without the Treadmill

Leverage Loops • Brandon Galang, 1 November, read in full

Brandon Galang’s essay is the lightest piece in today’s edition and the most immediately usable: a practical system for sharing your work consistently when you are trying to move into applied AI, without turning posting into a daily treadmill. The core move is decoupling writing from publishing with a queue, and treating your AI-agent sessions as raw material for posts instead of starting from a blank page. Visibility should be a by-product of the work, not a second job.

The system has four parts. First, the queue: he uses Typefully to keep posts queued ahead, spends a day getting ahead to build a buffer, writes in bursts when inspired, and reorders when something more relevant comes up. If every post must be written the day it publishes, one busy week kills the habit. Second, talk, don’t type: most of his writing now starts on walks, talking to an AI agent in live voice mode — brain-dumping an idea, reacting to a post he disagrees with, working through an argument out loud. He does not need the argument worked out first; the back-and-forth produces notes, angles and a rough draft to react against. Seeing the wrong version can make his point easier to explain. Third, voice discipline: he tells the agent to read his prior posts before drafting, so drafts are grounded in how he actually writes. He notes that GPT-6 Astra and Gemini 4 Argon are currently best for his writing — Astra for instruction-following and flatness, embellishing less — while Claude writes well but has a recognisable signature and sometimes shifts a sentence away from what he meant. Fourth, mine your agent sessions: if you are building with agents, your sessions already contain the questions you asked, approaches tried and problems worked through. His prompt: look through this session for three things I learned or decisions I made that would be useful to someone doing similar work, and show the source for each before drafting. A decision and its reasoning tells a hiring manager more than a screenshot of a finished project.

The guardrail matters as much as the system, especially when starting out: do not publish AI output as-is. Use it as notes you rewrite yourself. People who spend time with these tools recognise familiar AI phrasing, and generic posts make AI-native teams wonder how much of the thinking is yours. His test: did your point and examples survive, and could you explain it in conversation? Once drafts regularly sound like you, codify the corrections into a reusable agent skill. He still handwrites and polishes most of his writing; X posts get the most of that treatment because he wants them to feel raw and like him talking. Typefully’s analytics sit alongside the history, so he can see which posts resonated and explore those threads further.

The takeawayBuild the buffer this weekend: one day of writing, a queue of posts, and a habit of mining your agent sessions for decisions worth sharing — then rewrite everything in your own voice before it goes out. Source: Brandon Galang, Leverage Loops.
