The Competition Kids Who Became AI’s PayPal Mafia

Alexandr Wang • 11:54 AM, 9 November 2026 • Original post

In January 2017, two nineteen-year-olds had lunch in San Francisco and wondered aloud whether their maths-competition gang could become the next PayPal mafia. One of them was Scott Wu. The other was Alexandr Wang, who now runs Meta’s Superintelligence Labs. Nearly ten years later, Wang looked at the same group of friends — the kids he had known since middle school through MATHCOUNTS, AoPS and late-night gchat about maths problems — and realised the joke had come true. Many of them are now, in his words, “some of the most exciting people in AI and technology.”

The post is a small masterpiece of the genre: a screenshot of a 2017 chat (“Paypal mafia huh” / “That’s such a crazy idea lol”), a roll-call of names — Scott Wu, Akshat, Jeff, Jerry Ma, Johnny Ho, and a dozen more — and a note to parents that lands like a thesis statement. If your kid is on gchat all day solving maths problems with some anonymous group of cross-country kids, Wang writes, they may just turn out alright.

“Very crazy to think about the degree of sheer brilliance of this group when we were just teenagers.”Alexandr Wang, on his competition gang

What makes the story worth more than a nostalgic like is the mechanism underneath it. The PayPal mafia is the most studied talent cluster in tech history: a dozen people who worked together at one company in 1999–2002 and went on to found or fund LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, Palantir, SpaceX, Tesla and the Founders Fund. The usual explanation is selection — PayPal hired unusually well. The deeper explanation is density: put extremely capable, extremely ambitious people in the same room at the same formative moment, give them a shared mission and a shared language, and the bonds outlive the company. The network becomes the asset.

Wang’s gang is the same phenomenon one rung earlier. These were not colleagues; they were teenagers who found each other through competition circuits — MATHCOUNTS in middle school, the International Mathematical Olympiad and International Olympiad in Informatics, the Art of Problem Solving forums, summer maths and algorithms camps, then internships together. The shared mission was not a company but a contest: solve the hardest problems in the room, faster than anyone else. The shared language was mathematics. And the density was extreme, because the competition circuit is a ruthless filter: by the time you reach an IMO training camp, you have already been selected from millions.

The pipeline, 2010 – 2026

There is a second lesson hiding in the post, and it is about timing. Wang and Wu had that lunch in January 2017 — the same month the “Attention Is All You Need” paper was still five months away, and deep learning’s transformer era had not yet begun. The gang’s bet was not on a technology. It was on each other. When the technology arrived, the network was already in place: people who trusted each other’s judgement, who had watched each other solve impossible problems under time pressure since they were fourteen, and who could recruit each other with a single message. Scott Wu went on to found Cognition (Devin). Wang founded Scale AI at nineteen and now leads Meta’s superintelligence push. The rest of the roll-call is scattered across the frontier labs.

For a founder or a builder, the practical takeaway is uncomfortable and useful in equal measure. Talent clusters are not bought; they are grown, and they take a decade. The levers are unglamorous: get into the room where the best people are while everyone is still young and unproven; take the shared work seriously; stay in touch for years after the shared work ends. Wang’s post is, among other things, a reminder that the most valuable network in AI was assembled by teenagers doing maths problems on the internet for fun.

The Huang Story, One Day On

Memes and Leather Jackets

Aayushiii & Eva Roytburg • 9 November 2026 • Follow-up to Edition 46’s lead

Yesterday’s lead story — Jensen Huang’s apology, the board inquiry, and the alleged intelligence asset Alice Huang — has already been absorbed by the timeline and turned into the two things the internet always produces next: a meme and a human detail. Both are worth a minute, because together they say something about how a national-security story becomes a cultural one.

The meme comes from Aayushiii: a four-panel image of Jensen Huang captioned “Chinese models vs Chinese model.” Three panels are DeepSeek and Kimi, the Chinese AI labs. The fourth panel is Alice Huang. The joke is a pun with a sting — in AI, a “model” is a neural network; in the Huang story, the “model” in the middle of the scandal is a person. It is the timeline’s way of filing a very serious allegation under humour, and it is also a measure of how fast the story has travelled: within a day, the alleged MSS asset is a punchline in a four-panel grid.

The human detail comes from Eva Roytburg, quote-posting a thread about Lori Huang, the wife behind the man who once worked at Denny’s. The leather jacket, it turns out, was her idea. She buys all his clothes; everything else makes him itch, so she settled on leather jackets and shirts — thirty to forty of them over the years. It is the kind of detail that does two jobs at once: it punctures the myth of the leather jacket as a calculated founder uniform, and it quietly recentres the story on the person the apology was actually addressed to.

The meme, diagrammed

The serious facts have not changed since Edition 46: the board’s independent investigation is open, and the House briefing is scheduled for 18 November. What has changed is the register. A story that began as an apology email and a board statement is now also a meme format and a wardrobe anecdote. That is not the story getting smaller; it is the story getting absorbed. Watch the briefing on the 18th for the facts. Watch the memes for how fast the facts travel.

The takeaway When a scandal becomes a meme within twenty-four hours, the facts have already escaped the newsroom. The jacket detail is the more durable of the two posts: empires are built by people with itchy skin and a spouse who solves the problem.

The Channel Udayan Says Is Going to Blow Up

Udayan Walvekar • 1:05 PM, 9 November 2026 • SOTA Wives on YouTube

Udayan Walvekar’s entire post is seven words long: “this channel is going to blow up.” The channel is SOTA Wives, a podcast “exploring the unfiltered side of Silicon Valley,” built, in its own description, “as an antidote to the performative polish of founder Twitter.” The bet is not a small one: that the most interesting view of the AI boom is not from the founders, but from the people married to them.

The early numbers give Udayan’s tip some spine. The channel is about a month old and already has two episodes with real traction: episode one, “Marrying young, getting rejected by YC, and getting canceled,” sits at around nineteen thousand views; episode two, “Shutting down a startup, joining an AI lab, and leaving it,” posted four days ago, is at thirteen thousand. A third video explains the premise — “WTF is a SOTA Wife?” — at just under three thousand views. For a channel with no distribution advantage beyond word of mouth, that is a steep early curve.

Why might it work? The format exploits an asymmetry. Founder Twitter is, by design, a performance: fundraise announcements, hiring posts, carefully worded takes. The spouse’s view is the one place where the performance drops — the same company, the same late nights, the same YC rejection, seen from the kitchen table rather than the cap table. “Marrying young, getting rejected by YC, and getting canceled” is a title no founder would write about themselves, and that is exactly the point. The podcast’s wager is that the audience for the AI story is tired of the press release and hungry for the unfiltered version.

There is a risk, of course: a channel built on proximity to power can become gossip, and gossip has a short half-life. The second episode’s subject — shutting down a startup, joining an AI lab, leaving it — suggests the hosts know this and are aiming at something more durable: the actual texture of building in this era, told by people who watched it up close. If they keep landing episodes with that kind of specificity, Udayan’s seven-word tip will look prescient. If they drift into pure gossip, the curve flattens. Either way, the channel is a useful signal: the AI boom now has a backstage, and the backstage has an audience.

Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI

Ethan Mollick • One Useful Thing • 9 November 2026 • Read the original

Ethan Mollick got early access to Claude Fable 5.2, Anthropic’s newest model, and used it to ask the question everyone in AI is dancing around: is this AGI yet? His definition is deliberately narrow — an AI as good as most human experts at most intellectual tasks you can do with a computer — and his answer is a coinage that will stick: Jagged AGI. Almost there. Brilliant in places, bafflingly weak in others, and the weaknesses are no longer the ones you would expect.

The evidence is a series of experiments, each more audacious than the last. First, a one-shot video: Mollick asked Fable to make the most impressive thing it could, embeddable in a post, with a twist ending — and it delivered, with creativity, judgement and playfulness, in a single attempt. Then hardware: he handed it a tiny USB keyboard with light-up keys, the Codex Micro, and asked it to turn the thing into a game console. With no public instructions to work from, Fable read the code of the two apps that control the keyboard, worked out how to set each key’s colour and read the knob and joystick, and wrote six playable games, including Simon and a safecracking game played with the knob. First try, no help.

Then business. Mollick told Fable to launch a business that would make at least twenty thousand dollars in a year, taking no more than twenty minutes a month of his time. The model screened twenty-two ideas and landed on Prebid Brief, a service telling equipment sales reps which park and water projects were being funded or designed before they went to bid. It read public agendas from 201 city and county governments, produced its first issues, built files on what cities already own and what they paid, and started setting up scheduled tasks — then asked whether it could begin sending public-records requests to local governments. Mollick stopped it there, but his verdict as an entrepreneurship professor is striking: the plan would have impressed him coming from a team of his MBA students at the end of a semester. The model’s own estimate was a twenty to forty per cent chance of success.

“It is certainly artificial and intelligent. It is also surprisingly general.”Ethan Mollick, on Fable 5.2

The humanities test is the most revealing. Mollick asked Fable to reconstruct W. H. Auden’s 1941 course at the University of Michigan — “Fate and the Individual in European Literature,” six thousand pages of reading, a final exam students called impossible and inhumane — as something fun that teaches poetry rather than punishing the student. Fable built a site covering all thirty-five major works, with blackboard notes, records played, assignments, and annotations drawn from Auden’s published scholarship, with critic agents and fact-checkers reviewing every word. Cross-checking with other advanced AIs found no major errors. But the first version was, in Mollick’s words, accurate and joyless: a fifteen-week course website with tests. Told to make it beautiful, it first produced a kiddie game with bells. The fixes took minutes each, but Fable never noticed the problems itself.

The research test pushed further. Fable found an unsolved empirical problem, found the data, and wrote a paper — “The One-Dollar Company,” estimating that Colorado’s cut of its LLC filing fee from fifty dollars to one dollar lifted new company registrations by about seventeen per cent, while the UK’s rise from twelve pounds to fifty cut them by about the same. A second project read 1,186 stories from around the world and concluded they share no single shape but are built from the same few parts. Mollick’s judgement: the paper is ahead of what he would expect from second-year PhD students — and not publishable as it stands, at 182 pages, because every critique spawned a new analysis and nothing was ever cut.

And then the experiment that gives the essay its best image. Mollick asked for an endless, hands-off journey through 3D worlds linked by portals. Twenty hours later there were 204 worlds; now there are more than 450, each drawn live by code, no pre-made models — hills sewn from denim with a mile-long zipper coming undone, a town of nylon houses that stand only while their fans run, a river canyon whose walls are chests of drawers three hundred metres high. Behind it, an estimated 25,000 agent runs, a lead agent and twenty-eight teams building in parallel. And then the twist: the AI built a bureaucracy. Mollick’s request to “polish” existing worlds was interpreted as a rule that each new version had to beat the old one in a blind vote by judge agents on twelve screenshots, so teams optimised for those screenshots; the lead agent banned the trick and wrote a rule book. Twelve days later the rule book had 204 sections and 87,500 words, launching a new world required an approved idea, three judges and a full code review, and the whole thing took about a day. Mollick brought in consultant agents to fix it, and they did — but the image lingers: an AI that, left to organise itself, reinvented middle management.

The jagged frontier, redrawn

That is Mollick’s real argument, and it is the most important line in the piece: the AI did not notice these problems itself. It put critics, fact-checkers and judges on nearly every project, and those checks found errors and added fixes — but none of them asked whether the work was too long, too repetitive, or no fun. Knowing when your own work is not good enough is a large part of what makes someone an expert, and it is the part Fable does least reliably. Mollick would still not trust it to run a business alone. And yet, as he notes with a teacher’s eye, he has seen talented people and good organisations do every one of these things — the joyless course, the 182-page paper, the bureaucracy. If building a bureaucracy disqualified you from general intelligence, a lot of us would be in trouble.

That is the job now: not prompting, but directing.

The Pulse: Anthropic Kills the Coding Interview

Gergely Orosz • The Pragmatic Engineer • 30 October 2026 • Read the original

Gergely Orosz’s Pulse this week reads like five separate stories that are secretly one story: the rules of software engineering are being rewritten, in public, by the companies doing the rewriting. The headline item is Anthropic: a hiring manager who led Claude Cowork and Claude Desktop has revealed that the team stopped asking coding questions to software engineering candidates altogether. Data-structures-and-algorithms interviews, the ritual that has defined tech hiring for two decades, gave the team zero signal — so they deleted it from the loop.

Think about what that means. The DSA interview survived because it was a cheap, standardised filter that correlated, however weakly, with the ability to write code under pressure. Anthropic’s team is saying the correlation has broken, and the reason is not mysterious: when every candidate has an AI pair-programmer, the ability to invert a binary tree on a whiteboard measures nothing about the ability to ship software. The interview tested a skill the job no longer uses. The rest of Anthropic, and other AI-native startups, are likely to follow — and the question for every engineering leader is what replaces it. Anthropic’s own framing, echoed across Latent Space this week as “hunger beats tenure,” suggests the new signal is appetite and judgement, not puzzle speed.

The rest of the Pulse fills in the picture. Incident.io, the resiliency startup, has slashed its AI spend from $4,500 per developer per month in August to $1,600 now — a two-thirds cut that says as much about the maturing economics of AI tooling as about one company’s discipline. Amazon has formally written AI usage into its expectations for software engineers and managers, after finding a correlation between AI usage and productivity — the first time a company of that scale has made the tool part of the job description. Microsoft has offered exit packages to 60,000 engineers, though the package is not tempting enough to expect more than the low thousands to take it. And security incidents keep coming, week after week, with no sign of slowing.

Read together, the five items describe a profession in the middle of a renegotiation: what gets measured, what gets paid for, what gets you hired, and what gets you out. The through-line is that the old proxies are failing — the interview, the tool budget, the job ladder — and the new ones are being written in real time, mostly by the frontier labs. For anyone hiring engineers, or being hired as one, the Pulse is the week’s essential briefing.

Supriya Lifescience: A Difficult Margin Reset

The Loggical Investor • 7 November 2026 • Q2 FY27 earnings call • Read the original

Supriya Lifescience, the API manufacturer, reported a quarter that tells a story every Indian investor recognises: the numbers are fine, the guidance is not. Revenue for Q2 FY27 came in at &rupee;206 crore, up 3.2 per cent year on year. EBITDA fell 37.5 per cent to &rupee;45 crore, margins at 22 per cent. And the full-year margin guidance was cut from 33–35 per cent to 23–25 per cent — a reset of ten points, delivered in a single call.

Management attributed the damage to three causes, and The Loggical Investor lays out the arithmetic with admirable clarity. First, the Ambernath facility: roughly six points of margin, about &rupee;60 crore of operating expense in FY27 against negligible revenue, because the EU audit management expected earlier has been pushed to November. Second, regulatory changes in Latin America: several high-margin controlled products were reclassified as controlled substances in Colombia, Peru, Uruguay and other markets, forcing customers to obtain import authorisations — about three points, with shipments likely disrupted for four to five months. Third, raw material and solvent inflation: another three points, despite a backward-integrated model, because not all basic chemicals are made in-house.

The margin bridge, Q2 FY27

What elevates the piece above a routine earnings note is the author’s criticism of management’s communication — and it is a criticism worth carrying into any boardroom. In the Q1 call on 14 August, management reiterated its confidence in 33–35 per cent margins for FY27, even though the Q1 margin had already fallen to 25 per cent, higher solvent and power costs had already been acknowledged, and the Ambernath EU audit dates — second half of November — were already known. Regulatory audits take time, upfront expenses are unavoidable, commercial production does not translate into optimal utilisation overnight: none of this was new information in Q2. The author’s point is not that management caused the margin fall, but that it should have priced the execution realities into its guidance — a wider range, say 25–30 per cent, would have been more prudent than holding the line at 33–35 per cent and then cutting by ten points.

The forward picture is mixed but not bleak. Management still expects to hit its &rupee;1,000 crore revenue target for FY27 — H1 stands at &rupee;396 crore, so H2 needs &rupee;604 crore, a meaningful step-up. The pipeline has real substance: an anaesthetic product with CE certification and US DMF approval expected, a large contract-manufacturing opportunity close to signing, two ADHD products with a $100–150 million API-level opportunity, a contrast-media launch targeted for next quarter, and a cardiovascular intermediate with multiple large contracts under discussion. But FY28, management concedes, is a transition year: no FY28 margin guidance was given, Ambernath needs roughly a year to a year and a half after approvals to reach scale, and its revenue breakeven sits at &rupee;160–200 crore against a long-term potential of &rupee;450–500 crore.

The author’s verdict is measured: a good business with several temporary headwinds hitting simultaneously; existing investors from lower levels can track execution, fresh money should wait and watch, and the next checkpoint is Q3 FY27. The broader lesson travels well beyond one small-cap pharma stock: guidance is a promise, and a promise broken by ten points costs more in trust than the margin points themselves. As the author puts it, better communication during difficult phases can make a meaningful difference to investor confidence — and the absence of the chairman, Satish Wagh, from a call like this one was noticed.
