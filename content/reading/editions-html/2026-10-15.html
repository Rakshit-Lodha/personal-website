The Harness Is the New Operating System

Via @gregisenberg — 48 hours in San Francisco, 21 observations
Illustration: the harness layer — a conductor platform routing tasks, memory and data between models and agents. In Isenberg’s new anatomy of computing, the model is the CPU, the harness is the OS, and skills are the apps.

Greg Isenberg spent two days in San Francisco with billionaires, data labs and AI founders running one-to-hundred-billion-dollar companies, and came back with twenty-one observations that read less like a trip report and more like an industry constitution. The sharpest one redraws the whole computer: the model is the CPU, the agent harness is the operating system, and skills and plugins are the apps. Everything you thought was a wrapper is infrastructure; everything you thought was the product was the chip.

The implication flips the last two years of snobbery on its head. The “GPT wrapper” sneer assumed the model did the work. Isenberg’s San Francisco consensus says the model contributes roughly twenty percent of the value — the harness, the routing between models per job, the evals, the memory, the A/B testing, does the rest. Vertical agent harnesses are, in his words, the good new wrappers: thin on UI, heavy on APIs, data layers and tools, doing one job’s workflow end to end. The best diligence question for an AI startup is no longer about the model at all: does a new model make you better or worse? If you get stronger when the base model changes, you own the durable layer. If you get commoditised, you were never more than a feature of someone else’s chip.

Two more observations from the twenty-one are worth pinning to the wall. First, value is migrating to data: data brokers are reportedly earning around two million dollars a week selling company data to labs, and legacy SaaS firms may be mispriced — not as software businesses, but as data companies sitting on the training sets of the future. Second, the next fight is memory: remembering the user beats raw model smarts, and real agent-failure logs inside companies are becoming uniquely valuable assets. Click-heavy legacy software doesn’t survive the agentic era; whoever exposes their internal data over MCP first — and almost everyone’s internal data is a mess — wins the integration race. It’s a consultancy-scale opportunity hiding inside every enterprise IT department.

Your saves this week prove the thesis is already productising in three directions. First, Dada’s Product Design Harness at Lumenridge: “STOP LETTING YOUR AI AGENTS BREAK YOUR PRODUCT,” his post opens, and the method is a loop the whole team runs — Document → Prototype → Govern → Build. Anyone on the team can now spin up a working prototype; without a harness, five prototypers hand you five forks of your product. With one, you get five tested ideas and one product — designers governing, engineers merging, the company’s own components doing the building. Second, Instinct, a Y Combinator-backed launch claiming the first frontier agent harness for individual traders: a team from Squarepoint Capital, CERN, the USC Quantum Computing Center and Toss, building agents with their own computers that synthesise market data, news and social in parallel — the organisational capacity of an investment firm, given to one person. “The individual becomes the portfolio manager,” their thread says; edge concentrates in worldview, judgement and bets. Third, Delian Asparouhov’s read on Ramp’s Glass launch: everyone thought Ramp was corporate cards, but they built a Codex/Cowork competitor that’s arguably better — because it’s a third party trying to save you money, not a lab selling you tokens.

“STOP LETTING YOUR AI AGENTS BREAK YOUR PRODUCT.”— Dada, @iamarnob6543, on the design harness

The remaining observations are a futures market in miniature. About twelve months from agents that learn your job by watching you work. Unprecedented cloning and reverse-engineering of software with agents — your moat’s source code is legible now. Physical AI rising, with deployment — not research — as the hard part. Cybersecurity, especially post-breach cleanup, heating up because each agent is a new attack surface. Dead startups’ data — Slack, email, code — worth licensing. Monetising the “agent web” still early. Agent insurance coming. The most-wanted hire: someone with taste who writes evals. A new career pattern: raise VC, become a creator, fail, bootstrap as a full-time creator. And the sobering closer: the bar is now a billion dollars of revenue in twelve months — but retention is the hidden risk, and distribution is brutally hard because attention is the scarce resource.

Main learnings

  • Own the harness, not the chip. The durable question for any AI startup: does a new model make you better or worse? Better means you own the OS layer.
  • Data is the new balance sheet. Brokers selling company data to labs at ~$2M/week; legacy SaaS may be undervalued as data companies; agent-failure logs are proprietary gold.
  • Memory beats smarts. The next platform fight is who remembers the user — raw model intelligence is table stakes.
  • Prototyping without governance is forking. Lumenridge’s loop — Document, Prototype, Govern, Build — is the template for teams where everyone can build.
  • Hire taste that writes evals. The scarcest role in the agentic org isn’t the researcher; it’s the person who can judge what good looks like, repeatedly.
Practical takeaway — this week, map your company’s data the way a lab would: what internal corpus (tickets, calls, failures, playbooks) could a harness route on? Then ask Isenberg’s diligence question of every AI tool you pay for — when the next model drops, does it make you stronger? Keep the ones that say yes.

Gemini 4 Argon Week: Launches Are Media Events Now

Via @sundarpichai, @PromptLLM, @OfficialLoganK — 5.4M views and counting
Illustration: a new frontier model lands and the whole skyline looks up. The launch is the product’s first act; the screenshots, clips and takes that follow are the second.

Sundar Pichai announced Gemini 4 Argon this week — “available globally today for developers and enterprises in the paid tier” — and the launch behaved less like a software release than a premiere. Five point four million views on the announcement post. A thirty-minute video with Sergey Brin and Josh Woodward on how they got here. Elon Musk quote-posting his congratulations with a line that doubles as media strategy: CEOs posting to the public directly on X is the best way to get your message out.

The ecosystem did the rest of the marketing for free. @PromptLLM’s “insane take from Gemini 4 Argon” screenshot pulled 377K views. Pichai himself quote-posted a creator’s video-generation clip, admitting he’d watched it a few times — “especially to the end” — which is the modern launch funnel in one gesture: ship the model, let the internet find the jaw-drop, amplify the best one from the CEO’s own account. It pairs neatly with something David Senra clipped from Alexandr Wang this week: modern product marketing is now all driven by the screenshot. The demo you can repost beats the benchmark you can’t.

The Argon week — how a model launch propagates

The deeper play is the developer surface. Argon ships globally to developers and enterprises first — the paid tier, the API, the tooling — because the model war is fought where the harness gets built. That’s why Logan Kilpatrick’s thirty-minute conversation with Brin matters more than the announcement: it’s the “how we got here and the path ahead” story that tells builders where to place their bets. And in the same week, Sameer Samat announced Google Play is evolving to help people discover “trusted and high-quality apps” — curation as the answer to infinite supply. When anyone can build, the scarce layer is the one that decides what’s worth finding.

Main learnings

  • The screenshot is the launch. Wang’s rule, via Senra: the repostable demo beats the un-repostable benchmark — build one jaw-drop moment per release.
  • CEOs are the media now. Musk’s read is right: the highest-leverage distribution for a launch is the chief executive’s own account.
  • Ship to the harness-builders first. Argon’s developer-and-enterprise-first rollout concedes where the moat forms: whoever routes the model into workflows wins.
  • Curation scales with supply. Play Store’s trust push is the platform answer to the wrapper explosion — when everything is buildable, discovery is the product.
Practical takeaway — if you ship anything this month, design its “insane take” screenshot before you design its landing page. One frame that makes someone repost without context is worth a hundred benchmark tables.

How to Be More Ambitious: A Tutorial for Humans with Models

Via @felixrieseberg — eng lead on Claude, 13.1K bookmarks

Felix Rieseberg, an engineering lead working on Claude, noticed that “be more ambitious” is the kind of advice everyone gives and nobody can follow — so he wrote the tutorial he wished existed. His essay breaks ambition into three trainable dimensions: Altitude (aim at outcomes, not tasks — crash-free apps instead of fixed crashes; redesigning the review instead of filling its template), Depth (expert-grade execution: the pros’ instruments not just their vocabulary, unreasonable goals, adversarially checking assumptions against raw data, an outside judge), and Range (cross-field thinking: assemble expert “crews,” swap nouns for verbs, never empty roleplay). All of it assumes the model has a computer and data access — ambition is now a tooling question.

The closing argument is the one to keep. Rieseberg reaches for the psychology of self-efficacy: belief in possibility drives accomplishment — Dantzig solving the “unsolvable” homework, the four-minute mile, The Inner Game of Tennis, Linux. As capabilities leap, he argues, we need deliberate techniques for bigger goals, because the bottleneck moved from what’s possible to what we dare to attempt. Scott Farkas’s gloss on the post is the TL;DR the feed deserved: most people aren’t using today’s AI to its full potential. 6.7K likes, 13.1K bookmarks — the most-bookmarked essay of your week, which suggests the bottleneck is real and widely felt.

The ambition ladder — after Felix Rieseberg

Main learnings

  • Altitude: restate the task one level up — from fixing crashes to shipping crash-free, from filling templates to redesigning the ritual.
  • Depth: use the instruments, not just the vocabulary — unreasonable goals, adversarial checks against raw data, a judge outside the loop.
  • Range: assemble expert crews and swap nouns for verbs — roleplay is cheap, cross-field structure is not.
  • Self-efficacy is the meta-skill: Dantzig, the four-minute mile, Linux — belief in possibility keeps being the binding constraint, now with better tools.
Practical takeaway — take one task this week and re-ask it at altitude: what’s the outcome one level above the request? Then give the model the computer and the data and ask for the deeper version.

Railway Hacked AI SEO: $20M to ~$100M ARR in One Year

Via @irabukht — 13 numbered points, quoting @nakul

Nakul Mandan’s line set the table: Railway started the year at $20M ARR and is now at roughly $100M — a solo founder, fully remote, about forty people. Ira Bodnar’s thirteen-point breakdown of how is this week’s most actionable thread, because the channel isn’t ads or outbound. It’s AI recommendations. Claude Code now picks Railway for 82% of Python apps, and the founder treats that as a growth channel the way a previous generation treated Google.

The playbook is thirteen moves, all legible: 6,653 how-to pages for self-hosting tools like n8n with one-click Railway setup. 127 framework deploy guides — Next.js, Django, Rails, FastAPI, Laravel, Go. Comparison and migration pages against Heroku, Vercel, Render, Replit, Lovable and five more. A help centre rewritten for AI: an index file plus plain-text versions. Agent-oriented docs for one-step Claude Code and Cursor setup. ChatGPT and Claude Code plugins for chat-driven deploys. And the killer detail: “ssh railway new” — a no-signup server for agents. No human onboarding, no friction; the agent provisions its own box. A weekly changelog since 2021 — 312 pages — keeps the corpus fresh, and every new model release lifts signups; there was no summer slowdown this year. All of it run by a seven-person GTM team.
