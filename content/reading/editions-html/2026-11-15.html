Bring Jensen Back: The Board Letter, the Petition, and Silicon Valley’s Open Revolt

Brad Gerstner & Gavin Baker • Elon Musk • Pavel Durov • Naval • hidecloud • 14–15 Nov 2026

Three days after Nvidia’s board pushed out the founder who built it, the counter-attack went public — and it is being fought the way Silicon Valley fights everything now: an open letter, a petition site that crashed under the load, and a timeline full of founders picking sides.

The sharpest move came on Saturday morning from Brad Gerstner of Altimeter Capital and Gavin Baker of Atreides Management. Their letter to the Nvidia board, published on Altimeter/Atreides letterhead, asks for “a slow, measured, and deliberate process regarding the future leadership of Nvidia that leaves all options on the table, including the potential return of Jensen Huang” — and warns the board not to make a decision to replace Jensen that undermines shareholders’ best interests. The letter notes the company will be capably led in the interim by Colette Kress and her team. It is, in effect, a demand that the board treat the succession as reversible.

Alongside it, a petition — standwithjensen.com, an open letter to the board — was overwhelmed by sign-ups within hours. Its form showed 254 of a 2,000-signature goal when the traffic hit. Amjad Masad of Replit stepped in, working with the page’s owner to raise the rate limit so it could scale, and posted: “It should now scale! Feel free to share.” The page is built and hosted on Replit — fitting, somehow, that the campaign to reinstate a chip founder runs on a vibe-coded site that needed a rate-limit fix in public.

Then Elon Musk signed. “A petition is underway to reinstate Jensen and I’m signing it too,” he posted at 2:21am on Saturday, quoting it again with a single word — “Jensen” — over an a16z video. Travis Kalanick answered with two hundred-points emojis. The video Musk quoted is a curious artefact: it is the speech from the film Air, Matt Damon as Sonny Vaccaro telling Michael Jordan his story will make people want to fly — “You’re gonna be remembered forever because some things are eternal.” Repurposed, it is now a eulogy for a living founder.

“Jensen Huang is the Steve Jobs of Nvidia. He is irreplaceable.”— Naval, 13 November

Not everyone is pleading. Pavel Durov, who was himself thrown out of VK by his board in 2014 for refusing spies, posted the week’s most Durov sentence: “Jensen made a rookie mistake. He had a board. In 2014, my board kicked me out for saying no to spies. In 2026, Jensen’s board kicked him out for saying yes to one. So the board gets you either way. Founders: Don’t have a board. Be the board.” It is half joke, half governance theory — and it lands because it names the real question. A founder-CEO who is technical, aggressive, and credible enough to be aggressive on day one is, as Shaun Maguire put it in yesterday’s edition, nearly impossible to replace. The board’s job is oversight; the market’s verdict this week is that oversight just destroyed the thing it was meant to protect.

The most human document in the pile is not a letter but a dinner story. hidecloud, founder of Manus, was seated across from Jensen at a dinner with President Macron at the Élysée Palace in June 2025, four months after Manus launched. Jensen was half an hour late; Macron asked him to give a toast; he picked up his wine glass and improvised. hidecloud typed the whole thing into his Notes that night, and posted the screenshot on Saturday. The English in the image is Manus’s translation of his Chinese note, so treat it as a paraphrase, not verbatim — but the argument is unmistakably Jensen’s.

Software culture, he said, is about not chasing perfection: every software company that chased perfection is dead. Ship it even if it is imperfect, then fix it. European culture clashes with this — everything polished to the extreme before it ships. He raised his glass of red wine: “Like this. You age it. We Americans sold all the wine years ago.” Then beta, and Japan’s wabi-sabi — the art of imperfection, the perfection of imperfection. AI is exactly that kind of imperfect perfection: don’t spend all day hunting for flaws and trying to regulate it; explore it, appreciate what it can actually bring to Europe; you can’t wait until every compliance box is ticked before you start using it. And he praised France — only France can build AI in Europe, because only France still insists on building nuclear power plants. Energy is what makes data centres possible.

Charlie Munger at 98: Avoid the Big Mistakes

Stripe Press • Poor Charlie’s Almanack • Interview transcribed in full by The Unread Pile

This interview sat in the pile for days while we worked out how to transcribe a 79-minute video. It was worth the wait. Munger, interviewed by Stripe Press about Poor Charlie’s Almanack, gives the cleanest statement of his whole system in one sitting: a house he designed himself, a career built on other people’s foolishness, and a closing line that should be printed on a card.

It starts, wonderfully, with architecture. Munger designed and built the house he lives in, 61 years ago, on graph paper — “my crossword puzzles are building designs.” One storey instead of two, the main room facing the backyard, ten-foot ceilings, a fireplace in both the library and the main room, a private courtyard for light, a big parking court because he had lots of children and they all had cars. It is worth, he says, at least two million dollars more now — maybe four or five million — just because it is more convenient to live in. “People kill themselves following in stairs and so forth.” The house is the philosophy in miniature: avoid the stupid way to die, and a lot of success follows.

That is the frame of the whole interview, and of the Almanack: avoiding mistakes is a better framing than seeking brilliance. Munger fell into investing, he says, because the wealth advisory business was full of people doing dumb things — “a hog heaven field for somebody who could develop anti-asininity.” His temperament and his location forced him into investing his own money shrewdly, and he was shrewd even when he didn’t have much money. “And when it worked, I just kept doing more and more of it.”

“I’d rather have a brief telephone conversation with somebody I trust than a 40-page contract prepared by the finest law firm in the world with somebody I don’t trust.”— Charlie Munger, on Berkshire’s culture

The method is the famous latticework, stated without mystique. He learned the big ideas in pretty much all the disciplines to fluency by constantly using them, ignoring the boundaries between them. When he met Pascalian probability he saw instantly how important it was and “utterly mastered” it, using it routinely all his life. Harvard Business School, in its early days, taught decision-tree theory with great pomp — which was, Munger notes dryly, remedial high-school maths for graduate students. “Somebody says that old Joe has common sense. They don’t mean that. They mean he’s got uncommon sense.” Sensible people, on pretty much everything they deal with, are uncommon, not common. “Most people are a mass of crazy prejudice.”

Asked what a 25-year-old investor should do over the next fifty years, he gives the same answer in different words: avoid the relatively simple mistakes that can be avoided, think of investing as owning the underlying business and its quality and sustainability, and take the punch-hard approach — a small number of investments rather than playing or timing the market. It requires considerable self-discipline and considerable skill, and “most people a little lack the discipline skill.” What do they screw up? They get carried along too much.

On Berkshire’s culture, the answer is gloriously literal. Bureaucracies, he says, create their own internal dynamics: everybody protects everybody else, nobody changes anything, nobody rattles any feathers, and the net result is stupid decisions. Berkshire avoids that mostly “by not having anybody around. You can’t be bureaucratic if they’re not there. There is nobody in the head office.” The other half of the culture is trust: a brief phone call with someone trustworthy beats a forty-page contract with someone who isn’t. And on scale: a hedge-fund manager he describes made a lot of money while the per-dollar return was zero, because as the fund grew he “really made a lot of dumb mistakes” — a wonderful record that was in fact terrible.

He extends the frame to societies, too. Societies need to avoid the major mistakes just like individuals do — like avoiding nuclear war. “We’re lucky we’ve done it so far, but if enough crazy people have enough hydrogen bombs, there will eventually be enough hatred… you could almost count on” an atomic war someday. In his generation it was quite unlikely; “I think it’s getting more likely, not less.” The interview ends where it should: “Find out what works, and then do [it]. Avoid the big mistakes. Take a multi-disciplinary approach.”

The takeawayMunger’s system is not a list of clever moves. It is a filter: learn the big ideas, refuse the dumb mistakes, concentrate your bets, and hire people you trust so you don’t need contracts. Everything else is commentary — including, pointedly, the architecture of your own house.

Siri Is Soooo…: The Beautiful Device With the Low-Grade AI

Vijay Shekhar Sharma • Josh Elman • Joanna Stern (video transcribed in full)

Vijay Shekhar Sharma’s verdict on the iPhone’s assistant is short and brutal: “It is absolutely shocking to get stuck on a beautiful device like an iPhone with such low grade ai. #siri is soooo…” The video he attached — Joanna Stern, speaking to camera — is the evidence, and we transcribed it in full.

Stern’s catalogue of failures is specific, which is what makes it damning. Siri mishears “snakes in rumination” as “snakes in formation or snakes in Rome?” Other devices in the room think she is talking to their Siri; the Siri across the room goes off on her phone; “sometimes Siri’s just talk to each other.” Her son asked Siri a maths question on a Sunday morning and Siri got stuck on it for three minutes until Stern came in and tapped the screen to quiet it down. And then the one that stings: Siri hallucinated that it didn’t have a camera on the HomeView, even though Stern was looking directly into the camera. “I don’t have a camera to see you.” “You don’t have a camera?” “That’s right.” “You’re gaslighting me.” Apple told her the hallucination will be fixed in a software update next month.

Josh Elman, quoting a HomeView review, adds the other half: these things work great with Siri AI on the phone, but the implementation on HomeView is still catching up, and Alexa isn’t much better. Then the line that matters: “It is a good reminder that non-deterministic LLMs -> deterministic functions is hard.” That is the whole story in one sentence. A voice assistant is not a chatbot; it is a probabilistic model wired into deterministic functions — timers, cameras, lights, calls. Every time the model’s judgement wobbles, a function misfires in the physical world, in front of your family, on a Sunday morning. The phone forgives it because the phone is a screen you can correct. The home does not.

The takeawayThe gap between “impressive demo” and “works in the house” is the non-determinism gap. Until the model-to-function layer is reliable, the most beautiful hardware in the world will keep getting embarrassed by its own assistant.

Bets Settled: The 2023 Prediction That AI Just Obliterated

Sheel Mohnot • Keith Rabois • 15 Nov 2026

In September 2023, Keith Rabois bet Sheel Mohnot that no San Francisco-based company founded between March 2020 and September 2023 would ever exit above ten billion dollars. On Saturday, Mohnot posted the receipt: “Bets settled. Thanks for playing ball, @rabois!”

The evidence is two companies. Cursor — founded in the window — sold for $60 billion. And Anthropic, Mohnot notes, “is set to exit above $1T next week,” with its IPO pricing on 18 November and trading the next day. Rabois’s second bet, that Democrats would lose the House, was listed alongside the first; the post settles the pair without spelling out the second outcome. The point of the post is the first bet, and it is devastating in the specific way only a timestamped prediction can be: three years ago, the smart money said the 2020–23 vintage could not produce a ten-billion-dollar exit. The vintage has now produced a sixty-billion-dollar sale and a trillion-dollar listing in the same week.

What makes the bet interesting is what it reveals about prediction itself. Rabois’s thesis was reasonable in 2023 — a cohort born into a downturn, in a city everyone was writing off. What it missed was a regime change: the AI wave turned a narrow founding window into the most valuable cohort in startup history. The lesson is not that Rabois was foolish. It is that bets about exits are secretly bets about whether the world will stay the same shape, and in AI it never does.

The takeawayWrite your predictions down, timestamp them, and settle them in public. The receipt — Mohnot posted the payment proof, partly obscured, alongside the original September 2023 posts — is the whole genre: accountability with a paper trail.

Paytm’s Long Climb Back: From ₹310 to the Doorstep of Its IPO Price

Madhav Chanchani • The Arc • 13 Nov 2026

Paytm’s share price is inching back to its IPO issue price of ₹2,150, and it has already crossed its listing price of ₹1,950. It hit an all-time low of ₹310 in May 2024, after the RBI’s action on Paytm Payments Bank. “What a comeback since then!”

The numbers tell the story. One 97 Communications, Paytm’s parent, was trading at ₹1,939.90 on 13 November — up 72.93% over six months, climbing from around ₹1,100 in July. The low of ₹310 in May 2024 came after the Reserve Bank’s action against Paytm Payments Bank, which froze the wallet business that had defined the company. The comeback has a regulatory chapter too: the RBI has granted in-principle authorisation to Paytm Payments Services Ltd to issue and operate prepaid payment instruments — wallets — under PPSL rather than the payments bank. The wallet business is coming back, under a different roof, with the regulator’s blessing.

Paytm’s round trip (₹ per share)

The deeper point is about what a comeback like this is made of. It is not one thing — it is a repaired regulatory relationship, a business that kept operating while its flagship product was frozen, and a market that eventually repriced survival as recovery. The wallet returning under PPSL is the cleanest symbol: same product, new structure, regulator onside. For a company that was written off as a cautionary tale about Indian fintech, the chart now reads as a case study in outlasting your own obituary.

The takeawayThe distance from ₹310 to ₹1,940 is not a rally. It is a rebuild — of the licence, the structure, and the story. Watch whether the wallet relaunch under PPSL converts the price recovery into a business recovery.

Sachin Bansal and Manav Garg Bet on Agentic Commerce

Chandra R. Srikanth • Moneycontrol (article read in full) • 13 Nov 2026

Flipkart and Navi founder Sachin Bansal is teaming up with Manav Garg for a new agentic commerce startup, Moneycontrol reports exclusively — and the numbers show how seriously the pair is taking it.

Garg, who serves as executive chairman of vibe-coding startup Emergent, has launched the venture with Bansal looking to invest. Bansal has committed $20–30 million (₹190–280 crore), and Garg is looking to raise a significantly larger amount — around $100 million (₹950 crore) as initial capital. Discussions with other potential investors are underway. Bansal is expected to play a more active role than capital alone: he will advise Garg on strategic decisions related to commerce, while Garg brings the AI expertise.

The CVs explain the pairing. Garg co-founded Together Fund, the AI-focused venture firm, in July 2021 with Freshworks founder Girish Mathrubootham; before that he founded and ran enterprise SaaS company Eka Software in 2004, which was acquired by private equity firm STG in 2024. He also co-founded SaaSBoomi, the community for India’s SaaS founders, since renamed AIBoomi, and has been an early investor in Emergent, Composio and RapidClaims. He joined Emergent as executive chairman only in June this year; the company says he continues to serve as chairman and remains closely engaged on its strategic priorities. Bansal, meanwhile, runs Navi, which Moneycontrol recently reported is raising around $40 million from Elevation, months after raising $100 million from Prosus — Prosus is a common investor in Emergent and Navi.

The context is a widening bet on the application layer. Garg is the latest executive to start an agentic commerce company, as a growing number of people believe commerce as it exists today is going to evolve. Vinay Singh, co-founder and partner at Fireside Ventures, recently quit to start his own agentic commerce company. And investors are increasingly looking beyond foundation models towards application-layer businesses and specialised AI use cases, with Peak XV Partners, Lightspeed and General Catalyst in talks to back at least half a dozen startups across AI agents, enterprise software and AI-led biotech.

The takeawayThe pattern to watch: commerce expertise plus AI expertise, funded at $100-million scale, aimed at a category that does not exist yet. Bansal knows how India buys; Garg knows how agents work. If agentic commerce is real, this is what its founding team looks like.

Advanced Evals: Stop Measuring the Wrong Thing

Lenny’s Newsletter • Hamel Husain & Shreya Shankar • 22 Sep 2026

Evals are becoming the defining skill for AI product people — Mike Krieger says writing evals is now probably the most important thing to teach product people, and Garry Tan calls evals “the real moat for AI startups.” But Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar, drawing on work with more than 50 AI companies, found that most teams skip the first stage and jump straight to writing metrics. They end up measuring the wrong things.

The skipped stage is error discovery, and the authors argue it is the eval equivalent of product discovery: just as product discovery shows which problems are worth solving, error discovery reveals which AI failures are worth measuring. Without it, teams build dashboards around generic metrics that waste time and steer the product toward the wrong outcomes. “If you only have time for one part of the eval process, you should prioritise it.”

The core problem is criteria drift. Reviewing examples changes your definition of good — you only discover what “good” means by looking at the data. Their example: an AI leasing assistant, Nurture Boss, answering a prospect who says a unit is out of budget. The assistant politely says goodbye. To most agents reviewing that trace, it looks like a success. But the product goal is to facilitate sales — the assistant should have explored cheaper units or other properties owned by the same company. You would only know to add “objection handling” to your criteria after seeing the trace yourself. In a study of 100 production traces, agents missed failures requiring product judgement, caught obvious failures like answers contradicted by tool output, and found issues humans missed — while flagging good responses as failures.

The error-discovery loop (30 minutes, once learned)

1. Collect tracesEvery user session logged whole: input, system prompt, tool calls, retrieved context, intermediate calls, final output.
→
2. Review 10 by handAnnotate what bothers you, in plain words a colleague could understand. No root-cause analysis. Stop at the first upstream error.
→
3. Let the agent suggestThe coding agent proposes more issues to accept or reject. Argue with it; several human-agent iterations before suggestions get useful.
→
4. Reach 100 tracesCluster annotations into failure modes and count them. Now you know what to measure — and what to fix first.

The proof points are the kind that make CFOs pay attention. Shopify used evals to guide an AI workflow builder that was 2.2 times faster and 68% cheaper than the frontier-model system it replaced. Cursor developed Auto Balance routing with evals, cutting costs 41% while raising satisfaction. Ramp lifted the precision of matching transactions in receipt photos from 35% to 83% with on-device models. Harvey rebuilt its AI contract reviewer with evals and nearly doubled its internal quality score. The mechanism is the same in each case: production errors flagged by evals become additional test cases, and the advantage compounds over time. This is directly the user’s own world — the LLM-as-judge eval pipeline behind the ET Money support agent, which cut monthly tickets from 19,000 to 9,000 — and the post is effectively the advanced manual for it.

The takeawayDo not start with metrics. Start with ten traces, read by a human, annotated in plain language. The failures you find by hand are the ones worth measuring; everything else is dashboard theatre.

Divergence With a Vengeance: The 19th Century Is Where the Action Is

Contraptions • Venkatesh Rao • 4 Oct 2026

Venkatesh Rao’s world-machines theory has a new update, and it relocates the hinge of modernity. The 19th century, he argues after months of book-club reading, is where almost all the action is for the Divergence Machine — the world system that emerged between 1600 and 2000. And much of it was precipitated by the French Revolution and the Napoleonic wars.

The framing is pure Rao: divergence was prototyped and theorised as a mechanism in 1600–1800, then “deployed to production” during 1789–1814, an explosion through which unbridled plurality, epistemic and ontological divergence, and liberalism in the modern sense took off across the globe. Like any exponential, network-effect process, the back half of the growth curve did the heavy lifting. He finally understands why Hegel tagged the Battle of Jena-Auerstedt (1806) as the candidate for the End of History — a breakpoint when one kind of historical process concluded and a different kind took off. Fukuyama was right to relocate it to the end of the Cold War, Rao says, because history only ended for elites in 1806; 1989–92 ended it for everybody.

Then the turn that makes the essay land in this paper: history may be restarting. “AIs are a new class of political actors that have yet to enter history,” Rao writes. Whatever your views on sentience or personhood, they are emerging as true actors in the same sense nations and corporations already are — and while nations and corporations were accounted for in Fukuyama’s end-of-history tag, AIs have emerged since then. The other relocation: 1848, not the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, is the right date for the nation-state. Christopher Clark’s Revolutionary Spring, on the wave of revolutions across Europe in 1848, is the one book he would recommend from the club so far. The 1848 wave, he argues, continued directly into the colonial freedom movements — the first war of Indian independence in 1857, Perry forcing Japan open in 1853, the Taiping rebellion in 1850 — carried by Western-educated lawyers like Nehru and Gandhi, who imported not just constitutional machinery but liberalism itself. Even the American Civil War, in this telling, was part of the same global wave of modern nations emerging, not a uniquely American event.

There is a footnote worth catching: at the Protocol Symposium, Rao gave a talk on Blygger, an RSS-3.0-ish, AI-native future-of-blogging protocol he has been developing with Claude. A week before the talk it was a rudimentary spec and a janky demo; now there is an active core group, multiple clients, and a dozen blygs already live. The protocol for the next divergence, being built in public.

The takeawayIf the 19th century was divergence deployed to production, the question is what the 21st is deploying. Rao’s answer: new political actors — AIs — entering history for the first time. Read the Munger piece above with that in mind: avoiding the big mistakes matters more, not less, when the actors are new.
