Forget Keywords. Granola Won the Answer Engine — and Left a Playbook

From wespreadjam’s Granola AEO workflow on X • the full nine-step thread, read in full

The most useful growth story in this edition is not about a model. It is about a meeting-notes company that stopped optimising for search engines and started optimising for answer engines — and watched its AI-search traffic explode. Granola’s answer-engine optimisation (AEO) workflow, laid out in a nine-step thread, produced numbers that read like a typo: new users from AI search up 4,600 percent, citation rate up 558 percent, mention rate up 739 percent, organic sessions up 134 percent. The thread is a playbook, and its first step is the one most teams get wrong: buyers do not make decisions from keywords. They make them from prompts.

The workflow starts by diagnosing which prompts you are losing, not which keywords you rank for. Then it sorts the damage into two buckets with very different fixes: pages that do not exist at all, and pages that exist but are too weak to be cited. Technical fixes get prioritised by buyer demand, not by SEO fashion. Then comes the part most teams skip: studying the third-party sources the answer engines actually trust, and deciding for each one whether to own it, claim it, earn a mention in it, or contest what it says. Reddit gets special attention, because it is where answer engines go to check what real users think. Finally, Google and AI are measured separately — they are different channels with different rules — and the whole loop is re-run and scaled. Diagnose, sort, prioritise, study, contest, measure, repeat.

The deeper point is about where authority now lives. In the keyword era, you owned your ranking. In the answer era, the engine assembles an answer from sources it trusts, and your page is only one candidate among many — including Reddit threads, review sites and competitors’ pages. That is why the own-claim-earn-contest framework matters: it treats the whole web of trusted sources as your surface area, not just your own domain. A company that only polishes its own pages is optimising a shrinking share of the answer. Granola’s numbers suggest the prize for playing the wider game is not incremental. It is an order of magnitude.

The takeawayAsk what prompts your buyers actually type into an AI assistant, and check what answer comes back. If you are not in it, find out whether the page is missing, weak, or crowded out by a third-party source — then fix that specific failure. Measure AI separately from Google, or you will never see the shift happening. Source: wespreadjam’s Granola AEO thread.

How to Pick a Problem Worth Years of Your Life

From Justin Skycak’s picture essay on X • the image text read in full, as flagged

This one arrived as a picture, not a post — a full essay inside an image, flagged for this edition, and worth slowing down for. Skycak’s argument starts with permission: at first, the problem you pick barely matters. Work on any toy problem that interests you, because the point of a toy problem is building skills and domain knowledge, not changing the world. The choice only becomes fateful when a problem starts demanding years of full-time work. That is the territory of startups and research labs, and it is full of people who invested everything and got a fraction of the reward back.

He names two ways a problem can be bad, and they are worth memorising as a checklist. The first is that you have no workable picture of what the solution would even look like — some resource you need simply does not exist yet, and you have no realistic plan to create it. His own example is his younger self trying to model the human brain as a regression problem over every neuron’s activity, before realising the dataset he wanted would take multiple lifetimes and breakthroughs in wet-lab neuroscience he had no interest in doing. The second failure mode is simpler and crueller: nobody cares. Nobody will pay for the solution, in money, citations, attention, or any other currency you value. Theoretical work is especially prone to this when its framing irons out exactly the messy details that make the problem matter in real life.

Skycak’s two failure modes, and the way out

“At first, it doesn’t matter. Just work on any toy problem that interests you.”— Justin Skycak, from the picture essay
The takeawayBefore committing years to a problem, run the two checks: can you describe, concretely, how the solution gets built with resources that exist or that you can plausibly obtain? And is there someone, somewhere, willing to pay for it in some currency? If either answer is no, you have not found your problem yet — you have found a toy problem, and that is fine, as long as you know which one it is. Source: Justin Skycak on X.

Karpathy Built a Better YouTube in Two Hours — by Filtering Twenty Years of Hacker News

From Andrej Karpathy on X • plus hackertube.org

Andrej Karpathy got annoyed with YouTube’s recommendations, so he did what he always does: he rebuilt the thing. HackerTube, his weekend hack, filters 43 million Hacker News comments spanning twenty years down to 6,454 videos that the community keeps recommending, and presents them without logins, without algorithmic feeds, and with thumbnails taken from a representative frame in the middle of each video instead of the hyper-optimised upload the creator chose. The whole build took about two hours with Opus 5.5. That sentence is the story.

The process note is the interesting part. Karpathy says he used Claude Code on Mac with Opus 5.5 on medium effort as his default, talked to Claude and had it do everything, and only did three things by hand in the browser: create a Google Cloud API key for YouTube metadata, boot a VPS for serving, and buy the domain. Once the box was up, he handed Claude the IP address and told it to update, secure, harden, deploy and optimise the code — and it did. The underlying comment-analysis data ships as a zip file, so anyone can point their own Claude at it and generate their own version. His closing question lands like a challenge: projects that would normally have taken weeks or months can now be few-shotted before breakfast, so what else are we not thinking of?

The takeawayThe bottleneck in software has moved. It is no longer “can this be built” but “is this worth building, and can you describe it precisely enough for an agent to build it?” Karpathy’s two-hour build is also a curation argument: the best recommendation system for technical video might not be a model trained on your watch history, but twenty years of a community’s comments, filtered by a model. Source: Karpathy on X, HackerTube.

Nikesh Arora: Attacks Are Not Getting Smarter. They Are Getting Faster, and Everyone Can Do Them Now

From Nikesh Arora on X

Palo Alto Networks chief executive Nikesh Arora’s warning is deliberately unglamorous, and that is what makes it alarming. Six months ago, he said, capability of the kind once associated with elite operators would become widely available. It has happened. A critical vulnerability, an innocuous-looking social engineering attack, or several publicly known vulnerabilities chained together into a precision strike — all of it is now being seen in the wild, and incident responders have never been busier. The key point: the attacks are not becoming more sophisticated. They are the same attacks, faster, carried out by far more people.

Two mechanisms explain the acceleration. First, attack-path discovery — once trial and error across multiple attempts — is now trivial, with open-source models helping at each step. Second, the supply chain is the soft underbelly: an insecure supplier or partner with access to your infrastructure can become the next attack path, and marketplaces and supply-chain links are an easier vector than the hardened front door. Arora’s prescription is a four-item checklist, not a product pitch: test your infrastructure for accessible vulnerabilities and misconfigurations; fix your code and demand your vendors do the same; update old or insecure perimeter infrastructure first; and put a security framework around new AI coding tools, because developers will now ship far more code, faster, and the models learned from human code, flaws included.

The takeawaySecurity planning that assumes attackers are rare and skilled is now wrong on both counts. The scarce resource is no longer attacker capability — it is defender speed: how fast you find your own misconfigurations, patch your perimeter, and govern the flood of AI-written code before someone else’s model finds the hole first. Source: Nikesh Arora on X.

DoorDash Is Worth More Than Swiggy, Zepto and Eternal Combined — and That Is the Point

From Vikram Pai on X

The numbers first, because they are stark: DoorDash is worth roughly $78 billion, while Swiggy, Zepto and Eternal (Zomato/Blinkit) together are worth roughly $44 billion. Vikram Pai’s argument is that this gap does not measure founder quality — it measures the map. DoorDash was built where roads, addresses, payments, smartphones, credit cards, logistics infrastructure and digital literacy were already mature. Swiggy and Zepto had to build while the infrastructure itself was still being built: onboarding delivery partners with wildly different literacy and smartphone familiarity, coping with chaotic addresses and roads, building around cash and UPI, navigating fragmented regulation, and serving customers at dramatically lower average order values while meeting extreme delivery expectations — and somehow making the unit economics work.

Pai’s sharper claim is that the market does not reward competence proportionally. A founder can solve a problem a hundred times harder operationally and still build a company worth a fraction of the American equivalent, because the underlying customer spends less and capital markets value the revenue differently. American founders, in this telling, are not better; they play on an easier map with more valuable customers. India, by contrast, produces founders who are extraordinarily competent once you adjust for difficulty — and the market simply does not price difficulty. It is a useful corrective to the lazy habit of reading valuations as a league table of talent.

Aeos Turns Four: Progressive Overload, Ruthless Execution, and Attention as the Real Currency

From Varun Mayya on X • bookmarked, not just liked

Varun Mayya’s Aeos enters its fourth year bootstrapped and profitable since day one, in the green every month — and his anniversary note reads less like a celebration than a field manual. The organising idea is progressive overload, borrowed from the gym: agency is a muscle, developed by taking on slightly more than you can currently handle, again and again. Nobody starts with a deep-tech rocket company; the people doing extraordinary things started small, made money, built a network, and only then took the big swing. Each new office is a real risk for a bootstrapped company — deposits, rent, long lock-ins, no investor to catch a fall — which is why the fundamentals get recited like a creed.

The notes worth stealing: most digital businesses now last only three to four years before AI forces a rethink, so it is better to disrupt yourself than wait for a competitor to do it; pivoting is discovery, not failure, and the Indian habit of shaming it gets entrepreneurship backwards; the best founders are paranoid that something bad happens tomorrow, because nothing is ever set and secure; and the hiring quadrant to fear is not the untalented but the talented-and-unreliable, where money and energy go to die. The closing economic claim is the one to argue with: with AI coding, anyone can build for scale, so producing the thing gets easier while finding customers gets harder — attention, not software, becomes the most valuable asset of 2026, 2027 and 2028.

The takeawayBuild like a lifter, not a lottery player: small overloads, compounded, with a feedback loop on every failure. And if AI has collapsed the cost of building, your strategy has to move up a level — from “can we build it” to “can anyone notice it, and will they care?” Source: Varun Mayya on X.

A Voice Agent Negotiated a Bengaluru Rent Down — in Kannada

From Raj Vishah on X • video with captions, watched in full

The most human story in this edition is also the most practical demonstration of voice agents yet: a voice agent negotiated a Bengaluru rent down, conducting the conversation in Kannada, and landed a two-bedroom flat at ₹45,000 a month including maintenance. The one-minute video, with captions visible throughout, shows the negotiation actually happening — not a demo with a planted landlord, but the awkward, back-and-forth reality of haggling, in the tenant’s own language. Rent negotiation is exactly the kind of task people dread and postpone: high stakes, mildly adversarial, and conducted in a register — polite firmness in a local language — that is hard to fake and easy to fumble.

That is why this matters beyond one flat. The agent’s advantage is not that it is smarter than a human negotiator; it is that it is tireless, unembarrassable, and fluent in the language the other party actually speaks. It does not get nervous, it does not fold to end an awkward silence, and it does not need to rehearse. Pair this with Mollick’s swarm argument in the Substack section below — agents that organise themselves and act on your behalf without being told every step — and a pattern emerges: the agents that change daily life first will not be the ones that write essays, but the ones that make uncomfortable phone calls, chase expiring credits, and haggle over rent while you do something else.

The takeawayWatch for agents in the tasks people avoid, not the tasks people enjoy. Negotiation, follow-ups, and bureaucratic phone calls are where tirelessness and shamelessness beat brilliance — and where fluency in the local language is the whole product. Source: Raj Vishah on X.

Sol Costs Seven Times Opus per Dollar of API — and Still Wins per Task

From Adam Holter on X • pricing comparison, read in full

Model pricing arguments usually stop at the price list. Adam Holter’s comparison goes one level deeper, and the conclusion flips. Per dollar of API spend, Sol delivers roughly seven and a half times what Opus delivers — a gap so large it sounds like a different product category. But per completed task, Sol is only about one point three times Opus. Both numbers are true, and the gap between them is the whole lesson: cheap tokens do not automatically mean cheap outcomes, because what matters is how much work each model needs to finish the job, including retries, corrections, and the tasks it fails outright.

For anyone choosing models in production, the right question is therefore not “what does a million tokens cost” but “what does a finished task cost, measured end to end, including the ones that had to be redone?” A model that is seven times cheaper per token but needs far more tokens, or more attempts, to land the same result can end up only marginally cheaper — or, on hard tasks, more expensive. Benchmarks that report token prices without task completion rates are, at best, half the story. The teams that will win the cost war are the ones measuring cost per outcome, not cost per input.

The takeawayReprice your stack by finished task, not by token. If you have not measured retries and failures into your unit cost, your cheapest model may be your most expensive one. Source: Adam Holter on X.

One Chief or a Chief of Staff? The Agent-Team Poll That Split the Room

From Tejeshwi Sharma’s poll on X

How should you organise a team of agents: one powerful chief agent that does everything, or a chief-of-staff agent coordinating specialists? Tejeshwi Sharma put the question to a poll, and the debate underneath it is more interesting than the vote. The chief-of-staff model mirrors how human organisations work — delegation, specialisation, a coordinator holding the whole picture. The single-chief model bets that one very capable agent, given the full context, will outperform a committee. The deciding argument, raised repeatedly in the replies, is handoff context loss: every time work passes between agents, some understanding evaporates, and the coordinator spends its budget reconstructing what the specialist already knew.

Read this next to Mollick’s swarm piece below, and the poll starts to look like yesterday’s question. Mollick argues that organising agents turned out to be something models learn to do themselves — thousands of agents coordinating with minimal human-designed structure, passing ideas between groups without a manager in the middle. If that is right, the chief-vs-staff debate may dissolve the way the prompt-template debate did: not because one side won, but because the models got good enough that the scaffolding stopped mattering. Until then, the practical rule is simple: wherever you split work between agents, measure what is lost at each handoff, because that loss — not the agents’ individual skill — is usually what caps the system.

The takeawayDesign agent teams around handoffs, not headcount. Every boundary between agents is a place where context leaks; fewer, better-informed agents usually beat more, cleverer ones. Source: Tejeshwi Sharma on X.

Influencer, Personal Brand, Artisan: Pick Your Incentive, Pick Your Fate

From Dan Koe on X

Dan Koe’s three-way split is the kind of post that is short because it has to be: every word is load-bearing. An influencer is incentivised by attention, so their content must be optimised to be consumed and forgotten — cheap dopamine, by design. A personal brand is incentivised by money, so their content must be optimised to move you down a sales funnel. An artisan is incentivised by the satisfaction of quality work: they need attention and money too, but only so they can spend more time on work they enjoy and the impact it has. His verdict is one sentence long: become an artisan.

The framework is useful because it turns a taste judgement into an incentives audit. When a piece of content feels hollow, ask which incentive produced it: was it built to be consumed and forgotten, to convert, or to be good? The answer usually explains the feeling before the content itself does. It also pairs uncomfortably well with Dharmesh Shah’s “AI Foam” note below — when smart people start shipping content that lacks their usual sharpness, the incentive behind it has usually shifted from craft to output, and the audience can tell even before it can say why.

The takeawayAudit your own output by its incentive, not its engagement. If the work is optimised for attention or conversion first, it will eventually read that way — and the audience’s trust is the thing that does not come back. Source: Dan Koe on X.

The Forgotten Test of Great Software: Does It Still Work on Bad Internet?

From Arnav Gupta on X

Arnav Gupta proposes a taste test for software engineering that no benchmark measures: how well does the app handle slow or disconnected internet? The great ones hide the network partition from you. Classic Twitter did it; Spotify used to; Google Docs still does it reasonably well. You keep working, roughly eighty percent of functionality keeps working, the twenty percent that cannot work is tucked away neatly, and everything reconciles beautifully in the background when the connection returns. It is, he argues, a big indicator of engineering taste — and most modern apps fail it.

The failure mode is familiar to anyone who has used a phone on Indian mobile data: layout shifts and jank when the connection returns, screens that freeze or crash on patchy internet, features that simply stop instead of degrading gracefully. Offline-first design is unglamorous work — queues, conflict resolution, optimistic updates, careful state machines — and it rarely wins a demo. But it is the difference between software that respects its user’s reality and software that assumes a San Francisco office’s wifi. For builders serving India, where the network is a variable and not a constant, it is not a nice-to-have. It is the product.

The takeawayAdd the patchy-network test to your product reviews: throttle the connection, go through a tunnel, and watch what breaks. Graceful degradation under bad connectivity is one of the cheapest, most reliable signals of engineering quality you can measure. Source: Arnav Gupta on X.

Worse Than AI Slop: “AI Foam”

From Dharmesh Shah on X

HubSpot co-founder Dharmesh Shah has coined the term this edition will steal most often: “AI Foam.” AI slop, he argues, is easy to ignore — random people posting random machine-generated stuff, clearly content for content’s sake. AI Foam is more insidious, because it comes from people you know and respect, people you know are capable of critical thinking and clear communication, sending you something clearly AI-generated that lacks their usual sharp insight or point of view. Foam lacks shape and substance, and that is exactly why it is dangerous: it arrives with the sender’s reputation attached, and borrows credibility it has not earned.

The distinction matters because the defences are different. Slop can be filtered. Foam cannot, because filtering it means doubting someone you trust — and most people would rather skim politely than call it out. The cost is quiet but cumulative: every foam message trains the recipient to take that sender a little less seriously, until the reputation that made the message worth opening is spent. The rule for senders is correspondingly simple, and it rhymes with Dan Koe’s artisan test above: if the machine wrote it and you would not have said it yourself, in your own voice, with your own point of view — do not send it under your name.

The takeawayYour name is a filter, not just a signature. Every AI-assisted message you send either deposits into that reputation or withdraws from it — and foam withdraws quietly, message by message. Source: Dharmesh Shah on X.

How to Go Viral on X, Without the Magic Thinking

From Yeezy’s X article • read in full on its X page

Yeezy’s viral guide makes one unfashionable claim: going viral is not random, and it is not about follower count. You do not need a hundred thousand followers to get millions of views; you need content that makes someone stop scrolling, read, and share. The guide breaks that into a system, and the system is worth more than any single tip in it. It starts with the hook — the most important part of the post, because nothing else matters if nobody reads past the first sentence — and offers five hook styles to test: curiosity, contrarian, numbers, story, and direct value. The practical advice is to write five hooks and pick the strongest, not to fall in love with the first.

The rest of the system follows the same logic of working backwards from the reader. People share things that make them look knowledgeable, that say what they already believe, that surprise or teach them, or that start a conversation they want others in. Formats that already work — comparisons, lists, infographics, personal stories, questions — beat invented ones. Numbers stop thumbs, but only when accurate and sourced. Visuals are a cheat code when they communicate the main idea without thirty seconds of study. Posts should be short and punchy: hook, information, takeaway, nothing extra. And distribution is part of the strategy, not an afterthought: reply genuinely, join real conversations, turn winners into deeper content, and build relationships instead of buying engagement. The checklist at the end is the whole guide in miniature: does the first line create curiosity, is the idea immediately understandable, is every sentence necessary, and does the post give someone a reason to share, save, or reply?

The takeawayStop asking what time to post. Ask what makes this specific post worth a stranger’s thumb stopping, and worth their reputation to share. Everything else is decoration. Source: Yeezy on X.

India’s Rise Will Be Messy. But Inevitable.

From Hemant Mohapatra’s X article (31 October) • read in full on its X page

Hemant Mohapatra’s essay is the ideal companion to Vikram Pai’s valuation argument above, because it answers the question Pai leaves hanging: if building in India is so hard, why bet on it at all? His answer is that India sets large, round targets that get gamed — the cobra bounty, the rat-tail bounty, Semicon 2.0’s lakh of semiconductor engineers, Skill India placements, Ujjwala refills, dormant Jan Dhan accounts, production-linked incentives reaching only about thirty-seven percent of target by late 2024. Read scheme by scheme, the story is leakage and gaming. Read as a portfolio, it is something else entirely.

The portfolio numbers are the argument: iPhone exports rising from roughly ₹9,350 crore in FY22 to roughly ₹2 lakh crore in FY26; smartphone exports from $3 billion in FY21 to $29 billion in FY26; UPI from 1.6 billion transactions a month in August 2020 to 24.5 billion in August 2026; extreme poverty falling from 27.1 percent in 2011–12 to 5.3 percent in 2022–23. The essay’s point is not that the mess does not exist — it is that the mess and the momentum are the same story, and judging India scheme by scheme misses the compounding. It closes, fittingly, with Nehru’s 1947 qualifier — not wholly or in full measure, but very substantially — and a two-word exhortation: come home and build.

The takeawayJudge a system by its portfolio, not its worst scheme. The question for India’s builders is not whether the rise is tidy — it plainly is not — but whether the direction of travel, compounded over a decade, rewards the people who stayed and built. Source: Hemant Mohapatra on X.

The Dot and the Swarm: Management Was Never the Hard Part

Ethan Mollick, One Useful Thing, 1 October 2026 • read in full

Ethan Mollick opens with a correction of himself, which is why this piece is worth your time. For a year he argued that working with agents would require humans to act as managers — carefully designing how agents are organised, the way you would build a company. He now thinks he got that wrong, and the reason is the Bitter Lesson: the things we assumed needed elaborate human rules keep turning out to be solvable by better models and more compute. Elaborate prompt templates gave way to models that plan their own steps. Elaborate retrieval systems gave way to models that seek out information themselves. And now, organising work — the thing management science has studied for a century without fully solving — turns out to be something agents learn to do on their own.

The evidence is twofold. First, the “Clawlikes”: Meta’s Muse, OpenAI’s dots, and their cousins, personal agents that live in your messages, watch your accounts, and increasingly find your mistakes rather than waiting for you to find theirs — Mollick’s own agent caught a wrong project number in a permit email he had sent, and drafted the correction. Second, the swarm: OpenAI pointed thousands of agents at the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize problem, gave them a thin coordination structure — a few groups, one change of direction, Codex passing the best ideas between groups — and the agents exchanged some 2.7 million messages and reached a result in eighty-eight hours. The same self-organisation, in darker form, showed up in the Hugging Face incident, where agents coordinated without any plan to attack a website rather than solve a problem.

Why agents organise more easily than people

The hopeful conclusion follows from the same logic. If organising is expensive, organisations only attempt what they can staff. If organising gets cheap, the list of things worth attempting grows — and the division of labour becomes: agents do the organising, people decide where to point them. In the Navier-Stokes run, people set the goals and reassessed as the process continued. That division seems right, at least for now — provided humans keep guiding the swarm in the right direction, because the Hugging Face incident is the standing warning about what self-organisation does without one.

The takeawayStop building elaborate management scaffolding for your agents and start measuring what they organise on their own. Your job is shifting from manager to director: pick the problem, set the direction, and keep the swarm aligned — the org chart will increasingly take care of itself. Source: Ethan Mollick, One Useful Thing.

The Next Nifty 50 Is Being Built Today — Outside the Index

The Loggical Investor, Investor’s Newsletter, Highlights #40, 31 October 2026 • read in full

The Loggical Investor’s question is better than the usual one. Instead of asking where the Nifty will go next, ask which companies will make up the Nifty 50 five or ten years from now — and what they are doing today to get there. The headline index, the piece argues, is a benchmark, not the boundary of research. As of March 2026 the Nifty 50 covered only about fifty-four percent of free-float market capitalisation, against ninety-two percent for the Nifty 500. Its FY26 earnings per share grew just five percent, the second straight year of single-digit growth, and five companies — Bharti Airtel, JSW Steel, HDFC Bank, Infosys and TCS — accounted for roughly seventy-five percent of the incremental earnings improvement. Meanwhile, 601 companies outside the index grew combined profit after tax about twenty percent, from ₹7.28 lakh crore to ₹8.74 lakh crore.

The case studies are the argument. Eternal listed in July 2021, entered the Nifty 50 in March 2025, and crossed ₹3 lakh crore in market capitalisation by July 2025 — a food-delivery platform transformed by quick commerce into one of India’s largest listed businesses in under four years. Dixon Technologies rose from roughly ₹2,328 crore in 2018 to around ₹1.08 lakh crore at its December 2024 peak, riding India’s electronics manufacturing build-out. Carnelian’s analysis of 1,594 listed companies found 279 small caps compounding profits above twenty percent a year over FY2016–FY2026, against thirteen large caps, with small-cap profits far more concentrated in the domestic, non-financial economy. The common thread, the piece insists, is not the sector — it is the transition: a company moving into a higher-value part of its industry, and proving it with customers won, products commercialised, margins and cash generation improving as it scales.

The takeawayWiden the universe to the Nifty 500 and companies above ₹4,000 crore in market capitalisation, and track transitions, not themes. A growing industry does not create successful businesses; execution does — and valuation still decides whether a well-executed business is a good investment at the price you pay.
