The Lead Story

Half the Speed of Sound Over the Grand Canyon

Via @elonmusk — with the timeline’s Z-axis chorus
Illustration: the sequel — yesterday’s disbelief becomes today’s demo itinerary. Snake River. Grand Canyon. On its wheels.

Musk doesn’t walk the specs back; he raises them. A day after the flying-Roadster reveal, he posted the sequel himself: “Future fun Roadster demos will include flying over the Snake River or maybe Grand Canyon and landing on its wheels and exceeding half the speed of sound.” The reveal is no longer a launch event. It’s a tour. The question shifted overnight from did it really fly? to where will it fly next? — which is exactly how spectacle marketing is supposed to work.

The numbers need restating because they still sound like a prank. A newly designed car weighing more than 4,000 pounds, doing flips, travelling at 380-plus miles an hour — past half the speed of sound — and landing on its wheels after flying over a canyon. MKBHD’s canonical disbelief stands unchallenged: there is, by his reading, no street tire invented yet that can handle anything near that. And yet the escalation continues, because the escalation is the product moment. Every new impossible detail is another free headline.

The speed ladder of the claim

“Future fun Roadster demos will include flying over the Snake River or maybe Grand Canyon and landing on its wheels and exceeding half the speed of sound.”— @elonmusk

Meanwhile, the Z-axis chorus kept writing the marketing department’s copy for free. Dilip Kumar’s four-word investment thesis: “Never bet against @elonmusk.” Vijay Shekhar Sharma wondered what the traffic-jam demo was even for. Derek Nee reported that traffic is a bit heavy in the Z-axis today. And Alexandr Wang asked the one question an engineer asks while everyone else laughs: “wait who the hell was driving the tesla.”

Main learnings

  • Escalation is the strategy. When the reveal gets mocked, don’t defend the specs — raise them. Disbelief converts to coverage at a fixed rate.
  • The sequel arrives in 24 hours. Musk compressed the launch cycle to a day: reveal, then the demo itinerary, then the memes become canon.
  • Half the speed of sound is the new Z-axis. The Roadster stopped being a car and became a flying-vehicle demo program.
  • The chorus is the distribution layer. Four one-line reactions reached further than the official video.
Practical takeaway — when a founder escalates an impossible claim the day after it’s mocked, don’t ask whether it’s real. Ask why you are still talking about it.

Claude Writes 90% of Claude Code’s Code

Via @Hesamation — quoting @ClaudeDevs
Illustration: the human-in-the-loop is now the rarest resource — the legend writing the other ten percent.

“Claude writes about 90% of Claude Code’s code,” Anthropic’s ClaudeDevs account posted — and the punchline wrote itself: the absolute legend is whoever writes the other ten percent. The numbers underneath are the real story. From June to September, merged pull requests more than doubled, while the share that got reverted fell by more than 40%. Volume up, quality up, humans mostly watching.

The mechanism is the code-review redesign they wrote up on claude.dev: the bottleneck in AI-written code was never generation, it was review. They rebuilt how code gets reviewed — how feedback is routed, what counts as approval, how the model learns from corrections — and then throughput unlocked. The 90% figure gets the headline, but the 40% reverts figure is the one that matters: writing code was the easy part; being right about the code is what the review machine solved.

Anthropic on Claude Code’s own development, June → September
Via @ClaudeDevs (blog: solving-code-review-at-anthropic).

Main learnings

  • Review is the bottleneck, not generation. Throughput doubled only after the review pipeline was redesigned.
  • The 10% is the premium. The human’s remaining share is judgement, taste, and the review design itself.
  • Measure reverts, not volume. Twice the PRs mean nothing without the 40% quality gain.
Practical takeaway — if your team’s AI coding output is stuck, don’t buy a bigger model. Redesign the review loop.

Let the Reset Wars Begin

Via @beffjezos, @thsottiaux — Codex day 12 & 13, Antigravity’s answer

Beff’s joke landed because it’s true: “Google has its own Tibo now. Let the reset wars begin.” Tibo Sottiaux, OpenAI’s Codex lead, has spent his 28-day series announcing limit resets the way musicians announce tour dates. On day 12 he detailed the engineering underneath: reusing context across subagents, keeping context cached longer, and better request routing to increase cache hits — the effective-usage improvements behind the reset. On day 13, the two-word event: “Le limit reset,” landing by end of day Pacific.

And Google answered in kind. Varun Mohan posted that Antigravity was resetting Argon and Gemini model quotas for everyone, telling builders to keep building through the weekend. The pattern is now visible: the coding-agent wars are fought not on model quality but on allowance — who lets you build the most before the meter runs out. Quotas are the pricing lever, resets are the marketing, and the builder’s weekend is the battleground.

“Google has its own Tibo now. Let the reset wars begin.”— @beffjezos

Main learnings

  • Context is the currency. Day 12’s real news is context reuse and caching — the meter only drops when the engineering gets smarter.
  • Resets are marketing events. A quota reset is a product launch that costs nothing and buys a weekend of builders.
  • The war is on allowance, not models. When models are interchangeable, whoever resets first wins the weekend.
Practical takeaway — budget your agent spend like media spend: the reset calendar is the new rate card.

The 17x Cheaper Eye

Via @deedydas — on Elorian’s Glimpse

Deedy’s thesis: “LLMs are either not capable or not affordable for visual reasoning tasks at scale.” The Elorian team — one of Jeff Dean’s best reports who led Gemini, an Apple LLM lead, an xAI lead, and a Harvard professor — just launched Glimpse, described as a visual-thinking model with competitive frontier performance versus Astra on key public visual-reasoning benchmarks, for 17x cheaper. The demo that sold it: Glimpse breaking down the physics of the flying-Tesla video, frame by frame.

The wedge is video inputs at industrial scale — manufacturing lines, robotics, gaming, content moderation — where every frame is a query and frontier prices are prohibitive. Deedy led the seed round with Striker and Altimeter, and it’s already on OpenRouter. The pattern repeats: every frontier capability gets a second act as a commodity input. First text, then code, now the eye.

Frontier visual reasoning, per-token cost
Claimed by Elorian per Deedy’s post; Astra as benchmark.

Main learnings

  • Video is the new text. The frontier moved from reading frames to reasoning about them — and the price collapsed first.
  • Pedigree is the moat signal. A Gemini lead, an Apple LLM lead, an xAI lead, a Harvard professor — teams like this get funded on a paragraph.
  • The demo is the physics. Breaking down the Tesla video’s physics is a better benchmark story than any leaderboard.
Practical takeaway — if your product touches video — moderation, QC, robotics — reprice your assumptions at one-seventeenth.

Reverse-Engineer Anything

Via @zodchiii and @AravSrinivas
Illustration: the closed app, taken apart — one prompt, down to the binary.

Two posts, one stack. darkzodchi’s version: REA, an open-source MCP server, plus Opus 5.5, is “dangerous” — one prompt and your agent reverse-engineers any app, down to the binary, maps unknown repos, rebuilds undocumented APIs. Aravind Srinivas’s version: Perplexity’s Computer has integrated the REA skill, and they’re handing out 10,000 bootstrapping credits so anyone can reverse-engineer an app of their choice — Computer inspects how the app works without its source code and builds a working version.

The reply sections tell the two halves of the story. One user burned 9,000 of the 10,000 credits on the first prompt — the economics of reverse-engineering are still absurd. The others called it a legal minefield. Which is precisely the point: the capability arrived before the pricing and the law. The binary was the last moat. It isn’t anymore.

Main learnings

  • The closed app is open now. Source code was never the moat; it was the inconvenience. That inconvenience is gone.
  • Credits are the new freemium. 10,000 credits that evaporate in one prompt is a marketing number, not an allowance.
  • Law lags the prompt. The capability exists; the case law doesn’t.
Practical takeaway — audit your own product’s binaries against the REA workflow. Whatever your agent finds, someone else’s already did.

From Inference to Execution: AI’s Next Economy

Via @JayaGup10 and Ashu Garg — article, liked & bookmarked

Jaya Gupta’s thesis, co-written with Ashu Garg, names the week’s through-line: the AI economy is shifting from supplying intelligence to executing work. The money moves from selling GPUs and renting models to agents that complete tasks end-to-end — and the power accrues to the agent-assembling companies, which decide the model-and-infrastructure mix underneath. Her bet: owning the most GPUs will no longer guarantee capturing the most value.

The evidence she cites: outcome-based pricing is rising — pay for the result, not the token — and CoreWeave’s Q2 revenue more than doubled to $2.575 billion on heavily financed capacity. The GPU landlords are doing fine; the question is who keeps the margin. When execution is the product, the stack picker — the agent company choosing which model, which cloud, which tool — captures the surplus, and the infra becomes fungible underneath.

Value capture: inference → execution
Thesis via Jaya Gupta & Ashu Garg, ‘From Inference to Execution’.

Main learnings

  • The surplus moved to the assembler. Whoever picks the model/infra mix keeps the margin.
  • Outcome pricing beats token pricing. Customers pay for completed work; token meters become an internal cost.
  • Infra becomes fungible. When agents choose the stack, GPUs are a commodity input — $2.575B quarters notwithstanding.
Practical takeaway — if you sell intelligence, start selling outcomes. The margin is following the result, not the token.

Buy the Dip, On Autopilot

Via @mignano — Supertake’s new feature

Michael Mignano announced Supertake’s new “Buy the Dip”: when one of your take’s bigger positions falls hard below what you paid, Supertake runs deep analysis to check whether the reason you own it still holds. If it does, it draws from the take’s cash reserve and doubles down — within the take’s limits. Autopilot takes do it on their own; otherwise Supertake asks first. Deactivate any time.

The thesis-check is the product. Retail dip-buying fails because the dip is priced before the thesis is rechecked; automating the thesis-check before the order converts a reflex into a discipline. The autopilot distinction is the honest part — autonomy is a tier, not a promise, and the take that opts in has pre-committed to its own rules.

Main learnings

  • Automate the check, not just the order. “Does the thesis still hold?” is the trade; the buy is the paperwork.
  • Autonomy is a tier. Autopilot vs ask-first is the right way to sell agency.
  • Pre-commitment beats discipline. The rules work because they were written before the dip.
Practical takeaway — write your buy-the-dip rules before the dip. Then let the machine follow them.

Play the Bad Guy: A Pre-Launch Security Ritual

Via @Voxyz_ai — liked & bookmarked

Vox’s Claude Code security ritual: before you ship, make Opus 5.5 play the bad guy — three subagents, test accounts, 14 attacks across impersonation, UI-limit bypass, and external-entry groups. Collect the proof, fix everything in one pass, re-test, then install Anthropic’s security-guidance plugin. The prompt is safety-constrained: test accounts and test data only, never production.

The replies worried the attack prompts could trip guardrails or get accounts banned — which is itself the signal: the ritual is powerful enough to scare the platform. Vibe-coded sites ship with vibes for security; this is the first playbook that treats AI-assisted attack as a standard pre-launch gate.

Main learnings

  • Adversarial testing is a gate, not a luxury. Three subagents, 14 attacks, one pass of fixes.
  • Constrain the blast radius. Test accounts and test data only — the prompt says so explicitly.
  • Re-test after the fix. The ritual is collect, fix, re-verify — not collect and hope.
Practical takeaway — add the bad-guy pass to your launch checklist. If it can break your product, better your agents find it than someone else’s.
