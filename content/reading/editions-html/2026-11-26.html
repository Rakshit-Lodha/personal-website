It is the work itself, rendered as something you can touch.

The crossing, step by step — what the 32 seconds actually say

Why this matters to anyone building with agents: the interface question is the moat question. Anthropic, meanwhile, loses the person who best understood that the demo was the product.

The takeawayTalent moves are lagging indicators of strategy, not gossip. Watch what the mover says they are going to build — Thariq told us, in 32 seconds: the artifact as interface. The next year of coding agents will be a fight over that surface.

The Argument · Who Decides?

“I Do Not Believe We Should Surrender Human Free Will” — Dario Amodei Answers Axios, and the Whole Industry Piles In

Dario Amodei · Axios (Madison Mills) · 25 November 2026 · Article read in full

The Axios scoop landed on Tuesday evening: senior figures in AI are increasingly resisting a view held inside Anthropic — that humanity should hand some decision-making to superintelligent systems — and critics warn it amounts to giving up free will. The headline read: “Rival AI leaders push back against Anthropic vision of surrendering human agency to AI.” Eleven hours later, Anthropic’s chief executive answered in a post longer than most essays, calling the characterisation “absurd and malicious” and insisting that everything he has written for years points the other way. The argument is no longer about whether machines might go rogue. It is about whether humans should voluntarily step aside — and it is now being fought in public, by name.

Axios’s reporting, by Madison Mills, is careful about what it claims and blunt about why it matters. Its core: inside Anthropic, a consensus has developed among some employees that humanity will, and in many cases should, safely hand authority to superintelligent systems rather than fight to retain control. The evidence it cites is Amodei’s own public record, assembled across years. In a February New York Times interview he said that in “life-critical, safety-critical cases, we really want to turn it over” to AI. In a CBS discussion that month he argued fully autonomous weapons may eventually be “needed for the defense of democracy”. In November 2024 he suggested the professor–graduate-student relationship could invert, with AI systems acting as principal investigators directing humans. And in Machines of Loving Grace he sketched a future economy in which superintelligent systems distribute resources based on “what the AI systems think makes sense to reward in humans”. There is also a product-vision slide from several years ago, surfaced in litigation, describing Anthropic’s “vision for a better world” as a “utopia ruled by god in a box”.

“We should not be okay with a ‘handover’ of society to AI.”Alexandr Wang, Meta · quoted in the Axios piece

Amodei’s reply does not retreat from the quotes so much as reframe the whole project. His constant theme, he says, across hundreds of pages of essays, is that AI must be governed by legitimate democratic processes — and he names the works: Policy on the AI Exponential, Machines of Loving Grace, The Adolescence of Technology, We Must Pace the Frontier. The argument he makes is older than AI: for millennia, advocates of autocracy have claimed it delivers better, faster, more decisive decisions than messy democracy, and even if that were true, legitimacy and self-determination are not to be sacrificed at the altar of efficiency or security. The same applies word for word, he says, to governance by AI. Machines of Loving Grace has an extended section on using AI to revitalise democracy — aggregating opinions, driving consensus, resolving conflict, finding common ground — with humans remaining the ones making political decisions. The Adolescence of Technology has a whole section fighting AI-driven tyranny, including the danger of AI labs usurping power. “I do not believe that AI should determine human futures,” he writes. “I believe that we should determine human futures, with the help of AI.”

The pushback was not his alone. Meta’s Alexandr Wang wrote that “we should not be okay with a ‘handover’ of society to AI”, reflecting on a coming superintelligence moment after an Anthropic breakthrough on a mathematics problem. OpenAI’s Sam Altman had already rebuked the view earlier in November: “I want people to have the agency over the future.” Dean Ball, OpenAI’s head of strategic futures, told Axios the view is “an extraordinarily common view in AI safety circles” — a “silent consensus”, in his phrase — and added that people do not always think it is good, while some do. Maryland governor Wes Moore, a potential 2028 presidential contender, invoked Genesis on The Axios Show: God gave humanity dominion over the Earth, “not to robots, not to agents”. And Elon Musk, in his own corner of the argument, recently repeated his long-held view that on reaching superintelligence humanity will become “like pet Labradors”.

Where each side actually stands — the spectrum, not the caricature

Step back and the pattern is familiar from every technology that concentrates power: the debate is not really about the machine. It is about the precedent. Amodei’s self-driving-car analogy — once the evidence shows autonomous vehicles are safer, refusing to hand over the wheel becomes irrational, even unethical — is doing a lot of work in his camp’s case. Accept it, and the logic extends smoothly from driving to diagnosis to resource allocation to governance. Reject it, and you need a principle that says some decisions stay human even when humans do them worse. Amodei says he has one: democratic legitimacy. His critics say the record shows the exceptions swallowing the principle, one life-critical case at a time. Axios’s bottom line is the sharpest sentence in the piece: the debate is no longer only about preventing rogue machines from destroying humanity; it is about whether humans should be willing voluntarily to step aside.

There is also a cruder reading, and Axios does not hide it: some AI leaders are privately frustrated by the handover rhetoric because they fear it is fuelling public and political backlash against the technology. That is the tell. When a debate about philosophy starts being fought with press strategy, the philosophy has become a liability — and liabilities get managed, in public, by the people with the most to lose. Amodei’s post is exactly that management: forceful, thorough, and — note the last line, repeated three times in the original — a man who knows the headline is the story now, not the essay. He says he will post a longer piece soon. Read it when it lands; the first draft of this argument is being written in public, in real time.

The takeawayDon’t pick a team; pick a test. Ask of any handover proposal: who decides the list of exceptions, and can they be voted out? If the answer is “the system”, you have your answer about the system.

Flipkart’s Decade Man Is Stepping Down — and the Reaction Says Everything About What He Missed

Entrackr, citing Moneycontrol · Adit Gupta · 25 November 2026 · Article read in full

Flipkart Group chief executive Kalyan Krishnamurthy is likely to step down over the coming weeks, ending a decade-long stint at the Walmart-owned e-commerce company, according to a Moneycontrol report carried by Entrackr. Flipkart has called an emergency town hall for Wednesday afternoon amid speculation around the exit. The news itself is a standard changing-of-the-guard story. The reaction in Rakshit’s saves is not standard at all: “Finally! Good riddance! Kalyan has been the most paper pusher leader Flipkart has ever had. Missed huge tectonic shifts in q comm!”

Krishnamurthy took charge after founders Sachin Bansal and Binny Bansal exited following the sale to Walmart, and has led the company through a decade of growing competition from Amazon and the newer entrant Meesho, expansion into newer categories including food delivery, and the build-out of its quick-commerce offering, Minutes. That is a real record. But read it against the context Entrackr sketches in three short paragraphs and the shape of the criticism sharpens: an IPO delayed, fundraising efforts dragging, a series of senior-level exits — and, looming over all of it, quick commerce, the tectonic shift Adit Gupta says he missed. Minutes now competes with Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart and Zepto in a market that went from side-bet to centre-stage while Flipkart was still organised around the big-box delivery model that made it.

The useful question is not whether one chief executive “missed” quick commerce — plenty of smart people dismissed 10-minute delivery as a burn-rate parlour trick until it wasn’t. The question is what kind of organisation misses a shift like that, and the answer is usually the same one: an organisation optimised for the last war. Flipkart’s machine was built for selection, price and two-day delivery at national scale. Quick commerce is a different machine — dense dark-store networks, tiny baskets, minutes not days — and it rewards a different kind of leadership: fast, local, operationally obsessive. A “paper pusher” is a harsh verdict, but it is a precise description of a failure mode: process excellence in a game that changed to speed. The emergency town hall will tell the staff what the press release will not: whether the next chief is hired to defend the old machine or to build the new one.

The takeawayEvery incumbent gets one free pass on a tectonic shift; the second one is the career. Flipkart’s next CEO is not being hired to run e-commerce. They are being hired to win a street fight in 10-minute increments.

Three Inflection Points in Twelve Months — and the Year Coding Changed Beyond Recognition

Simon Willison · 24 November 2026

Today is the one-year anniversary of the release of Claude Opus 4.5, and Simon Willison’s keynote at the AI Engineer Code Summit is built around a simple claim: there have been three significant inflection points in the abilities of LLMs and coding agents in the past twelve months — and each one changed what it felt like to build software. Read together, they are the cleanest timeline of the year this paper has been covering story by story. The tools did not get a little better. They crossed three separate lines, and each crossing made the previous way of working feel obsolete within weeks.

Inflection one: November 2025 — the agent becomes reliable. With hindsight, Opus 4.5 was a consequential model. Anthropic’s announcement quoted testers saying it handled ambiguity and reasoned about trade-offs without hand-holding, and figured out fixes for complex, multi-system bugs that were near-impossible for Sonnet 4.5 weeks earlier. Willison’s own test is blunter: it was the first model he used with Claude Code — nine months old at that point — where the default result was generally the right thing. Before that he had to correct it often enough that it did not feel like a clear accelerant; after it, for most daily coding problems, Opus would get them right, and it was clearly faster than working through the problems himself. OpenAI’s GPT-5.1 family landed the same month — Instant, Thinking, Codex and Codex-Max — with a similar effect on Codex. The timing was convenient: December was for tinkering, and by January people were using the tools for their actual work. That is the shape of an inflection point — not a launch, but a December of play turning into a January of production.

Inflection two: June and July — the Fable class. The first hint came in April, when Anthropic announced Claude Mythos and said it was too dangerous to release, because of its improved cybersecurity capabilities: a model that could not just find vulnerabilities but figure out how to exploit them. In June came Claude Fable 5 — a defanged Mythos, which would solve most problems but actively block security work. After three glorious days, the US government took it away under export controls; it would not come back until 1 July. Willison’s definition of the class it introduced is worth memorising: if you can define a goal and provide the necessary tools, they can solve your problem with brute force. His examples are the kind that make you laugh and then go quiet. Asked to fix a display glitch in Safari, Fable spun up its own screenshot mechanism and a CORS-enabled localhost logging server to resolve the bug. Given five WebAudio microphones in a room full of boxes, Opus 5.5 and then Fable 5.2 triangulated the source of an infuriating mysterious beep to within about forty centimetres. “Give it a goal, give it the right tools, and watch it cook.” He currently classes Fable 5, 5.1, 5.2 and Opus 5.5 — “cheap Fable” — in the class, plus GPT-6 Astra, and thinks the new Gemini 4 Neon may count as well. These are now the baseline for what he expects from a frontier model.

The three crossings — one year, three lines

Inflection three: September and October — space, and fun. Over four months, frontier models got really good at 3D modelling: set them up with Blender or ask for Three.js code and they produce credible models of almost anything. Willison first noticed it with Fable 5, but it kicked into gear with Opus 5.5, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra and most recently the Gemini 4 family. The reason it matters is not the models: 3D spatial awareness is crucial if you want vision LLMs for robotics, and nobody is sure whether the modelling skill is a side-effect of the labs focusing on robotics or vice versa. Then came the stranger crossing. Back in August he had Fable 5 build him a raccoon heist game: it had 3D graphics, looked like a game, and played really poorly. GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra did a much better job, but the game was still dull after ninety seconds. He was relieved — designing a game that was fun appeared to remain a uniquely human pursuit, and he used that line in a September talk to reassure people humans still had a role. Then he told Opus 5.5: “This game isn’t fun enough yet. Make it a LOT more fun.” And it worked. His Command & Conquer-style real-time strategy game went from boring to deeply entertaining overnight, after Opus spent twelve hours playtesting new features and rebalancing gameplay. Opus 5.5, released 22 September, was the first model he had used that appeared to understand fun — and since then, at two different conferences, people have shown him game projects that really did feel like proper, worthwhile games. The indie scene has descended into a full-blown culture war over AI-assisted programming, and plenty of people are calling GTA VI the last pre-generative-AI blockbuster.

And then the open question, which is the most interesting paragraph in the piece. Willison is still deciding whether Fable 5.2 is itself a fourth inflection point or just another incremental improvement — but his experience points one way: “I’m getting so much done with that model right now!” It seems to have taste that almost exactly matches his own. He has been burning through gnarly problems he has been thinking about, or procrastinating on, for several years. The release notes for Datasette and his LLM tool — a flood of fixes, features and improvements, all in the last three weeks — are the receipts. If it is an inflection point, the key feature seems to be this: if you can imagine and then clearly describe a piece of software, Fable can build it. His closing line is the quietest detonation in the piece: “Software development has transformed beyond recognition in the last year. I don’t think I’ll ever go back to typing most of my code by hand.”

“If you can imagine and then clearly describe a piece of software, Fable can build it.”Simon Willison, on Fable 5.2

Read this against the Pragmatic Engineer essays above and the two pieces complete each other. Willison is describing the capability curve; the essayists are describing what happened to the humans standing on it. The capability story is euphoric — a beep found to forty centimetres, a game made fun overnight, a year’s procrastination cleared in three weeks. The human story is the tax on that euphoria: review collapsing, identity wobbling, the shift from rowing to steering. Both are true at once, and the people who will do best next year are the ones who can hold both — using the Fable-class leverage while refusing to let the understanding go to zero.

The takeawayInflection points are only visible in hindsight, and Willison has just drawn the map: reliability, then brute-force goal-seeking, then taste. If your workflow still assumes the first line, you are a year behind — and the fourth line may already be here.

The Year AI Wrote All the Code — Told by the Engineers Who Lived It

The Pragmatic Engineer · Gergely Orosz · 24 November 2026

To mark its fifth birthday, The Pragmatic Engineer ran an essay competition on how building software has changed since AI models got good enough to write most of the code. The grand prize — $10,000 — went to Neil Kakkar’s ‘How software engineering changed: a tale of two eras’. Six category winners took $2,500 each. This week’s instalment shares excerpts from three categories, with links to forty more essays, and the picture that emerges is refreshingly honest and raw: output exploded, review collapsed, and the job changed underneath everyone while they were still doing it.

The winning essay in the first category — what happens when AI writes all the code — is Anton Zaides’ ‘An engineering manager’s crazy year’, from HoneyBook, and it reads like a confession. It opens on a June afternoon in 2026: a 10,000-line pull request from the team’s designer, full of hacky solutions and dummy data where backend calls should have been, and an engineering manager wondering how he got there. The answer is a year-long slide. The team split work across three repositories with three different review regimes: the shared AI-workflows codebase got two reviewers per PR; the thin frontend layer, atlas-vibe, barely had PRs at all; the backend made reviews optional. Slowly it became tempting not to ask for reviews — “addicting”, Zaides calls it — to ship a feature and have it in production immediately, with no open end in the brain. The peer pressure that used to force understanding evaporated: when you know nobody will review your code, it is much easier to cut corners in your own understanding too. In a couple of months the team went from two people reviewing and understanding the code to zero. The result was what you would expect: a buggy codebase full of duplication and non-optimal patterns.

“We are turning into software machinists.”Paul D’Ambra, PostHog · in his essay-challenge entry

And yet the output numbers are staggering. Zaides went from averaging one PR a week from last October to March, to more than twenty a week now — and the nature of the work changed with the volume: small side features gave way to serious work, six to eight parallel sessions running at once, multiple big features a day on meeting-free Tuesdays and Thursdays. His conclusion is the one managers least want to hear and most need to: to be a good engineering manager today there is “absolutely no other way than to join the building activity”. He got rid of tedious work and feels ten times more productive hands-on — but only because he refused the ‘I don’t know, Claude wrote it’ approach. “Even if you don’t request a review from someone, it’s still your responsibility to read and understand it. You can’t let Claude make the decisions and ‘drive’ the work.”

The runners-up sharpen the picture from different angles. Paul D’Ambra, an engineer at PostHog, supplies the metaphor that will stick: software engineers are not turning into AI engineers; they are turning into software machinists — replacing handmade software with increasingly cheap, plentiful machine-made software, building tooling, inspecting samples, producing in large numbers. His predictions: continuous integration and review change next, and “we make tools for our workshop”. Kira Howe, a staff engineer at Seeq, admits she was unimpressed by ChatGPT in the early days and found the hype insufferable — then concluded she had to “either accept my AI-centric fate and learn how to use them well, or leave the industry”. Marc-André Moreau, CTO at the bootstrapped Canadian company Devolutions, tells the most operational story: a company-wide AI-native push with a voluntary departure package for anyone who did not want to be part of “Devolutions 2.0”, and a migration originally estimated at three years completed in under one for Windows and Linux — a 1,500-screen application, a hackathon that produced sixty useful skills, and AI spending that grew to $2 million a year. Carlo Beschi, a lead agile coach at Treatwell, lists sixteen changes including the “80/20 rule” — 80% of the work is easy to get done alone with AI, people are needed for the final 20% — roles blurring as developers work outside their domains and PMs write more code, and a quiet mourning for the craft: many engineers are having a hard time accepting that AI is taking over what they love, writing well-crafted code. Bruno Felix, CTO of the eight-person startup XEO360, names the transition: the engineer’s main activity is shifting from rowing — focusing on one thing at a time and producing good code — to steering — managing multiple workstreams, context-switching all day, exercising judgement throughout. “That is mentally taxing,” he writes, “and at times at odds with the skills, and sometimes the identities, we’ve built over the years.”

One team’s year, in numbers — Anton Zaides, HoneyBook

The third category — reflections on AI, won by Jacob Bartlett’s ‘The new core skills: agentic engineering at Granola’ — and the second — solo builders, won by David Veszelovszki’s ‘I’m a 100x engineer now!’ — point the same way: agents can write the code, so the human’s job is to understand the system, stay in control of it, and shape it. That is the through-line of all forty essays, and the reason this collection is worth an hour rather than a skim. Every one of these writers is describing the same transition from a different chair — manager, staff engineer, CTO, solo builder — and they all land on the same uncomfortable fact: the leverage is real, the understanding is optional, and the teams that treat understanding as optional are the ones shipping the bugs.

The takeawayRowing to steering is not a promotion; it is a different job, and it is mentally taxing. If your team’s review count has quietly gone to zero while output went up twenty-fold, you have not automated engineering — you have automated the part that kept it honest.

The Dose: “This Is Just How We Do Math and Physics Now”

Not Boring · Packy McCormick · Weekly Dose of Optimism #217 · 30 October 2026

Some weeks the Dose is a roundup; this one is a status report on a new way of doing science. OpenAI announced three new discoveries in mathematics, computer science and physics. In maths, its models produced a proof of Christopher Sogge’s 1991 local smoothing conjecture in every dimension — which would also settle the Kakeya conjecture and Stein’s restriction conjecture. For scale: Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl proving Kakeya in three dimensions was one of the biggest maths stories of 2025. In computer science, a model refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis, the widely held belief that problems like 3-SAT cannot be solved meaningfully faster than brute force. In physics, the models produced a rigorous proof that interacting quantum field theories, including QCD and pure Yang–Mills, exist in four dimensions — half of a Millennium Prize problem. Packy’s verdict is the headline of this story: this is just how we do maths and physics now.

What is different this time is not the results but the process. When OpenAI previously dropped solutions on the internet’s head and walked away, there were quasi-embarrassing episodes — the claim that GPT-5 had solved ten open Erdős problems turned out to rest mostly on solutions already sitting in the literature. This time the outputs are being shared as research data with experts Packy calls “shepherds” in each field, who choose what to do with the data and where and when to publish. Shepherds do not sign NDAs, do not get paid, and do get the credit for explaining the work. Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi — who proposed the Exponential Time Hypothesis — are leading the review of its disproof with MIT’s Ryan Williams. Nobel laureate David Gross is leading the physics group, which chose to withhold the AI material and write it up themselves. The maths proof and the ETH disproof are on GitHub with Lean formalisations: Lean guarantees the proof proves the formal statement, and the human shepherds still have to confirm the formal statement is the right one. It is, in other words, a division of labour — machines prove, humans vouch — and it is becoming the standard pipeline.

The same week, Axiom announced a complete Lean auto-formalisation of the Classification of Finite Simple Groups — the “periodic table of symmetry”, a proof of about 15,000 pages written by more than a hundred mathematicians over several decades, so big no single person has ever checked all of it. For scale, it took Georges Gonthier’s team six years to formalise just one piece of it, the Feit–Thompson theorem, back in 2012. Axiom’s machines can do a lot of human-years quickly — and they will need to, to keep up with the maths and physics coming out of the labs. A physics neolab, Physical Superintelligence, released results on five problems from IQOQI Vienna’s list of open quantum problems the same week, each with a paper, a Lean formalisation and open-source verification code. One of them asked whether vacuum correlations can violate the CHSH Bell inequality at arbitrarily large separations. Machines did it. Find a new hobby.

The longest section of the Dose, though, is not about maths. It is about drugs — and it is the clearest picture yet of what it looks like when an AI lab stops selling a model and starts running the process. Anthropic’s life sciences team has spent months partnering with six organisations to run drug programmes of its own, because, as Packy explains, discovering molecules is the easy part: the bottlenecks are downstream, in development and getting drugs to market. So Claude is now working the whole pipeline. At the discovery end, Divergence Labs announced positive results in food allergies: Claude turned the goal into a design plan and, largely on its own, generated about 35,000 antibody designs in a month; three early hits dislodged IgE — the trigger for allergic reactions — better than the standard medication. Generate Biomedicines used Claude Code to redesign part of CAR-T, the therapy that uses the body’s own immune cells to hunt cancer, so it could eventually work on solid tumours: one design was about 70% shorter than the standard version and got cells to make roughly ten times as many cancer-spotting receptors. At BigHat Biosciences, Claude proposed improvements to two antibody fragments and BigHat’s automated lab made and tested about 1,010 variants in ten weeks; the best bound more than ten times as tightly, and along the way Claude and an Anthropic researcher figured out that the standard stability metrics were misleading and proposed better ones. Then the paperwork: at Enveda, where manufacturing scientists spend up to 75% of their time on it, Claude reviewed 1,061 pages of manufacturing records, caught all of the issues human reviewers had found plus seven they had missed, cut review time by 89%, and drafted a regulatory document in four hours that takes about three months by hand. Bristol Myers Squibb is using Claude to assess early-stage drug plans and estimate their odds of success. The Column Group is launching Pleiades, a company built to run many more drug programmes with Claude than a small team normally could. And in July, Anthropic acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person company whose founder used Claude to run experiments across eleven outside labs and took a treatment for a rare muscular dystrophy from idea to promising results in mice in under eighteen months. In one cohort of 42 kids with the severe form of the disease, only one ever walked on their own. If the treatment holds up, human trials could start as soon as late 2028. Anthropic is upfront that nearly all of this is preclinical — most of these drugs have not been tested in people yet — but it showed speedups in every part of the process, and the tricky thing about complex processes is that you have to improve the whole thing at once, lest any one unimproved part slow the whole thing down.

Claude across the drug pipeline — the same model, five different jobs

Two more items, because the week refused to stop. An AI agent performed brain surgery on a mouse: given nineteen gene-therapy vectors in a spreadsheet, a mouse model of epilepsy, a motorised surgery rig with cameras and one goal — reduce seizures — it picked the drug, picked the brain region, and did the operation. And Neuralink released early numbers from VOICE, its system for restoring speech: one participant hit 75 words per minute with a 5.8% error rate across a 125,000-word vocabulary in their first week with the implant, against 62 words per minute with nearly a quarter of words wrong for the best published result on the same vocabulary size, Stanford’s 2023 system. Natural conversation runs around 160 words per minute, so the field is about halfway there — and the system can speak in the participant’s pre-ALS voice. Elon Musk also announced that Blindsight, the product that will restore sight to the blind, is ready engineering-wise and waiting on regulatory approval. Age of miracles, pending paperwork. Elsewhere in the Dose: Lilly’s Retatrutide — the triple-hormone shot that mimics GLP-1, GIP and glucagon — now seems to work on sleep apnea too, cutting breathing stoppages by 34 events an hour against 10 on placebo in 80-week Phase 3 trials, on top of diabetes, heart, knee and liver wins; and Anduril announced BlueChip, its own Made in America computer, on Palmer Luckey’s principle that “if you don’t control your technology, you can’t control your future”.

The takeawayThe week’s real story is institutional, not technical: shepherds for maths proofs, a lab running drug programmes end to end, a mouse operated on by an agent. The models are becoming infrastructure for whole processes — and the winners will be the ones who redesign the process, not the ones who rent the model.
