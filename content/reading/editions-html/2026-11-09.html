The Affair That Became a National Security Story

The Nvidia files, as saved • New York Times & Financial Times reporting, Nvidia’s board statement, and the timeline’s verdict • 8–9 November 2026

On Sunday, the chief executive of the most important company in the AI race sent an email to every employee apologising for cheating on his wife of 41 years. Hours later, Nvidia’s board opened an independent investigation into whether Jensen Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence operation. The woman at the centre of it, Alice Huang — no relation — is, according to US intelligence officials cited by the New York Times and the Financial Times, an asset of China’s Ministry of State Security, recruited to gather information on the Nvidia chief and potentially to influence the company’s push to sell its most advanced chips to China. It is the strangest story of the year in tech: a love affair, a spy allegation, a $4.8 billion chip trail, and a board that says it still has confidence in its CEO.

Start with what is established, because the timeline spent the weekend blurring it. The New York Times reported on 29 October that Huang had developed a close relationship with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore company that rents computing power from Nvidia chips to Alibaba and other Chinese firms. US agencies conducting electronic surveillance of her concluded she was passing information to Beijing; a classified memo that circulated among top US officials this spring, overseen by Secretary of State Marco Rubio, described her as an asset of China’s main spy agency. Her company’s Malaysian sister firm bought at least $4.8 billion worth of advanced AI equipment containing Nvidia chips between June 2024 and February 2026. Singapore police have confirmed a separate, ongoing investigation into Megaspeed, and the US Commerce Department has referred the Megaspeed case to the Justice Department. None of that is a finding that secrets were taken. It is the reporting that forced the board’s hand.

What Huang actually admitted is narrower than the headlines, and the distinction matters. His staff memo, posted by Nvidia on Sunday, says: “I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry.” It does not name Alice Huang, does not confirm the reported relationship with her, and does not concede that any information passed to a foreign service. The board’s statement is equally careful: a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside independent counsel, will examine all available information, while the board has “no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised” and retains confidence in Huang. He remains CEO, says he will cooperate fully, and asked for privacy for his family. Three Republican House committee chairs — Foreign Affairs’ Brian Mast, the Select Committee on China’s John Moolenaar, and Intelligence’s Rick Crawford — have asked him to attend a closed-door briefing on 18 November and to respond by 11 November.

The timeline’s reaction split into three camps, and the split is itself the story. The first camp treated it as pure governance: Matt Van Swol’s long explainer walked through the chain — a suspected intelligence asset close to the CEO, a chip deal, a president reportedly warned while Huang was not, a confession, an investigation, a company insisting everything is fine — and called it “one of the biggest national security stories in years”, because Nvidia’s chips are the backbone of the global AI race and Washington has spent years trying to keep them from China while Huang has pushed back against the restrictions. The second camp treated it as a leadership question: Sequoia’s Shaun Maguire posted five points — Jensen is one of the greatest entrepreneurs of this generation; he did something incredibly dumb; it was bad for America; his guess is there was no bad intention; and Jensen is irreplaceable for America’s AI leadership — and Elon Musk replied, simply, “I agree.” The third camp reached for gallows humour: Nikita Bier wrote that the fact he had only one extramarital affair with a state asset, after leading the greatest technological revolution in history, is what impresses him — “for anyone else that number would be double, even triple, digits.” A Chinese-language post that circulated widely put the human geometry most sharply: to the world, Huang is leather jackets, chips and a trillion dollars; to his wife Lori, who met him when he was just a student in a university lab, he has no aura at all — “today, Lori is just a grain of rice stuck to Old Huang’s leather jacket, while Alice Huang is the cinnabar mole on his heart.”

There is also the part nobody on the timeline could answer, and it is the part that will decide whether this is a scandal or a catastrophe: what, if anything, actually crossed the line. Bloomberg’s Emily Chang, who followed Huang across Europe last month for an upcoming episode of The Circuit, released unreleased footage of an interview filmed less than a month ago in which Huang defended engaging with China and criticised US chip restrictions — the position he has held publicly for years, and the position that now reads very differently. If the relationship was, as Huang told Bloomberg, routine contact with a customer, the board inquiry closes with embarrassment and a warning. If information moved, it becomes the most consequential counter-intelligence failure in the AI era, because the asset at stake is not a document but the man who decides who gets the chips. That is why the board’s phrasing — “no reason to believe” — is doing so much work: it is a statement about the evidence today, not a verdict on what the inquiry will find.

The takeawaySeparate the three questions and answer them in order: what he admitted (infidelity, in his own words), what is alleged (espionage, reported by the Times and the FT, not found by anyone), and what is at stake (the chips that run the AI race). The board inquiry exists because the second question cannot be left to the timeline — and because, in the AI race, the CEO’s judgment is itself a national asset.

The Frontier Is Flat. The Founders Are Jagged.

Hemant Mohapatra (Lightspeed India) • “Jagged Frontier, Jagged Founders, Flat Financiers”, an X long-form article read in full • 7–8 November 2026

Two years ago, Andrej Karpathy called LLMs “jagged intelligence”: a model that solves olympiad maths and then tells you 9.11 is bigger than 9.9. Hemant Mohapatra’s argument is that the jaggedness has moved. Today’s frontier models are no longer jagged — they are flat, roughly as good at everything at once: writing, coding, maths, reasoning, science. What is jagged now is the founder. “The frontier is flat, the founders are jagged. The money had better learn to hill climb.” His exhibit: a dozen of India’s youngest deep-tech founders, twelve of them backed before they could legally drink, who have raised about $340 million between them, much of it in the last eighteen months.

The chart in the post: “Backed before they graduated”

The three examples Mohapatra opens with are deliberately extreme. Shreyans Jain started Celestial Aerospace at 16, dropped out of BITS Pilani after his first year, took his first cheque from Lightspeed at 16, and now builds engines that will be tested in space. Naman Pushp started Airbound at 19 and has raised more than $50 million to build drones that can carry parcels the length of India; his cofounder Yash is 18 and also dropped out of BITS Pilani. Anirudh Sharma started Digantara at 20, raised $64.5 million, and is building what Mohapatra calls a Google Maps for space traffic — more money, he notes, than any other space-tech company in India has raised, and he is only twenty. Awais Ahmed started Pixxel at 20, raised a $24 million Series B at 21, and later the largest round by an Indian space-tech company, a $36 million round led by Google; his company sits at $195 million raised in the chart. The rest of the roll call reads the same way: solid rocket motors, AI-native supercomputers, a platform for physical AI, an AI tutor for K-12, a computational-biology platform, a computational platform for vaccine design. “They are not building these companies because they want to be founders,” Mohapatra writes. “They are building them because they cannot not build them.”

His definition of jaggedness is doing the heavy lifting, and it is worth stating precisely, because it is not “young” and it is not “smart”. A jagged founder is spiky rather than well-rounded: deep in one domain, obsessed with one problem, with a very specific and unusual set of skills, and no polish. Four reasons he thinks they win in this regime: no baggage — nothing to unlearn, no mental models to discard, no best practices to obey, so they think from first principles; high risk tolerance — nothing to lose, no career or reputation to protect; a long time horizon — decades ahead, so they can build things that take a long time; and nativity to the technology — they do not see AI as a tool, they see it as a way of thinking. Why India’s version is special, he says, is talent density, an ecosystem at a special moment where a generation that grew up with the internet and smartphones is not waiting for permission, and the fact that India’s hard problems are the world’s problems: “if you can build in India, you can build anywhere in the world.” Many spent their teens building rockets, drones, satellites and robots. They are building in India because they want to, for the world, not only for India.

The second half of the article is the indictment, aimed at his own profession. “The investors need to catch up. The money has not yet caught up with the founders. The founders are more jagged than ever, and the investors are still flat.” Four things investors must do: write bigger cheques earlier (“If you are not writing a $2M cheque at the seed stage, you are not in the game”); move faster (three months to decide is too late); be more comfortable with risk, including backing a 17-year-old dropout; and understand the technology deeply, because without that you cannot evaluate the company at all. The article’s own replies supplied the first test of the thesis in real time: Soham Sankaran, whose PopVax the chart listed as “Grants, ~$5.7m”, replied that the company had received no grants at all, but more than $20 million in research contracts from Vitalik Buterin’s Balvi, the Gates Foundation and Ren Philanthropy, plus a $2 million prize from the US Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority and $7.5 million in equity — and, pointedly, “notably, nothing from Indian VCs.” Mohapatra replied: “Thanks Soham, fixing up the article!” A founder correcting his own investor’s chart in public, with better numbers, is either a sign the article is wrong in its details or a sign the thesis is exactly right. The jagged founders, it turns out, keep better records than the flat financiers.

The takeawayWhen the model is flat — equally expert at everything — the edge moves to the human who is not. Obsession, not breadth, is the moat. And if you are the one writing cheques, a 17-year-old who dropped out to build rocket engines is not a risk to manage; he is the market telling you your process is too slow.

The Full-Text iOS App Playbook: Xcode, But Inside Claude Code

Greg Isenberg • Full text of the post, read in full • 8 November 2026

The trigger for this one is a product decision from Apple that most people missed: Apple put Xcode inside Claude Code, as an official plugin. Greg Isenberg’s post is the operating manual for what that unlocks, end to end — find the idea, validate it, build it, monetise it, rank it, grow it — and his thesis is that the economics of iOS apps have flipped: “welcome to the golden age of ios apps, because now anyone can build one.” The playbook is specific enough to execute, which is why it is here in full.

1. FindAppfigures or Sensor Tower for category revenue; filter rating below 3.5, no update in 6+ months, keywords low difficulty but high revenue; check “you might also like”; ideabrowser to find the winning category.

2. ValidateApp-store-scraper to cluster 1–2 star reviews; look for complaints about the product, not the price; search Reddit for “alternative to”; if you can name the fix in one line, it’s a real gap.

3. BuildClaude Code + Apple’s Xcode plugin; XcodeBuildMCP for building and testing; ios-simulator-mcp; write the top 5 complaints into a skill; Apple Foundation Models on-device, $0 inference; App Intents; import button; Maestro tests; Sentry; TelemetryDeck; TestFlight with 20 people.
→
4. MonetiseRevenueCat plus Superwall; paywall right after the first win, not the first open; annual as default with a 3-day trial; weekly as anchor; win-back flow for cancellers.
→
5. Rank & growFastlane snapshot + frameit for screenshots that answer the complaints; localise to 10 languages; ask for a review right after a win; 10 TikTok creators; fresh accounts; Apify TikTok scraper hook bank; 3 hooks a day in CapCut; Arcads or HeyGen UGC; Postiz; faceless slideshows 3× a day; Spark Ads; Apple offer codes; AppsFlyer; Apple Ads on competitor names.

Two details are worth pulling out of the list. First, the validation rule: cluster the 1–2 star reviews and look for complaints about the product rather than the price. A price complaint means the product works and people want it cheaper; a product complaint means the incumbent is vulnerable. Second, the monetisation rule: put the paywall after the first win, not the first open. Let the user feel the value before you ask for money, then make annual the default with a 3-day trial and weekly the anchor — the anchor exists to make annual look like the sensible choice, not because weekly is the product you want to sell. And the build step is where the Apple plugin matters most: on-device Foundation Models mean inference costs $0, App Intents mean your app can be invoked by Siri and Shortcuts, and the simulator MCP means the agent can see the app the way a user does. Isenberg’s framing is that this is the moment the constraint on indie apps stops being “can you code” and becomes “can you find a gap and ship into it”. The tools are the commodity now; the taste is the job.

The takeawayThe cheapest validation on the App Store is a one-star review of someone else’s app. Find a product with angry users and no updates in six months, build the one-line fix with an agent, and put the paywall after the first win. Apple just removed the last excuse.

Deepinder Goyal’s Diwali Defence of Temple

Deepinder Goyal • Ten points, in his own words • 8 November 2026

This is the third day of the Temple saga in these pages — the lead of Edition 44, the follow-up in Edition 45 — and Deepinder Goyal used Diwali to put his fullest case on the record. Ten points, and they are worth reading as a single argument rather than a thread: Temple was never made for India. It is a health-tech company building a brain-imaging device, three years in the making, that pivoted from building a better MRI to something entirely new. It is hardware-first, priced in the thousands because it cannot yet be manufactured at scale, with peer-reviewed research and open science behind it, and the full results come in the next few weeks. Brain Flow, the feature everyone has been arguing about, is not the main product. High-resolution brain imaging is. And the company he is building is not a product launch; it is a decades-long project in mental health and neurotechnology, because the brain is the most important organ in the body and the one we understand least.

Two smaller posts complete the picture. The first is a correction with teeth: in a post that reads as a reply to a specific claim, Goyal wrote, “Please correct yourself. He is not an investor in Temple. I haven’t met him for years.” The second is from Rajan Anandan, who posted a photo from Delhi with the caption “#Temple in Delhi” — one of the two men in the frame wearing the device on his temple, the thing itself moving out of the demo room and onto a person in the capital. The through-line of the three days is consistent: the product is real, it is expensive because it is unscaled hardware, it is not for the Indian market at Indian prices, and the argument about it is really an argument about whether a founder who built Zomato is allowed to build something the country cannot yet afford. Goyal’s answer, across all three days, is that he is not building it for the country; he is building it for the world, with deep discounts for India, and the results will be published. The next few weeks will tell whether the science lands.

The takeawayRead the ten points as one sentence: a health-tech brain-imaging device, three years in, hardware-first, expensive because it is unscaled, with peer-reviewed results coming in weeks — and Brain Flow is the distraction, not the product. Judge it when the data is published, not when the price is quoted.

Pavel Durov: “You Gain Energy by Doing Something”

Beyond The Veil • 1 minute 20 seconds of video, transcribed in full • Carried over from 7 November, resolved today

This clip has been sitting in the pile since 7 November, waiting for its words. Today it got them. Pavel Durov, asked about depression, says he does not remember having it in the last twenty years at least — and the reason, he says, is mechanical, not motivational. “I start doing things, identify the problem, I can see a solution and I start executing the strategy. If you are stuck in this loop of being worried about something, nothing’s ever going to change.” The mistake, he says, is the rest-and-recover model: “people often make this mistake thinking, oh I should just have some rest and then regain energy. This is not how it works, you gain energy by doing something, so you start doing something, then it happens, you feel motivated, you feel inspired.”

The mechanism he describes is compounding in miniature: do something, then a little bit more, then a little bit more, and “then a few years, you may end up achieving great things.” His example is the gym: there are many days when you do not want to start working out, but you have to overcome the initial reluctance, and then you get to a point that you enjoy it and think, oh my god, it was such a good idea to come to the gym today. The replies under the clip put the same idea in plainer language: “Movement breaks the loop because even a wrong first step hands you new information” and “Money just buys a nicer room to be depressed in.” It is a simple rule, and it is the opposite of the advice the internet usually gives, which is to wait until you feel ready. Durov’s version is that readiness is the product of starting, not the precondition for it.

“You gain energy by doing something, so you start doing something, then it happens, you feel motivated, you feel inspired.”— Pavel Durov
The takeawayDo not wait to feel motivated and then act. Act, and let the motivation be the by-product. The first step does not have to be right; it has to be taken.

A Touchscreen MacBook, a Glowing Logo, and an iPad Mini That Costs More Than It Should

HTX Studio • 17 minutes of video, transcribed in full • Carried over from 5 November, resolved today

Apple has done the thing Steve Jobs said, sixteen years ago, would never work: it put a touchscreen on a MacBook Pro. The review that has been waiting in the pile since 5 November is the most thorough teardown of the decision anywhere — seventeen minutes, an X-ray of the glowing logo, benchmarks against the previous generation and against a $7,000 Windows gaming laptop, and a second product, the iPad mini 8, reviewed in the same breath. The verdict on the touchscreen is blunt: “using it is awkward, tapping the screen, making wobble, holding your arm for so long, is tiring, and most importantly, fingerprints.” Apple has not fixed the problems with touchscreen laptops, the reviewer says; it has softened them.

The history matters here, because Apple was not merely late to touchscreens; it was ideologically opposed. Jobs called them ergonomically terrible. Craig Federighi, asked in 2021 whether Macs would ever get touchscreens like Windows laptops, said the iPad was built for touch while the Mac was built for interact controls, and “we don’t think that needs to change.” Ten years ago, when everyone else added touch, Apple made the Touch Bar and kept touch off the screen itself. Until now. What has changed is not the ergonomics but the framing: tap the menu bar and the options spread further apart than a mouse click; small buttons expand when touched; the hinge is stiffer to reduce wobble; software steadies the front camera during video calls so tapping does not shake the image. But the tired arm is unsolved, most apps still have tiny buttons that are not built for fingers, and the reviewer’s only genuinely useful case is testing your own app by touching the screen, which is closer to how users will actually use it. Apple itself, he notes, said gestures on the screen feel as intuitive as on a trackpad — which invites the obvious question: then why not just use the trackpad?

The numbers that matter

Everything else about the machine is the real story, and the touchscreen is the distraction. The display is tandem OLED, the technology already in the iPad Pro, stacking two light-emitting layers: HDR peaks at 2,100 nits with a window covering about 40% of the screen, up from 1,600 on the old model, and the difference that matters is not brightness — eyes do not perceive brightness linearly — but the end of blooming, the halo around bright spots in dark scenes, because each OLED pixel makes its own light and turns off where it should be black. The cost is 240Hz PWM dimming, a low flicker frequency that some people find tiring. The glowing logo is back after nine years, and the engineering is a small delight: the old logo glowed because the LCD backlight shone through a hole cut in the lid, which also meant a bright light at the back could pass through to the screen; OLED has no backlight, so Apple uses a separate circuit, confirmed by X-ray, and when the logo is off it is a mirror. The notch is gone, replaced by a Dynamic Island that shows live updates like music and timers. The 16-inch model is 0.7 pounds lighter. The M5 Max scores 68% higher in CPU performance than the previous generation in the reviewer’s benchmarks, and in a Blender test the M5 Pro scored nine times the previous Pro. The cooling holds: with both CPU and GPU at full load, both generations reach 49 degrees Celsius on the surface and draw 95 watts, and the new one runs two degrees cooler at 13% higher power. And for the AI-curious, the unified memory lets you run local models — a 4-bit Qwen 3.8 at 42 tokens a second, decent for simple questions, clearly behind a cloud model on anything hard, and one test, animating a pigeon riding a bicycle, took 52 minutes of thinking and produced a pigeon with misaligned legs. “Let’s just say it tried its best.”

The iPad mini 8, reviewed in the same video, is the sharper disappointment. The screen grows from 8.3 to 8.5 inches, switches to OLED — manual brightness from 460 to 600 nits, much higher contrast, and the end of the jelly-scrolling effect that plagued the old model in portrait — and then stops at 60Hz, in 2026, on an OLED screen. The reviewer’s explanation is cynical and probably right: ProMotion is the iPad Pro’s last screen feature, and the mini is not allowed to steal it. Everything else is genuinely better: the A20 Pro is 49% faster in single core and 59% in multi-core than the A17 Pro, with nearly three times the graphics performance; Death Stranding runs at 40 frames per second where the old mini managed 18; battery life in heavy use is 4 hours 38 minutes, an hour and 25 minutes longer, on a slightly smaller battery; the speakers have no visible holes, because Apple split them into a bass unit under the screen that vibrates it and a treble unit near the edge, and the tablet is now IP68 rated. But the price has jumped by $200, starting at $699, and fully specced it costs well over $1,000. “The new iPad mini is lighter, but buying one feels heavier than ever.” The review’s closing argument is the one that will outlast both products: under Jobs, Apple went from zero to one, creating whole new kinds of products; under Cook, from one to 100, growing ten times in market value; and once you reach 100, what is left is the gaps between products — so the MacBook gets a touchscreen and the mini gets OLED but not ProMotion, and the iPhone Duo tries to fill the last gap between phone and tablet. “They’re all good, just what we expected. This time, the glowing Apple logo is back. But to make Apple truly shine, they still have more work to do.”

The takeawayThe touchscreen is a free extra you can take or leave — turn it off in Settings, Accessibility, Touch if you hate the fingerprints. The real upgrades are the tandem OLED display, the lighter body and the M5 chip. And the iPad mini’s 60Hz screen is not a technical limit; it is a product decision, made to protect the Pro.

47 Simple Steps to Go From $0 to Selling Your Company

David Park • The full 47-step list, condensed • 8 November 2026

It takes sixteen years, David Park notes in the parenthetical that is the whole joke. The list is his own life, compressed: watch The Social Network on your 14th birthday and decide startups seem pretty cool; end up working at Chipotle because you do not know anything about building; finish high school early and move to Seattle alone at 17; get a job as a software engineer at Amazon; build a Ruby on Rails side project on weekends; notice your credit score is terrible and build a personal-finance app to fix it; pivot to a credit-score app; spend ten minutes convincing Experian to give you data; build an app that helps people save money and discover the retention is terrible; read Rework; build a tool that disputes errors on credit reports for $10 a month; get a hundred signups in week one; add TransUnion and Equifax; hit a hundred thousand downloads; get into Y Combinator; raise $3 million, then $5 million from Sequoia; spend ten years learning how to be a CEO; hire a hundred and fifty people; hit $10 million in revenue; try to sell, watch the deal fall through; hit $20 million; sell. The takeawayThe honest version of every founder story is a long list of unglamorous steps with one line that does all the work: ten years learning the job. The rest is just the list.

Find All the Implicit State Machines in My App

Vox • Quoted by Garry Tan • 8 November 2026

The prompt of the day is one line long, and it is the kind of line that makes you re-read your own code. “Find all of the implicit state machines in my app (including flags, conditionals, and any combination of states that affect behavior). For each state machine, map out all the possible states, list the transitions between them, and identify which transitions are missing, invalid, or could lead to unexpected behavior.” Then the second half, which is the point: “Please consider how the app should behave in each state and how to manage any transitions to elevate UX, DX, AX.” The last two letters are the giveaway: DX is the developer experience, AX is the agent experience. Garry Tan’s reply — “Asking my software factory to see if this works will report back” — is the experiment the prompt invites. Most apps are full of state machines nobody drew: a boolean here, a status flag there, a loading state that can never exit, a combination of two flags that should not exist together but does. The prompt asks an agent to find them all and draw the map. Run it on your own codebase before your users find the missing transition for you.

The takeawayEvery app has state machines its author never drew. The bug you cannot reproduce is usually a transition you never listed. Ask the agent to draw the map; fix the missing arrows first.

Nikesh Arora’s Acquisition Rule

David Senra • A two-minute clip, text complete • 8 November 2026

David Senra’s clip of Nikesh Arora contains a rule that would have saved most acquirers most of their money: no deal until the founder agrees to a joint product roadmap. Arora’s version is blunt about why. He wasted six months going back and forth on a deal before he learned to ask the question first. The numbers he attaches to the rule are the proof: Palo Alto Networks went from $18 billion to more than $250 billion on the back of acquisitions done this way, and at Google, where he ran the business side for a decade, revenue went from $2 billion to $62 billion. The mechanism is the interesting part. An acquisition is not a purchase; it is a marriage of two product roadmaps, and if the founder will not agree to the joint roadmap before the deal closes, the deal is a press release, not a strategy. The rule forces the hard conversation — what gets built, what gets killed, who decides — into the open, before the lawyers have billed their first million. Six months of going back and forth is the tax you pay for learning the rule late.

The takeawayBefore you buy a company, agree the joint product roadmap with its founder. If you cannot, you are not buying a business; you are buying a press release.
The Archive • From the Saves • Transcribed

Ilya Sutskever, 21 November 2015: “I Hope So!”

Internal Tech Emails • Four images, transcribed in full • Filed in Musk v. Altman (2026)

Eleven years before the present, in a group chat that has now been filed as evidence in the Musk v. Altman case, the co-founder of OpenAI was worried about the man who was about to bankroll it. “Elon might spend half a day a week with us,” Ilya Sutskever wrote to Greg Brockman on 21 November 2015. “I imagined how it will be and I worry that our work environment can become very stressful. And since he’ll be bankrolling it it’ll be hard to stop it.” Brockman’s reassurance is the kind that reads differently with hindsight: “I talked about this explicitly with Sam (who I trust on this), I think it’ll be fine. Talking to Elon tomorrow, will let you know how it goes.” Then, the management theory: “In general I think these things are mostly a mix of setting expectations and having good working relationships. So pretty addressable if you know what you’re worried about, and sometimes even if you don’t know.”

Sutskever’s reply is the line that makes the exchange worth printing: “Sounds reasonable. In you call with Elon tomorrow, be sure to remind him that research takes longer than one might expect, and that deepmind is a relaxed environment.” Brockman: “For sure. Don’t stress about it. It’ll be fine!” Sutskever: “I hope so!” Read today, the exchange is a small tragedy in three acts. The worry was right: the environment did become stressful, and the funder could not be stopped. The reassurance was wrong in the specific way reassurances usually are: it assumed the problem was expectations, when the problem was power. And the request — remind Elon that research takes longer than expected and that DeepMind is a relaxed environment — is a man asking, politely, for the one thing a bankrolling founder cannot give: patience. The whole history of OpenAI is, in a sense, the answer to “I hope so!”

How Stanford Teaches AI-Native Engineering

Latent Space • Mihail Eric of Monaco on CS146S, “The Modern Software Developer” • 8 November 2026

Stanford’s most popular new course is not a course about AI. It is a course about what it means to be a software developer now that the floor has moved. CS146S, “The Modern Software Developer”, is in its second run, and Mihail Eric — the Monaco founder teaching it — frames the change as a height problem: the floor is much higher for new graduates, because an agent can write the boilerplate, and the ceiling is higher too, because the same graduate can now build things that used to need a team. The course’s answer is to teach the work that remains human: intent, judgment, verification, and the design of systems an agent can work inside safely.

The ten-week curriculum reads like a manual for the new job. Week one: build your own Claude Code in about two hundred lines, so you understand what the tool is actually doing before you trust it. Then a man-in-the-middle proxy exercise, where students watch the model’s traffic and learn what it sees; the Claude Code system prompt, roughly twenty thousand tokens, which students cut by eighty percent and watch regrow as the model asks for the context back. Then the RPI framework — research, proposal, plan — a discipline for turning a vague request into a verifiable plan before any code is written. Then skills: small, reusable units of expertise the agent can load. Then agent-ready codebases, the code-review bottleneck (the human is now the slowest part of the loop), security, background agents, and the software factory — the idea that a team of agents, properly orchestrated, is a factory floor. The assignments are real open-source contributions, reviewed by the maintainers of Factory, Cognition, OpenAI and Pi. And the experiment that underwrites the whole course is almost insultingly small: ten minutes a day, every day, using the tools, because the compounding is the point. The choke points, Eric says, are intent and verifiable systems — knowing what you want, and being able to check that you got it. Everything else is negotiable, and the agent will do it.

The takeawayThe course’s real syllabus is two words: intent and verification. If you can say what you want and check that you got it, the agent is a factory. If you cannot, it is a slot machine. Ten minutes a day is the price of learning the difference.

Why PMs Are Suddenly Valuable Again

Lenny’s Newsletter • Tulsee Doshi, Head of Product at Gemini, in conversation with Lenny Rachitsky • 8 November 2026 • 58-minute episode, transcript read in full

The model is not built. It is grown. That is Tulsee Doshi’s opening image, and it explains everything that follows: building a frontier model is “kind of like Snape’s potions class. You can put all the ingredients in, but you also have to expect that there will be a little bit of magic, and that magic is going to be surprising.” You cannot predict what comes out. You can only shape it. And shaping it — writing the spec, building the rubrics, designing the reward modelling, deciding what the model should be like — is the product manager’s job. The model’s personality is the user experience. That is why PMs, who spent the last decade being told the job was dead, are suddenly valuable again.

Doshi’s framework for deciding what to build is the 60-20-20 rule: sixty percent of what you build should work now, twenty percent should work soon, and twenty percent should be glimmers — things that barely work today but point at the future. Traditional startup wisdom says build an MVP that works; her version says build features that do not always work yet, because the model will catch up to them. Her rule for evaluation is equally practical: start with five prompts. A mini-eval of about twenty. Evaluate end to end, on the production stack, not in a sandbox. And the hiring test is now a taste test: she wants PMs who understand the vibes of the models, who have opinions, who play with the models every day and stress-test them on things they are excited about. The episode’s most useful idea is the oldest one in it: flash teams, a Stanford idea from twelve years ago under Professor Michael Bernstein, in which a team assembles for a single task and dissolves — the human version of what agent swarms are now doing. And the warning that lands hardest is about slop: the model gives you data, humans give you insight, and the product that forgets the difference is the one that ships a thousand dashboards nobody reads. The burnout, she says, comes from churn and chaos, not from hours. The job is to turn chaos into order, and to keep the signal from drowning in the noise.

The takeawayIn the frontier era, the PM’s job is not to write tickets. It is to shape a personality: to decide what the model should be like, and to build the evals that prove it. Start with five prompts. Build sixty percent for now, twenty for soon, twenty for the glimmer.

How to Start Conversations With People in Applied AI

Leverage Loops • Brandon Galang, adapted from Steve Dalton’s “The 2-Hour Job Search” • 8 November 2026

The advice in this one is almost embarrassingly simple, and that is why it works. Brandon Galang’s piece, adapted from Steve Dalton’s The 2-Hour Job Search, is about the cold message — the email or DM to a stranger in applied AI — and its thesis is that most cold messages fail for one reason: they ask for something the recipient has never done. Ask for the experience they already have. The piece gives two real messages, one to a fellow alum and one to an AI-award winner, and both follow the same shape: a specific question about something the person has actually done, a request for fifteen minutes, and an explicit invitation to reply in writing instead. Shorter beats longer, because a long message feels mass-produced even when it is not. The point is not networking. The point is that the people who can teach you the most are the people who have already done the thing, and the way to reach them is to ask about the thing, not about themselves.

The takeawayDo not open with who you are. Open with a specific question about something they have already done. Fifteen minutes, written reply welcome. The shorter the message, the less it smells of a mail merge.
