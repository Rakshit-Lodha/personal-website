Claude Claims the Riemann Hypothesis: a 250,000-Page Proof Nobody Has Read

From the saves — Deedy, and the primary source: Levent Alpöge’s four-page note

On Friday morning, a mathematician at Anthropic posted four pages on his personal website and claimed the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics. Levent Alpöge wrote, in the first paragraph: “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.” The method, he says, reaches further — it resolves the stronger Generalised Riemann Hypothesis, and he expects the Extended version too. The proof itself is not in the note. It exists only as code: seventeen million lines of Lean, split across fourteen archives. Written out in ordinary mathematical prose, Alpöge says, it would run to more than 250,000 pages.

That sentence — the proof exists, but no human has read it — is the whole story. Mathematics has never faced a claim quite like this one, and the next weeks will decide whether it is the most important result of the century or the most instructive false alarm in the history of the subject.

The claim, in one picture — the critical line

What was actually claimed. Alpöge is a serious mathematician — Harvard undergraduate, Princeton doctorate, Junior Fellow at the Harvard Society of Fellows — and his note is careful about what it is and is not. It is a claim of a proof, published as a formalisation: the argument is written in Lean, a language in which a computer checks every step, so that in principle nothing is taken on trust. What he did not publish is the readable version. He tried generating one: “I of course amused myself, and hit 250,000+ pages.” He calls that draft “some massive, unreadable travesty of ‘slop’” and decided it would be inappropriate to post it. So the world gets four pages of explanation, a timeline, and the code.

The timeline in the note is itself remarkable. The decisive ideas landed late on Sunday night, 15 November, and the proof was complete the following day — days before Anthropic staged the largest IPO in history on Thursday. Alpöge messaged Dario Amodei — “Hypothetically, if I were to have…” — to ask whether he could announce at all. He addresses the timing directly in the note: “There is an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false.” He also admits, with unusual candour, that he has not yet mastered all the mathematics in his own argument: “I am presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics.” He expects the argument to be “massively simplified, likely immediately.”

“Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”Levent Alpöge, on working with Claude — alpo.ge

The idea, in one line. The proof descends from Stepanov’s argument for the Riemann Hypothesis over function fields, where a divisor with multiplicity does the work — but multiplicity is hard to find over the integers, “corresponding to something like differentiating whole numbers.” Claude’s contribution was a chain of theta lifts, each mimicking multiplicity, with the length of the chain growing as the square root of the summation range: for a von Mangoldt sum of length X, take √X lifts. Alpöge guided the search — he put the team on the line of combining two ideas from a toy setting — and then, in his words, Claude “began to leave me in awe” and “far outpaced me near the finish line.” The argument also builds on earlier AI-assisted work: OpenAI’s release last month of a quasi-Riemann result, which Alpöge recognised as “Stepanov without multiplicity.”

How it is being checked. This is where the story stops being about one man and becomes about infrastructure. A Lean proof can be compiled: either every step follows, or it does not. Within hours of the note appearing, MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland had downloaded the code and reported that it compiles — no errors. Elliot Glazer confirmed by preliminary inspection that the formalised statement is indeed the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis, not something that merely looks like it. Terence Tao, who blogged about the claim on Friday, wrote that the proof “seems to comprehensively apply modern research results in the Langlands Program and the field of arithmetic geometry” and pointed readers to a thread on the Lean community’s Zulip chat as the best place to follow verification, adding: “I imagine the digestion process will take some time.” James Maynard of Oxford said that if it fully checks out it would clearly be a historic milestone. As of Saturday morning, no outside party has confirmed the claim, and the hypothesis officially remains open.

Note the distinction the mathematicians are drawing, because it matters. A Lean compilation proves that the code proves something. It does not by itself prove that the something is the Riemann Hypothesis as mathematicians mean it — the formal statement must match the intended theorem, and the argument must be understood, not merely executed. That is the digestion Tao is talking about, and it is the same gap the formal-verification industry in today’s Substack section exists to close.

It did not come alone. Alongside the Riemann claim, Alpöge released results on finite-time blowup for the three-dimensional Euler and Navier–Stokes equations — also formalised in Lean, also with heavy AI assistance. Tao lists all three and orders them by how digested they are; the Riemann one comes last. Deedy’s post calls this the second of the six Millennium prizes “solved by AI, after Navier–Stokes” — worth treating as enthusiasm rather than record, until the community has had its say on both.

Why it matters beyond mathematics. If the claim survives, it is the first time an AI system has closed one of the seven Millennium Problems — and it happened the week its employer became the most valuable listing in history. But the deeper point is the one NYU’s Maksym Radziwill put to Science: “The point of math — and of science — is understanding why things happen, not just collecting statements of truth.” A 250,000-page proof nobody can read is a strange kind of knowledge. Alpöge knows it: he intends to spend his time now understanding the argument, simplifying it, and teaching it. The prize, if it comes, will be for a proof. The understanding is still to be earned — and that, paradoxically, is the more interesting race now under way.

The takeawayThe claim is real, the code compiles, and the best mathematicians alive are taking it seriously — but “compiled” is not “confirmed.” Watch the Lean Zulip thread, not the headlines. And notice what this week has actually demonstrated: the bottleneck in frontier mathematics is no longer producing proofs. It is checking them, and understanding them. That is a verification problem, and it is the subject of the first Substack below.

He Plays GTA VI With His Thoughts — and Flies a Seaplane With His Right Hand

From the saves — Neuralink, video 1:59, transcribed in full

Noland Arbaugh, the first person to receive a Neuralink implant, has played GTA VI with it — and the two-minute clip Neuralink posted on Friday is the most concrete demonstration yet of what a brain–computer interface actually feels like to use. Not a cursor moving across a screen. A boat chase, a thread-the-needle drive through traffic, and a seaplane takeoff, all driven by imagined movement.

The commentary is Arbaugh’s own, recorded as he plays. On the boat mission: “Sharp left turn… Oh, thread of the needle, look at that.” On the flying: “So to pull the nose of the airplane up I essentially have to slide my right hand forward, so it’s more like… almost like the mouse motion.” The controls are mapped onto his body in a way that is by now second nature to him: a tap of the ring finger fires, and he has added his feet as extra buttons — “Once you start adding extra buttons, and then we added my feet in, or at least my foot… you get to stay in the air.” When the plane lifts off the water, he says it twice: “We did it. We did it.”

The part of the clip that stays with you is not the gameplay. It is the before-and-after, delivered in about twenty seconds. Before the implant, Arbaugh played games with a mouth stick and an iPad: “I was essentially like a bird just pecking at an iPad… There’s like a few games I could play, like obviously you made your jaw sore, made like everything sore.” After: “So then I get a chip and it’s like, well, what does this let me do? Immediately… controlling a computer is like boom, portal to the world. Like you immediately just get your portal to the world back.”

Three things are worth noticing. First, the mapping is embodied: he does not think “up”, he slides his hand forward as if moving a mouse — the implant reads motor intention, and the game reads it as control. Second, the setup is already multi-modal: fingers, feet, and a decoding dashboard that shows, live, which neurons are firing and which keys they map to. Third, the game itself is the point. GTA VI’s launch week has become the unofficial benchmark for every new interface this month — Zepto’s search spikes, Rippling’s sick-day data, and now a man flying a seaplane with his thoughts. The most demanding consumer software of the year is also, accidentally, the best demo of the most advanced hardware of the year.

The takeawayBrain–computer interfaces are usually discussed as medical devices or as science fiction. This clip is neither: it is a person playing a video game, badly and joyfully, with a body that does not obey him and a chip that does. The “portal to the world” line is the product pitch, delivered by the only person qualified to make it.

The War on Slop Has a Weapon: Proof

Latent Space — The War Against Slop and an Emerging Weapon to Fight it: Formal Verification, 20 November 2026

The word of the year in the AI industry, the piece argues, is “slop” — low-quality output from a large language model. And with most code now AI-generated, avoiding slop has become a daily concern for every working engineer. The proposed weapon is old, unfashionable, and suddenly central: formal verification, the use of mathematical proofs and logic to verify that software does what it claims.

The cast of companies is new. Harmonic’s Aristotle is an automated theorem prover; Theorem sells verification as the only defence that holds while adversaries scale; Axiom Math builds AxiomProver and a toolkit for exploring proofs; &Truth, the newest entrant, is building a “verification machine” on top of Lean, the open-source proof assistant. Its co-founder is Clark Barrett, a Stanford professor and co-creator of cvc5, an automated reasoning engine that Amazon now calls billions of times a day in production pipelines — including specification analysis and test generation in Kiro, Amazon’s agentic development environment. “It’s been fun to see my tools go from a good idea in the lab to cvc5 is now called billions of times a day in production pipelines at Amazon,” Barrett told Latent Space.

The distinction at the heart of the piece is between testing and proving. When coding-agent companies say they “verify” their work, they usually mean the agent ran tests, critiqued its own output, or had another model judge it. Barrett’s reply is the line to keep: “The main difference is really just, how do you know when you’re done? So if you’re testing, patching, scanning — using all these existing tools — they’re quite good. I mean, they find a ton of problems. They raise those issues. But you’re never quite sure, did I find all of them or is there one more lurking there that my tool can’t find?” Testing finds bugs. A proof rules out the existence of the next one.

The shiftYour time moves from writing and reviewing code to writing and reviewing specifications. The code works the first time.

Lean is the hinge. It is the language frontier models now use to solve supremely hard mathematics: just this week, GPT-6.2 Astra produced what is believed to be the first Lean proof of Smoktunowicz’s Gap Theorem, and Anthropic used Lean to formalise Fermat’s Last Theorem — a project of thirteen million lines. “What Lean does is it just checks that you didn’t make a mistake,” Barrett says. “So you can think of it as a very sophisticated checker for mathematics.” Inside Harmonic’s Aristotle, “AI writes the proofs and Lean checks every step of them, so a result never rests on the AI being right.”

Two details point at where this goes. First, verification is getting cheaper: on a set of thirty-six small verification tasks, Anthropic reported that Opus 5.5 reached a 50% solve rate using about 40% fewer tokens than Opus 5 — and the trend is expected to continue as models are trained on long verification tasks. Second, verification is becoming a training signal, not just a filter: Axiom Math uses formal verification to provide correctness feedback for training its mathematical reasoning systems, with proof attempts improving future models. Its CEO, Carina Hong, puts the ambition plainly: “Formal verification, or verified AI, to us it’s not just about handling or kicking out the lousiness, the hallucinations, the mistakes. It’s about scaling brilliance. It’s about super-intelligence.”

The cybersecurity case is the most urgent. &Truth’s first tool, &Atlas, maps the world’s open-source C code; the aim is to go through all the C code in the world and ensure it is memory-safe, pointing at what is not and needs fixing. Theorem and Anthropic call the approach “LLM-scaled formal verification” — security guarantees at a scale that was previously impractical.

Read this piece next to the lead story and the connection is hard to miss. The Riemann claim exists as Lean code precisely because Lean is now the only venue in which a 250,000-page argument can be checked at all. The same week, a whole industry is forming around the idea that the same machinery should check the code your agents write on Monday morning. The coda is a tweet from Cognition’s Nader Dabit, quoted at the end of the article: when you tell a team of agents what you want and they go and build it, you need a way to check the correctness of what they deliver. He calls it “outcome engineering.” Whatever the term, the job description is the same: the human stops writing the thing and starts writing the test the thing must pass.

The takeawayTests tell you what you found. Proofs tell you what is left. As agents write more of the world’s code, the scarce skill is not prompting — it is specifying precisely enough that a machine can check the result. “Veri-coding” is the name for the workflow that follows, and Lean is the checker it runs on.

The Week Everything Got Cheaper: Starship, Cancer, and a Thousand-Year Seastead

Not Boring — Weekly Dose of Optimism #220, 20 November 2026

Packy McCormick opens this week’s Dose with a claim that is hard to argue with: “What a week to be alive. Since we spoke last Friday, GTA 6 is finally out, Anthropic is public, Starships are reusable, leukemia may not be as deadly, we may be able to live on the open seas, and… there’s just so much more.” The edition’s through-line is cost curves collapsing — of launch, of drug discovery, of finding metal, of building industry itself. Five stories, one pattern.

The final boss of reusability is dead. Over the weekend, Starship Flight 15’s Ship 42 launched from Starbase, Texas, reached orbit, deployed a batch of Starlink satellites, spent more than twenty-two hours in space, survived fiery hypersonic re-entry, flipped upright, and dropped cleanly into Mechazilla’s chopsticks. SpaceX had caught Super Heavy boosters before, which was already absurd; catching the orbital upper stage — the part that goes to orbit carrying the payload — was, in McCormick’s words, “the final boss of rapid, full reusability. The final boss is dead.” The economics are the story: the upper stage was roughly 90% of the marginal cost per flight, and having to build a new one each time put a hard floor of $30–40 million under every launch. Reusable, the marginal cost drops to something like $2–10 million per launch, or $20–100 per kilogram versus Falcon 9’s $1,000. The goal is two launches a week by the end of 2027, hundreds a year by 2029, and multiple a day beyond that — carrying satellites, data centres, drug factories, “and eventually all sorts of things we’ll need to thrive in space and on other worlds.” Heat-shield durability and turnaround time remain the work ahead. For now: watch the slow-motion footage and marvel.

Leukaemia may not be as deadly. On Tuesday, SELLAS Life Sciences and Memorial Sloan Kettering announced positive topline results from the pivotal Phase 3 REGAL trial of galinpepimut-S, an immunotherapeutic vaccine for acute myeloid leukaemia — a brutal blood cancer with roughly a 33% five-year survival rate that kills around 11,500 Americans a year. The trial followed 126 patients whose AML had relapsed and who had fought back into a second or later remission, ineligible for stem-cell transplant: a high-risk group with very few options left. Patients who received GPS alone, with no chemotherapy alongside it, had a median overall survival of 24.0 months, against 14.7 months for best available treatment — more than nine extra months of life, and a 41% reduction in the risk of death. Unlike Merck and Moderna’s personalised mRNA melanoma vaccine, GPS is an off-the-shelf peptide vaccine, which should make it faster, cheaper and more widely available if approved — and the WT1 protein it targets shows up in a number of blood cancers and solid tumours, so it may work beyond AML. And then, as McCormick was wrapping up, the BBC reported a new pill for pancreatic cancer that shrank tumours in one in twelve patients so completely they were undetectable in scans, and stopped tumour growth in 90% of the 61 patients. “Experts are calling it the biggest breakthrough in pancreatic cancer treatment for decades.” Neither is a cure. Both are nine months — or a halted tumour — plus a call option on much longer.

Palmer Luckey’s thousand-year seastead. Fresh off announcing Warhound and a drumbeat of Anduril news, Luckey announced on X — pre-empting a journalist working on the story — that he is “developing a seastead called SLAB with a 1,000 year design life.” He has been privately funding and building it since selling Oculus to Facebook for $2 billion in 2014; he and friends capitalised it at a $1 billion valuation and put more money in recently. SLAB is a massive offshore floating industrial platform, meant to be cheaper than land while operating outside the regulatory morass that chokes American heavy industry: on it, future Palmers may build factories for metal processing, desalination, oil refining, chemical engineering, and frontier medical research. It is not, Luckey insisted, “just another guy doing an ocean data center thing” — it is, in McCormick’s phrase, the “Thousand Year Luckey Empire.” (Regular readers will recall Luckey’s seastead project led Edition 56’s briefs last week; this is the fuller picture.)

Tesla’s Powercore, and two industrial bases. Tesla announced solid-state transformers with its new Powercore — another chunk of the capability stack that eliminates a bottleneck, and one that plays well with the rest of the Musk stack. McCormick has covered solid-state transformers for years in the context of Heron Power, founded by ex-Tesla SVP Drew Baglino, for their ability to speed up and cheapen data-centre construction and stabilise the grid. His judgement: both Heron and Tesla will do well; there is a ton of demand. The larger point is structural: “A few years ago, we barely had one modern industrial base. Now we have two.” Elon builds practically everything inside his own companies; a parallel American ecosystem is emerging to serve the growing number of electric companies not in the Elonverse.

Earth AI finds a 3-kilometre seam. In 1969, explorers drilled a copper-gold deposit in New South Wales and wrote it off; in 1997 they tried again and reached the same conclusion. This week Earth AI — a Not Boring Capital portfolio company — announced its Alpheus model found a continuous copper, gold and platinum-group metals system stretching more than three kilometres at Fontenoy, with hints it may be eight. Back in January the models re-read the old data and recognised the site was not discontinuous pockets but a massive magma-filled fault line; five weeks later Earth AI started drilling — it owns its own rigs and labs — and has now drilled 35 holes tracing mineralisation from 30 to 478 metres deep. The context: the world spends about $14 billion a year searching for new mineral deposits, but major discoveries have dropped roughly 70% over the past decade. Discovery is only the first step toward metal out of the ground, but with 4,500 more targets on its list, Earth AI has “the potential to be one of the world’s most beautiful businesses.”

The takeawayFive stories, one mechanism: a cost that used to be a wall becomes a slope. A launch that cost $30 million now costs $2 million. A vaccine brewed for one patient becomes one brewed for everyone. A deposit written off twice becomes a three-kilometre seam because the old data got re-read by a new model. Optimism, in this newsletter’s sense, is not a mood. It is a bet that the cost curves keep bending — and this was a very good week for that bet.

Linear’s $100 Million Lesson: Sell the System, Not the Seats

Latent Space — Scaling Linear’s GTM to $100M ARR — Cristina Cordova, COO, 19 November 2026

Linear was founded to build a better issue tracker. Seven years and $134 million in funding later, its CEO published a post titled “Issue tracking is dead.” In this episode of Latent Space, COO Cristina Cordova — seven and a half years at Stripe, platform and partnerships at Notion, partner at First Round before joining Linear in 2023 — explains how the company navigated that, and how its go-to-market changed along the way. The numbers first: Linear passed $100 million in ARR this year with more than 40,000 paying customers and 177% net revenue retention; in August, employees sold $99 million in stock at a $2.5 billion valuation.

Half the work is now created by agents. Linear shipped its MCP server in May 2025 — Cordova says it was basically the first in its category — and a year and a half later more than half the issues created in Linear come from agents, up from 3% a year ago. The company has shipped a platform for building agents into Linear, integrations with Codex, Cursor and GitHub Copilot, and its own Linear Agent. But Cordova is explicit that becoming a database with an MCP server on top would be a mistake: if the agent workflows inside your product are not better than the ones outside it, nobody has a reason to open your app. So she watches two lines — agent usage and app usage. If agent usage climbs and app usage does not, you are becoming a commodity. At Linear, both are up and to the right, because people still come back to the app to see and coordinate the work.

When Karri Saarinen published “Issue tracking is dead” in March, most customers agreed — and then asked the obvious question: so what am I buying? Linear’s answer is a product development system built around agent orchestration, where humans stop doing the manual work of filing, labelling, assigning and closing issues. Customers had been pushing it there: most frontier AI labs run on Linear and wanted it to move faster, and pre-AI companies like Ramp and Coinbase were building their own internal agents and needed an API to plug them in. Cordova says Linear is lucky it is small enough to cannibalise itself. Google has a much harder problem: put an AI answer above every search result, and fewer people look at the ads.

The AI budget behind $100 million in months. The thing speeding up purchases right now, Cordova says, is AI budget: companies carved out money for AI tools that did not exist before, and any product that fits inside it skips the usual fight for spend. It is already starting to change. Buyers are signing one-year deals and paid pilots, not three-year contracts, because nobody knows what they will want in two years. And some overnight-$100-million stories are a handful of $30–40 million contracts with defence companies or giant law firms — a very different business from Linear’s 40,000 paying customers on what was, until June, entirely seat-based pricing. “When those one-year deals come up for renewal, we’ll find out whose ARR was real.”

The 2025 enterprise roadmap went in the bin. Three weeks after joining in 2023, Cordova crossed “move upmarket to enterprise” off Linear’s company goals. It had one salesperson. She went after growth-stage companies that would have to rip out an existing tool instead — the same way Stripe won new businesses long before it won switchers. Linear finally declared an enterprise push in 2025, with a roadmap full of features for large organisations. Then agents happened: the MCP server, an agents platform with a whole new set of APIs, integrations with Codex, Cursor and Copilot. None of it was planned, and a lot of the enterprise work never got done. Cordova chose to race ahead anyway. Competitors waited, and enterprises started putting Linear on their shortlists.

No SDRs, no forward-deployed engineers. Linear has never hired a single SDR. Cordova thinks the qualification call wastes the customer’s time: they want a demo, not questions about their budget. Account executives do their own outbound, with tooling to keep it personal. It does not do forward-deployed engineering either — Linear should be easy enough to adopt that nobody needs access to your systems. Solutions engineers sell alongside enterprise AEs instead, sometimes building a working ServiceNow integration overnight to show a prospect it can be done. And support stays in-house: Linear will not let a large language model answer tickets on its own. AI drafts replies and a human decides what goes out. Customers sometimes assume the replies are AI because they arrive so fast. In three and a half years, Cordova has never had an escalation over a bad support experience.

Everyone wants outcome-based pricing. Cordova would love a model like Intercom’s Fin, which charges per support conversation its AI resolves — $0.99 each. It is harder for Linear: what is auto-triaging a bug to the right team worth when one company thinks that is the PM’s job and another does not? Until someone cracks that, Linear charges for what costs it money and keeps the rest free. Seats stay. Linear Agent chat is free even though it runs on frontier models. In coding sessions, users pick OpenAI or Anthropic models and harnesses, and Linear passes through the published API price plus a small markup. Loops run on AI credits. Linear does not start charging for things that used to be free: new AI features launch as free betas with notice that pricing is coming, and prices start high and come down rather than the other way around.

Why not hire a hundred salespeople? Linear runs on about half the headcount of companies at its stage and revenue. Cordova says a sales team bigger than the rest of the company would have cost it the trust engineering now has in sales. From Stripe she brought a 40% rule: at least 40% of the company should be engineering, product, design and data. If sales wanted more people, it helped recruit engineers first. Hiring is slow on purpose — candidates do a work trial on a real problem. Hers was writing Linear’s go-to-market plan for the next six to twelve months, which then became her job. At about 170 people, the only C-suite is the founders and her.

The takeawayLinear’s GTM is a bet that the product is the salesforce: no SDRs, no forward-deployed engineers, no AI answering support alone, and a headcount rule that keeps builders in the majority. The agent wave did not change that bet — it raised the stakes. If your agent workflows are better inside your product than outside it, customers stay; if not, you are a database with an API. Watch the two lines Cordova watches: agent usage and app usage. Both have to climb.

Wispr Flow Comes to the iPhone

Tanay Kothari quoting Wispr Flow · silent demo, 16 seconds, frames verified

Wispr Flow, the voice dictation tool, is live on iPhone — and the point of the launch is what it removes. “No more bouncing between apps to use Flow,” the company writes. The sixteen-second demo, which has no audio track, shows the idea in two frames: a chat with Maya (“Did you get the Flow update?” / “Installing it now”), a tap on the microphone, and the keyboard gaining a “Wispr Flow” key.
