Lead Story · The Mindset Test

Playing Product vs. Doing Product

Shreyas Doshi · X, 28 November 2026 · Table transcribed in full from the post’s image

Shreyas Doshi has been sitting on this one, by his own account, for a while — “way overdue”, he calls it. The distinction is not between good product people and bad ones. It is between two people who can look identical on a résumé, run the same rituals, and use the same vocabulary — one of whom is performing product management, and the other of whom is actually doing it. “I see people Playing Product all the time, in startups and big companies,” he writes. “Interestingly, someone can have strong product skills, and yet end up Playing Product. It is mainly about their underlying mindset.”

That is the sting in the piece. The usual diagnosis of a failing product org is a skills gap: hire better PMs, teach better frameworks, run better discovery. Doshi says the failure mode survives all of that, because it is not a capability problem. It is a motivation problem. The Playing Product person wants to feel like a great product person; the Doing Product person wants the product to win in the market. Everything downstream — where attention goes, how evidence is treated, what gets measured — follows from which of those two hungers is actually driving the work.

Read the table as a diagnostic, not a personality test, and three rows do most of the work. The first is hypotheses: tests written “to look rigorous” are the product world’s cargo cult — the experiment exists so the deck can say an experiment was run, and inconvenient results get explained away rather than allowed to change the plan. A test that cannot change your mind is theatre, and theatre is expensive: it burns the one resource a team cannot recover, which is the weeks spent building the wrong thing with confidence. The second is customer research: collecting quotes that support the existing idea is the most common form of self-deception in product, because it feels like listening while functioning as confirmation. The honest version of the job is to go looking for what would prove the idea wrong — and to be relieved, not threatened, when you find it early. The third is the failure row, which is really the same test applied after the fact: when something fails, do you protect the narrative, or do you ask why customers chose something else? The second question is the only one that contains information about the future.

“Values ideas that turn out true, even boring ones.”Shreyas Doshi — Doing Product, on insights

There is also a quiet warning in the row about studying the greats, and it lands harder in an industry that runs on founder lore. Copying the visible habits of great product people — their phrases, their rituals, their stories — is the fastest way to sound like them and the slowest way to think like them. The useful question is never “what did they do?” but “why did that decision win in their market?” Strip away the market and the constraints and you are left with a style guide, not a strategy. Doshi’s table is, in the end, a test you can run on yourself in about ninety seconds: look at last week’s work and ask, for each row, which column it actually belonged in. The uncomfortable part is that the answer is usually mixed — and that the mix is the point. Nobody plays product all the time or does product all the time. The job is noticing which column you are in, and moving.

The takeawaySkills don’t separate the two columns; mindset does. If a test can’t change your plan, a metric can’t tell you you’re losing, or research can’t prove you wrong, you are performing product work, not doing it — and the fix is not another framework, it is asking, honestly, what you are optimising for.
From the Saves · Standards, Learned First-Hand

Patrick Collison’s Advice to People in Their Twenties: Go Find the Highest Standards and Stand Next to Them

a16z, quoting Patrick Collison with Dwarkesh Patel, 2024 · X, 26 November 2026 · Video (0:53) transcribed in full from the audio

The clip is fifty-three seconds long and the advice in it is almost offensively simple: if you are in your twenties, find where the highest standards are, and go experience them firsthand. Collison’s claim, as a16z posts it, is that this is the single most repeated lesson he hears from people in other domains — that working with a particular person, organisation, or environment taught them what great actually is, and that the lesson permanently recalibrated what they demanded of their own work.

The mechanism is worth spelling out, because it is the same mechanism Doshi’s lead story is circling from the other side. Standards are not really transmissible as information. You can read that Stripe’s bar was high, or that a great lab’s review process is brutal, and understand it intellectually without it changing anything about how you work on Monday. What changes you is proximity: watching someone you respect refuse to ship something you would have shipped, and realising the gap between your “good enough” and theirs is not a matter of taste but of discipline. Collison’s version of a career strategy, then, is not “learn skills” or “build a network” — it is to deliberately place yourself inside an environment whose standards embarrass your own, early, while you still have the time to absorb them. “Maybe one version of what people in their twenties should do,” he says, “is figure out, where can you learn the highest standards? Where are the highest standards embodied, and where can you go and experience that firsthand?”

The standards ladder — how a bar becomes yours

It pairs, almost too neatly, with the lead. Doshi’s Playing Product person has strong skills and the wrong hunger; Collison’s answer to that trap is environmental, not instructional. You do not fix a miscalibrated bar by studying harder. You fix it by standing next to a higher one until your own moves. For a twenty-something choosing between a prestigious-but-mediocre team and a demanding one that will make them feel slow for a year, the clip is a tiebreaker: take the room that makes you uncomfortable. The discomfort is the education.

From the Saves · The Super-App Creep

WhatsApp Wants to Sell You Your Next SIM Card

Kunal Shah · X, 26 November 2026 · WhatsApp blog, “Stay Connected on WhatsApp When You Travel”, read in full

Kunal Shah’s post is a single provocation — “What if WhatsApp could work anywhere you go in the world without any struggle" — attached to a real announcement. WhatsApp is beginning explorations around eSIMs: a way to buy and install a data eSIM for international travel inside the app itself, in a few taps, without leaving WhatsApp. Land, and you keep using WhatsApp on the same number, without the airport kiosk, the café Wi-Fi hunt, or the roaming bill. Shah’s framing gives it a name, WAnderlust, and an open invitation: “Inviting partners everywhere to join us in this important mission.”

The blog post, published 26 November, names the partners WhatsApp is working with: Jio, Smart Axiata, Smart Communications, Verizon, and others. Rollout begins “in select countries soon”, and the feature will simply appear in the app when it reaches you — no dates, no country list, no pricing yet. That vagueness is itself the tell. WhatsApp is not launching a travel product; it is testing whether the world’s most-used messaging app can become the front door to connectivity itself. The pitch is friction, not technology: the eSIM already exists, the telcos already sell it, and the airport kiosk already works. What WhatsApp owns is the moment of need — you are already in the app, messaging the person picking you up, when you realise you have no data. Owning that moment is worth more than owning the network.

How the WAnderlust flow is supposed to work

There are two ways to read this. The generous one: it is a genuinely useful feature for the two-billion-person app that travellers already live inside, and the Jio partnership in particular matters for India, where WhatsApp is effectively infrastructure. The sharper one: every super-app in history has followed the same path — start with the thing people do every day, then absorb the adjacent transaction that happens next to it. WeChat did it with payments; WhatsApp has done it with business messaging and payments in India and Brazil; connectivity is the next adjacency, and it is a much bigger one. The question to watch is not whether the eSIM works — it will — but what WhatsApp becomes once it sells you the connection you use to reach WhatsApp. The company that owns the pipe and the conversation on top of it has a different kind of leverage, and telcos, who spent a decade being reduced to dumb pipes by exactly this app, will have noticed the irony.

The takeawayWatch the distribution, not the feature. An eSIM sold at the moment of landing, inside the app you already have open, is a distribution play — and distribution is what decides whether this stays a travel convenience or becomes WhatsApp’s next platform.
From the Saves · The Creator Escape Hatch

The New Founder Career Path: Raise, Fail, Become a Creator, Never Raise Again

Greg Isenberg · X, 27 November 2026

Isenberg says he is observing a new trend, and he lays it out as a six-step loop: a startup raises venture capital; it uses the venture dollars to become a creator; the startup fails; the founder falls back on becoming a full-time creator and makes five hundred thousand to five million dollars a year; they incubate other products, bootstrapped; they never raise venture capital again. It reads like a joke and a business model at the same time, which is usually a sign it is describing something real.

1 · RaiseStartup raises venture capital
→
2 · CreateUses VC dollars to build an audience
→
3 · FailThe startup doesn’t make it
→
4 · MonetiseFull-time creator, $500k–$5M a year
→
5 · IncubateBootstrapped products from the audience
→
6 · Never againNo more venture capital

The interesting step is the second one, because it reframes what the venture money was actually buying. In the old story, VC funded a product. In Isenberg’s loop, it funds an audience — content, distribution, a personal brand — and the product is almost incidental. When the startup dies, the audience does not, and the audience turns out to be the durable asset: it converts into creator income at a scale (half a million to five million dollars a year) that most venture-backed startups never reach, and it becomes the distribution channel for the next, bootstrapped product. The venture capitalist, in this telling, has unknowingly funded someone else’s media company. Whether founders are doing this deliberately or discovering it by accident matters less than the incentive it creates: if building in public is the rational strategy even when the startup fails, then the loudest founders are not necessarily the most committed to their companies — they may simply be the ones who understood, early, which asset survives.

The loop also has a quiet sting for the venture model itself. Step six — never raise again — is the founder keeping the lesson. Once you have an audience that pays you directly and a product you own outright, the venture bargain (money for control, growth at all costs, an exit on someone else’s timetable) stops looking like a good deal. Isenberg is describing a world in which the best outcome of a failed startup is a founder who never needs venture again, which is a strange thing for the venture ecosystem to be producing at scale. The open question is how much of the five-hundred-thousand-to-five-million range is survivorship bias — the creators you hear about are, by definition, the ones it worked for — and how many founders in step three are quietly discovering that an audience of a few thousand does not pay rent. The loop is real. The conversion rate is the part nobody posts about.

Instinct, Onboarded to the Family: Time-to-Value Is Now Measured in Minutes

Logan Kilpatrick · X, 28 November 2026

Logan Kilpatrick has spent the last week onboarding non-technical family members to Instinct, and his report is the one every agent builder wants to hear: “it’s crazy how fast people just get it, time to value for good agent experiences in 2027 is going to be minutes or less.” The reason it sticks, he says, is that “you don’t need a new app. Texting for the win!” That last line is the whole thesis. The agent that wins the mainstream is not the one with the best benchmark; it is the one that meets people inside the interface they already live in, with no install, no onboarding flow, and no new habit to form. Family members are the harshest possible test of that claim — they will not read your docs, they will not tolerate a learning curve, and they will tell you, immediately, if it is confusing. If they get it in minutes, the product has crossed the line from a demo for the already-converted into something ordinary people can use. The prediction embedded in the post — that 2027’s good agent experiences will have time-to-value measured in minutes — is a bet that distribution will be decided by familiarity, not capability. The best model in the world loses to the adequate model that arrives by text message.

Drive, in One Sentence

Kunal Shah · X, 26 November 2026

“Drive is often the core force behind any success. Drive often manifests as being competitive or being persistent or having high agency.” Three sentences, one idea, and a useful reframe: drive is not a trait you either have or don’t, it is a force that shows up wearing different costumes — competitiveness, persistence, agency — depending on the person. The practical version is that you don’t need to manufacture motivation in the abstract; you need to find which of its forms you actually have, and put yourself where that form is an advantage. A competitive person should pick a game with a scoreboard. A persistent person should pick a problem that rewards outlasting everyone else. A high-agency person should pick a situation where nobody is coming to help. Same fuel, different engine.

From the Substacks

How Anthropic Builds Software in 2026: The Rust Rewrite, the Review Agent, and the Death of the Coding Interview

The Pragmatic Engineer · Gergely Orosz with Katelyn Lesse, who leads platform engineering at Anthropic · 25 November 2026 · Read in full

This is the most useful inside account yet of what a frontier lab’s engineering actually looks like once agents are doing real work — not a demo, but the daily operating system of the company that builds Claude. Katelyn Lesse leads platform engineering at Anthropic and previously led teams at Stripe; her team owns the Claude API and the infrastructure behind Claude Code and Claude Desktop. The episode’s headline story is the rewrite of the API that serves every Claude token — the Messages API, which does authentication, tokenisation, billing, and safety checks before calling the GPU stack — from a Python monolith to Rust, in about five months, by a team in which nobody was a Rust expert.

Start with why the monolith existed at all. In 2023 Anthropic was still a research company, the API team was small and overlapped heavily with Research, and researchers worked in Python, so Python was the natural choice — OpenAI made the same call, and later made the same rewrite. The problem was the CPU footprint: as the API grew, the overhead of Python became a real cost, and adding CPU capacity was hard in a global CPU shortage. The rewrite took a few engineers and a few months, and the detail that matters is how it was verified. The team did not do strict code reviews. Instead they had an unfair advantage: the existing Python API was a perfect test harness. They ran the new Rust API in shadow mode against production traffic and iterated until every response was identical. Lesse estimates engineers looked closely at only around twenty per cent of the generated code. Her rule for when that is acceptable is the one to remember: “As long as you can really boil something down to make it verifiable in some way, then you don’t have to look at every single line of Rust code that’s getting written.” Verifiability, not review coverage, is the control. And the reason the team felt able to attempt a language none of them knew is the quietest revolution in the piece: “By 2026, they felt that Claude was good enough at Rust, and Claude helped engineers learn more about Rust during the rewrite.” The model was the onboarding.

Twelve learnings structure the episode, and the ones that travel furthest outside Anthropic are about what did not change. Planning and alignment time has not shrunk: engineers spend more time coordinating than implementing, and kicking off a project still takes the same effort to get Inference, Research, Safety, and App teams on the same page. Two-pizza teams of five to eight people are still the unit, because that is the right size to understand a complex system and staff an on-call rotation — and it is the team, not the individual, that gets paged. What changed is everything around those constants. Anthropic runs a Claude code-review classifier on every pull request, deciding whether a human review is needed at all (a mechanical change like an SDK bump, or a flaky-test fix, can be reviewed, merged, and deployed by Claude alone), how many human reviews a change needs, and which team should do it. A Claude agent has been shadowing the API on-call rotation for months, and now categorises incoming alerts first — real or noise — before a human is woken. The long-term goal is for the agent to handle incidents without waking anyone; they are not there yet. Onboarding for new engineers is, in Lesse’s words, “talk to Claude”: Claude is connected to the codebase and internal systems, explains the architecture, and can generate interactive tutorials and visualisations. And a product manager shipped a feature to production — the “software factory” is not a metaphor inside Anthropic, it is the org chart.

Two learnings will matter most to anyone hiring or being hired. First, Anthropic’s team phased out the traditional coding interview, because strong candidates who crushed every other interview were starting to fail it — they no longer write code by hand. The team is experimenting with interviews conducted together with coding agents, while design and deep-dive interviews stay unchanged. Second, for promotions, “time to complete a project” is now meaningless: a year-long project can be done in days or weeks with AI, so the team evaluates impact instead — CPU saved, latency reduced, incidents avoided. Lesse’s closing message is for engineers having an identity crisis about whether their knowledge still matters: the agentic infrastructure problems — multi-agent systems, verification, the hardest problems in software — are wide open, and “you and your skills can be the difference in whether or not [they] get tackled”. The through-line of the whole episode is a single shift: the scarce skill is no longer writing code, or even knowing a language. It is making things verifiable, so that the machine can do the writing and a human can still stand behind the result.

The takeawayAnthropic’s rewrite worked because the old system was a perfect test harness, not because the team were Rust experts. The general lesson: the bottleneck in AI-era engineering is not code generation, it is verification — and the teams that get this right will be the ones that can trust what the machine writes.

The Dose #221: Quantum Gravity, a Million Satellites, and a Pig Kidney That Just Turned One

Packy McCormick’s Friday Dose is, as ever, a tour of the week’s good news — and this week the news refused to stay in its lane. It is not a Theory of Everything — vacuum only, no matter, unconfirmed by experiment, and unlikely, on early commentary, to be the final answer — but it is a concrete, background-independent attempt to turn general relativity itself into a quantum theory, and it arrived the same week as a proof of the biggest problem in mathematics. The Dose’s framing is the right one: the breakthroughs are arriving faster than the institutions built to check them.

Suraksha Diagnostic: Why Owning an MRI Machine Is Not a Moat

The Loggical Investor · Investor’s Newsletter #64 · 27 November 2026 · Read in full

The Loggical Investor’s latest deep dive is a first-principles tour of a business most investors file under one word — diagnostics — and shows why that word hides two completely different economics. Suraksha Diagnostic, a Kolkata-centred integrated chain across Eastern India, runs pathology, radiology, and doctor consultations under one network: 1 central reference lab, 7 satellite labs, 19 hubs, 58 spokes, and 227 franchisee-owned sample collection centres, plus over 160 polyclinic chambers and a network of more than 1,000 doctors. In FY26, radiology contributed about 44.26 per cent of revenue and pathology 48.85 per cent, with the rest from consultations — a balance that makes it a useful lens for the whole sector, because the two halves behave nothing alike.

Pathology is a volume business with modest entry barriers: a basic lab can be set up for roughly twenty-five to seventy-five lakh rupees, and routine tests — CBC, glucose, lipid profile, thyroid — are standardised, commoditised, and under constant pricing pressure. The advantage there comes from distribution reach, processing efficiency, brand trust, and volumes, not from owning equipment; Thyrocare is the proof that a scaled pathology network can generate a return on capital employed near 35 per cent. Radiology is the opposite: a 1.5T MRI machine costs roughly three to five crore rupees, a fully equipped imaging hub ten to twelve crore before it earns a rupee, and the work needs trained radiologists whose judgement cannot be replaced by technicians. The decisive variable is utilisation — an MRI costs the same whether it does eight scans a day or twenty, so the fixed cost per scan collapses only when patient volumes are high enough. Owning the machine is not the moat. Keeping it busy is.

Suraksha’s hub-and-spoke: access feeds the expensive machines

The cautionary tale is Thyrocare’s Nueclear. Thyrocare built a superb pathology business and then diversified into radiology through its nuclear-imaging subsidiary — and the capital efficiency collapsed: pathology generated a return on capital employed near 35 per cent, Nueclear just 6.95 per cent, with declining revenue and continued investment needs. In September 2026 Thyrocare’s board approved selling its entire Nueclear stake to Trovera Healthcare for about a hundred and forty-one crore rupees. The lesson the piece draws is precise: entering a capital-intensive segment is not hard because of the investment; it is hard because generating the throughput to justify the investment is a different business from the one you already run. Suraksha starts from a different place — it already has a substantial radiology presence, and its spokes and polyclinics can feed referrals into the hubs — but the test is the same: can it convert network reach into utilisation?

The numbers to track are given in unusual detail. In the second quarter of FY27 Suraksha served about 4.2 lakh patients and conducted around 24 lakh tests — roughly 5.7 tests per patient — with revenue per patient of 2,348 rupees, up from 2,083 a year earlier, and an indicative blended revenue per test of roughly 410–415 rupees. The maturity curve is the heart of the story: centres operating for more than two years improved their EBITDA margin from 38.2 per cent in the first quarter of FY26 to 42 per cent in the second quarter of FY27, while centres under two years moved from negative 19.6 per cent to positive 11.8 per cent, and the consolidated margin rose from 31.7 per cent to 36.7 per cent. The catch is that every new centre re-opens the same hole — rent, staff, and equipment costs arrive before patients do — so consolidated margins improve only if the existing network matures faster than new openings dilute it. The piece also flags the accounting: reported EBITDA of 36.7 per cent under Ind AS 116 is 29.2 per cent on the older IGAAP basis, and the two should never be mixed when comparing companies. Seasonality matters too: the third quarter is typically the weakest, with management guiding to a sequential revenue decline of around four to five per cent, and the monsoon quarter can lift testing demand. The mature integrated comparison is Vijaya, with about 37 per cent of revenue from radiology and an FY26 EBITDA margin of 41.4 per cent — the target Suraksha is chasing, from an earlier stage, in Eastern India. The investment question for Part 2, which the author promises next, is whether the current valuation offers an attractive risk-reward for that chase. The author discloses a possible position in the securities discussed; the piece is not investment advice.
