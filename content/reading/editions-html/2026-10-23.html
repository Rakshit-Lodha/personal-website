The Lead Story

The Five-X Verdict

Via @madhavchanchani — and @sumanthr’s essay on how we got here

Swiggy’s share price fell five percent on Thursday to an all-time low, and the scoreboard now reads: Eternal is worth five times Swiggy — roughly ₹2.96 lakh crore against ₹58,000 crore. When Swiggy went public in November 2024 the gap was barely two times. In under two years the verdict has more than doubled. What makes the saves interesting is not the number but the archaeology. Sumanth Raghavendra’s essay reaches back to the “halcyon days” when, he writes, “there was only one arbiter in the India startup ecosystem — Softbank.” Softbank chose Swiggy. Zomato, starved of private-investor enthusiasm, was pushed toward the public markets — and the listing that was supposed to be the consolation prize became the machine that made it.

The scoreboard Sumanth Raghavendra’s essay explains: Softbank’s chosen horse trades at a fifth of the one it passed over.

The mechanism is the part worth sitting with. The essay’s argument is that private-investor apathy was the primary reason Zomato went public — and going public early, with all its quarterly discipline and currency for acquisitions, turned out to be the superior strategy to winning the private funding sweepstakes. Softbank’s blessing was the hot hand in 2018. By 2026, the market cap ratio says the blessing was the wrong side of the bet. There is a broader product lesson buried here for anyone who has sat in an investor meeting: the consensus of the smart money is a lagging indicator of compounding, not a leading one. Zomato’s edge was not better food delivery. It was earlier access to the one thing private capital could not give it — a public currency and the discipline that comes with reporting to everyone, every quarter.

“There was only one arbiter in the India startup ecosystem — Softbank.”— @sumanthr, on the halcyon days

Learnings

  • Being listed early beat being picked early. The public market gave Eternal a currency, a discipline, and a compounding window that private cheques could not.
  • The arbiter is a lagging indicator. When one investor’s taste decides a category, the contrarian path — listing into apathy — can be the winning one.
  • Watch the ratio, not the price. Swiggy’s 5% down-day is noise; the 2×-to-5× drift since its IPO is the signal. Markets render verdicts slowly, then all at once.
Takeaway — If you are building in a category with an anointed winner, remember the scoreboard above. The anointed winner gets the coverage; the public compounder gets the compounding. Pick which game you are playing on purpose.

Security week

When the Builds Stop

Via @championswimmer (Arnav Gupta) — and @cgtwts

On Wednesday, October 21st, Railway published an urgent security disclosure and stopped builds. Arnav Gupta’s post is the human side of that sentence: “I know there are a lot of DMs re: Railway’s builds being stopped and the security disclosure. I am heads down working with the rest of the team.” The internet’s reaction, as usual, was part concern and part meme — one quoted post simply read “scenes at Railway HQ right now” over a two-minute video. But strip the jokes away and this is the week’s most instructive operations story: a developer platform with millions of deployments chose the expensive option — halt the factory, disclose, fix — in public, on a Wednesday.

Railway’s playbook this week: stop the line, say it out loud, fix it in the open. The status page is the product during an incident.

Pair it with the week’s scarier save. Deedy: “I just saw with my very own eyes that even non-technical people can exploit CVEs like PHP2Shell using certain AI models and I am actually frightened.” Read the two posts together and the picture sharpens: the same models that make your team faster make the attacker’s learning curve flat. Railway’s disclosure is the professional response to that world — assume breach, communicate fast, eat the downtime. The uncomfortable corollary is that “non-technical people can exploit CVEs” is no longer a forecast. It is a field report.

Learnings

  • Disclose like Railway: halt, publish the notice, point everyone at the status page. The cost of the pause is smaller than the cost of the whisper network.
  • The exploit bar just fell. If AI models let non-technical people weaponise known CVEs, your patching SLA is now a race against script-kiddies with copilots, not against experts.
  • Memes are a signal too. “Scenes at Railway HQ” going around means the incident is legible to everyone — which is exactly what a public disclosure buys you.

The agent economy

Agents Get Phones (and a Leaderboard)

Via @spikeysanju · @mfranz_on · @yoheinakajima

Three saves, one thesis: agents are moving out of the chat window and into hardware. Abdullah Babakre defined the category this week — “phone-use”: an MCP/API that gives AI agents their own phones, cloud phones, real Androids and real iPhones; “tell your agent what to do, and it can operate a phone just like you do.” Sanju’s reply is the kicker: his team launched droidclaw.ai eight months ago and it went super viral — fully open source, MIT licensed, bring-your-own-device, agents controlling Android end-to-end from a dashboard. The category was declared this week; the product has been shipping since spring.

The week’s agent stack: your prompt, the agent, a phone-use API as its hands, and a real phone on the other end. droidclaw has been doing this since spring.

The other two saves complete the picture. Marco Franzon: “You SHOULD start learning/using two things: Hermes agent — local AI. That is the future,” quoting Nous Research’s note that Jensen Huang closed his GTC Berlin 2026 keynote with a Hermes demo as the finale — his line: “the way to build an agent is to ask an agent to build it for you,” installing a customized Hermes in one prompt. And Yohei Nakajima flagged monid.ai — “Product Hunt for agents,” a live leaderboard of the tools agents actually use. Read together: agents get bodies (phones), they get local (Hermes, on-device), and they get a market (a rankings page for what agents pick). The infrastructure of the agent economy is being built in public, one leaderboard at a time.

“The way to build an agent is to ask an agent to build it for you.”— Jensen Huang, via @NousResearch (quoted by @mfranz_on)

Learnings

  • Categories get declared after products ship. “Phone-use” was named this week; droidclaw has been viral for eight months. Naming follows building.
  • Local is the second bet. Hermes + local AI, per Jensen’s keynote close: the agent that builds itself, running where your data lives.
  • Watch what agents choose. monid.ai’s leaderboard is the demand-side signal — which tools agents actually reach for is the closest thing the agent economy has to a market share table.
Takeaway — If your product has an app and no agent story, the phone-use category is your warning shot: agents will operate your UI with or without your API. Better to be the tool they rank than the screen they scrape.
Original: @spikeysanju on droidclaw.ai · @mfranz_on on Hermes + local AI · @yoheinakajima on monid.ai

Wispr Flow’s India Playbook, From the Inside

Via @nitprashant · @tankots

Prashant Sharma, Growth Advisor for Wispr Flow’s India push, posted the field report: since September 14th, partnerships, creators and YouTube activity are compounding, one partnership pilot is now scaling, and they’re hiring for India growth — with a three-month Pro referral code (FLOWWITHPS) attached. Pair it with Tanay Kothari’s reintroduction: WisprFlow’s co-founder and CEO, who built a voice assistant at eleven with 2.5 million users, started Wispr in 2021 as a brain-computer interface, and pivoted to Wispr Flow. The India thesis is explicit — voice-first productivity for a market that types less and talks more — and the growth is being run like a creator-led GTM, not an ads-led one.

Learnings

  • Creator-led GTM travels well in India. Partnerships and YouTube first, ads later — the pilot-to-scale pattern in one post.
  • Voice is the wedge. A BCI pivot to voice dictation, now pushing India: the input method is the moat where typing never took hold.
