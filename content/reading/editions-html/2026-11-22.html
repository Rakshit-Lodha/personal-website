Lead Story • Mathematics • Verification, Not Just a Claim

GRH Has Fallen: The Lean Community Confirms Alpöge’s Proof

From the saves — Deedy, the MIT verification page (read in full), and Latent Space

On Friday, Levent Alpöge of Anthropic posted a four-page note claiming a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, written with Claude, existing only as seventeen million lines of Lean code. Yesterday the story moved from claim to check. The Lean Formalisation and Research Organisation and the Mathlib Initiative released a joint statement, quoted by Deedy: “We have full confidence that Alpöge’s proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement.” The Lean community has spoken. GRH has fallen.

That is a remarkable sentence to be able to write about a problem posed in 1859. But the more interesting document is the verification page itself, maintained by MIT’s Andrew Sutherland, with checks run using Claude Code. It is the most thorough public audit of a mathematical claim ever published — and it is worth understanding what it actually establishes, because the caveats matter as much as the headline.

The proof exists as code, not prose: 17 million lines of Lean, 175,187 modules, a 61.5-gigabyte export. No human has read it. Several machines have now checked it.

What was actually checked

The submission proves five theorems in Lean’s Main.lean: the Riemann Hypothesis itself, the same statement written out in the author’s own words, and three forms of the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions — no zeros to the right of the critical line, zeros in the critical strip lying on the line, and the same for primitive characters. The checks, as of the page’s last update on 22 November:

  • It compiles. All 175,187 modules compile under Lean v4.33.1 and Mathlib v4.33.1, with zero errors.
  • It assumes almost nothing. The five theorems depend only on the three standard axioms — propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound — and no axioms are declared outside Lean core.
  • It reproduces. An independent sandboxed lake build on a separate machine, with no network and the source tree read-only, reproduced the export byte for byte: 61,570,595,788 bytes, matching hash.
  • Independent kernels agree. The full export was replayed through the Lean kernel (175,187 of 175,187 modules), through nanoda, an independent Rust kernel (27 minutes 42 seconds on 80 threads), through con-leche and its Rust port con-ron, and through lean4lean, module by module. All accept.
  • It proves something checkable without zeta. Combined with an independent formalisation of Robin’s and Lagarias’s criteria, the proof yields two concrete inequalities about divisor sums — statements with no mention of the zeta function at all — which also pass every checker.

The verification ladder — four independent ways of saying yes

And then a second proof appeared

Within hours of Alpöge’s note, Alessandro Masullo released a 940-page, entirely human proof of the same result — work he had been doing for twelve years, undergoing secret manual review. Latent Space, in its AI News roundup, frames the contrast perfectly: the AI-written solution is probably correct but incomprehensible, and the human solution is probably technically incorrect but understandable. One of the two will be the proof the textbooks carry. The other is the proof the machines carry.

“Claude far outpaced me near the finish line. No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.” — Levent Alpöge, on working with Claude

The market, for what it is worth, did not celebrate: Anthropic’s stock fell about 10% on the day. And the cautionary tale still stands — OpenAI’s September Navier–Stokes claim is still unverified, with critics arguing its Lean proof did not match its natural-language result. Both AI-claimed Millennium results will take years to digest. The verification page is not the end of the story; it is the beginning of the part where humans have to understand what the machines have done. That is the weak-to-strong problem, stated in its purest form: we now need to control, check and learn from intelligence smarter than us in a domain where we can no longer follow the working.

The takeaway: a proof nobody has read has now been checked by four independent machines, and the people who maintain the checking infrastructure are confident. That is a new kind of mathematical fact — true by verification rather than by understanding — and mathematics will spend the next decade deciding what to do with it.

Science • A Hypothesis, Honestly Labelled

Is Gravity Quietly Ageing Us?

From the saves — Deepinder Goyal, with Laura van Rosmalen and Satchin Panda; published in Ageing Research Reviews (doi:10.1016/j.arr.2026.103444)

Last year Deepinder Goyal raised a provocative idea: gravity might be an overlooked contributor to ageing. This week the full paper — “Gravitational Stress, Cerebral Blood Flow, and Aging: A Hypothesis” — is published in Ageing Research Reviews, a top-three journal in its field. It is worth reading precisely because of how carefully its author refuses to overclaim.

We spend most of our waking lives upright, with the brain above the heart. The heart pays for that geometry every minute.

The argument is simple enough to state in a paragraph. We spend most of our waking lives upright, with the brain above the heart. The heart must therefore pump blood uphill, against gravity, all day, every day, for decades. Brain blood flow is known to decline with age. The paper proposes that a lifetime of gravitational stress could contribute to that decline — and that the brainstem and hypothalamus may be especially vulnerable, which matters because those regions regulate much of the rest of the body, and could therefore contribute to whole-body ageing.

What makes the paper admirable is the list of things it does not say, which Goyal spells out in his post: it does not say gravity is the sole cause of ageing, it does not say how big the effect is, and it does not suggest anyone try an intervention. It is a hypothesis paper. Peer review checked that the argument is strong enough to publish; only experiments can prove whether it is true or false. Experiments to test it are now under way, and the authors explicitly welcome collaboration — including work aimed at disproving it.

“Please note that this is a hypothesis paper. Peer review checked that the argument is strong enough to publish; only experiments can prove whether it is true or false.” — Deepinder Goyal

There is a method lesson here that travels well beyond ageing research. A hypothesis stated this cleanly is falsifiable in the good sense: it names a mechanism (gravitational load on cerebral perfusion), a vulnerable subsystem (brainstem and hypothalamus), and a test (does the effect survive controlled experiments?). Most big ideas fail at the first step — they are too vague to test. This one is not. Whether it turns out to be right, wrong, or a small contributor among many, the paper is a model of how to put a strange idea into the scientific record without dressing it up as a discovery.

The takeaway: the most interesting sentence in the paper is not the hypothesis but the invitation to disprove it. Science advances fastest when the person proposing the idea is also the person most interested in breaking it.

AI • Research • Ask the Model in Words, Not Numbers

Colgate Killed the Focus Group — by Letting the AI Talk First

From the saves — How To Prompt; the paper read in full: Maier et al., “LLMs Reproduce Human Purchase Intent via Semantic Similarity Elicitation of Likert Ratings” (arXiv:2510.08338), PyMC Labs × Colgate-Palmolive

A toothpaste company has quietly shown how to make AI market research actually work, and the trick is almost embarrassingly simple: stop asking the model for a number. Ask it to talk instead.

Fifty-seven real product surveys, 9,300 real humans — and a synthetic panel that matched them, once the researchers stopped asking for scores.

The problem is familiar to anyone who has used an LLM as a survey respondent. Ask it “rate this product from 1 to 5” and it gives safe, middle-of-the-road garbage: almost everything comes back a 3, with occasional 2s and 4s. The distribution looks nothing like real humans, who disagree with each other, love some things, hate others, and spread across the whole scale. The model is not being stupid; it is doing what it was trained to do — regress to the typical answer.

The paper’s method, Semantic Similarity Rating (SSR), sidesteps the number entirely:

  • Give the model a persona. A demographic profile — age, income, gender, location — and a product concept, shown as an image with a description.
  • Let it write. The model produces a short free-text reaction, in character: what it likes, what it would pay, what puts it off. A lower-income persona talks about value; a middle-aged one weighs brand trust; a younger one focuses on efficacy claims.
  • Measure meaning, not digits. The free text is embedded (OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small), and its cosine similarity to five anchor statements — one per Likert point, from strong rejection to strong intent — is turned into a probability distribution over the five ratings. The model never chose a number. The number fell out of the geometry of what it said.

The results, against 57 real Colgate-Palmolive personal care surveys with 150–400 US participants each: SSR recovers both the shape of real response distributions and the ranking of product concepts by mean purchase intent. GPT-4o reached about 90% correlation attainment — that is, 90% of the maximum correlation achievable given noisy human data — and Gemini 2.0 Flash reached 92%. Direct Likert prompting managed a distributional similarity of just 0.26 (GPT-4o) and 0.39 (Gemini). With SSR it rose above 0.85. And the personas matter: strip out the demographics and the correlation collapses from 92% to 50%. The average still looks right; the model just can no longer tell which products different people prefer.

Two caveats, both in the paper’s favour for being stated. First, the validation is on US personal care products; other categories, countries and languages are less tested, and the model only knows what was in its training data. Second, the synthetic panel is a screen, not a replacement: narrow the concept pool before committing to the full study, don’t skip the humans on big decisions. But the economics are the story — a month-long panel becomes a thousand hyper-targeted interviews overnight, with qualitative feedback deeper and more critical than what actual humans wrote, because the model will happily explain its rating in prose.

The takeaway: the failure was never the model; it was the question. Numbers force an LLM to average itself out. Words let it be specific — and specificity is where the signal lives. Every team using AI panels, evals or synthetic users should check whether they are asking for a digit when they should be asking for a paragraph.

India • Payments • A Quiet Crossover

India’s Credit Cards Just Passed Debit Withdrawals — for the First Time

From the saves — Manish Singh (chart: RBI / India Dispatch Research, verified against the underlying RBI data)

In October 2026, Indians paid more by credit card than they withdrew in cash with debit cards — $26.4 billion against $25.1 billion. It is the first time the two lines have crossed, and the chart tells a six-year story in one picture: credit-card payments climbing steadily from about $5 billion in mid-2020, debit-card cash withdrawals drifting flat between roughly $22 and $31 billion the whole time.

The numbers underneath the crossover are just as telling. India had 126.72 million credit cards in October, and cardholders made 683.3 million payments worth ₹2.45 lakh crore. Almost two-thirds of that value was spent online. Debit cards, by contrast, number over a billion — about eight for every credit card — but their job is overwhelmingly cash access: in September, debit cards were used for nearly 40 crore ATM withdrawals worth about ₹2.19 lakh crore, against only about 7 crore point-of-sale purchases and 2.6 crore online purchases. UPI, meanwhile, took the small payments entirely: 85.5% of all payments by count.

Read the three instruments together and the shape of Indian payments is clear. UPI owns the everyday transaction. Debit cards are the cash pipe. Credit cards are the credit and online-spending instrument — and they are growing fast enough (payment volumes up roughly a third year on year) to have overtaken the cash pipe in value for the first time. It is not the death of cash; ATM withdrawals are still enormous. It is the moment credit stopped being the junior instrument.

The takeaway: the crossover is a lagging indicator of a decade of infrastructure — UPI for small payments, e-commerce for online spend, and a credit-card base that has compounded while debit stayed a cash tool. Watch the next print: if credit keeps growing at this rate, the gap widens quickly.

Building • Opportunity Map

Greg Isenberg’s 41 Openings — Sorted Into Six Bets

From the saves — Greg Isenberg, “The biggest opportunities right now” (full list at ideabrowser.com)

Greg Isenberg posted 41 things to build right now, numbered and unadorned, ending with two words: KEEP BUILDING. The list is a mood board for the post-chatbox economy — but it is more useful sorted than listed. Here it is, grouped into the six bets it is really making.

41 openings, six bets — how the list breaks down

The AI–human boundary (11 items) is the biggest cluster, and the most interesting: build for throwaway software, for agents that need to spend money, for the 90% automated with 10% human sign-off model, for the “undo” button for agents, for people whose ego just got bruised by AI, and for the anti-AI premium — things that take effort on purpose, because effort is becoming the signal. The through-line is that the interface is moving past the chat box (item 15: “life after the chat box”), and the winners will be whoever designs the handoff between machine and human, not whoever builds the cleverest model.

Loneliness and connection (7) runs from solving loneliness outright to small private rooms, the friend with great recommendations, and keeping marriages alive. Privacy and ownership (6) wants places with no cameras and no recording, stuff that only works locally, and software you actually own. The physical world returns (6) — the return to the physical, the caregiving wave, the death of third places, the food and toxin awakening. Craft, attention and learning (6) covers learning that actually keeps up, infinite creative testing, and programmatic free tools instead of blog posts. Money and life stage (5) is the sober tail: housing and rootlessness, people who can’t retire at 65, the sports-betting and day-trading hangover, people who don’t own anything any more.

Read as a portfolio, the list is a bet that the next wave of valuable software is not about capability — the models are a given — but about the edges capability creates: trust, taste, privacy, physical presence, and the human moments automation cannot absorb. The single most contrarian item may be number 16: build for markets too small to matter before, like 500 lobster fishermen. When the cost of building collapses, the smallest markets become fundable.

The takeaway: pick one item and notice how many of the others it touches. The best opportunities on this list are the ones that sit at the intersection of two clusters — say, a local-only tool for a tiny market, or an agent that sits on hold for you and then asks a human to sign off.

Careers • The Founder Trap

Nobody Hires Ex-Founders — Except Ex-Founders

From the saves — Jason Levin and Ujjawal Asthana

Jason Levin put it in one line: being a founder is weird because you are good at a lot of things and could probably get a job in Product, Marketing, Engineering, Sales, GTM or VC — but the combination, mixed with your temperament, means you are unemployable and must build your own thing. Ujjawal Asthana, who has lived it, spent a long post stress-testing that claim at a panel with two other founders. All three had shut a company down or done a strategic sale. The hiring question came up. Two of them said the same thing: nobody hires ex-founders except ex-founders.

His account of looking for work after a strategic deal is precise about the mechanism. Senior or middle management at corporates and ex-founders, he writes, are like the same poles of a magnet: they repel with maximum force. Corporates run on functional expertise — people doing one thing for years. You could have built an outstanding product and sold it yourself, but interview for a PM role without ten years as a PM and the right jargon, and you won’t be entertained. The only two kinds of people who were interested: ex-founders, because they know what a founder brings, and boards starting a new business unit, or wanting someone who has seen a company’s full lifecycle to take over a unit and transform it. He landed great roles, but they came through a headhunter looking for a CEO to transform an existing business, or through reaching out to a founder directly. Never through a corporate hiring process. The third panellist, notably, had a great response from the market — she had been a corporate lifer before she built.

“Nobody hires ex-founders except ex-founders.” — the consensus of three founders at a panel

The lesson is not that founders are unemployable; it is that the hiring market prices the wrong thing. A founder’s value is generalist judgement across the whole lifecycle — exactly what a functional ladder cannot score, because its rubrics assume depth in one function. The market does clear, but through different channels: other founders, boards, and transformation mandates. If you are an ex-founder navigating it, the practical advice embedded in both posts is to stop applying through the front door. The front door was built for someone else.

The takeaway: the generalist premium is real but illiquid. It is paid by people who have done the job themselves, not by processes designed to filter for specialists. Plan the job search accordingly.

History • From the FTC Files

Zuckerberg’s 2012 Email: “Winning in Mobile”

From the saves — Internal Tech Emails; full text at techemails.com (document from FTC v. Meta, 2025)

On 22 April 2012, Mark Zuckerberg wrote to a colleague with a heads-up: he was pushing Mike Schroepfer — Schrep — very hard to cancel or scale back many of the projects in his group that were not directly related to mobile apps, so the team could focus more completely on mobile apps, and specifically on mobile messages. The email, an exhibit in the FTC’s case against Meta, is a masterclass in ruthless prioritisation — and in how a founder forces a focus shift through an organisation that does not want to make it.

The cancel list is the interesting part. Desktop messenger. The web messenger code rewrite. Shoebox, for syncing private photos for easier sharing on desktop. The mobile photos app — “since we’re acquiring Instagram.” The mobile location app, which the team wanted to start and Zuckerberg worried about starting at all. All good projects, he wrote, that the company needs to do eventually. But “winning in mobile at the core things we do is more important in my opinion.” He was explicit about the intent: these people should work on mobile messages, not monetisation. Messaging apps, he argued, are by far the most used app on almost everyone’s phone, and it is therefore strategically important for Facebook to be the best at them.

He also knew the organisation would resist. Schrep was moving slowly, he wrote, because of the disruption involved, and there were decision points coming up. “I am hopeful that he makes a big enough shift here so that I don’t have to make this decision for him, but I will do that if I am put in the position where I need to.” That is the whole email in one sentence: the polite version of “do it, or I will.”

The context is what makes it land. April 2012 is the month Facebook acquired Instagram for $1 billion — the deal the FTC later put at the centre of its monopoly case, with Zuckerberg’s own emails as the smoking gun. The same week he was killing the mobile photos app because Instagram made it redundant, he was buying the company that made it redundant. The email is a snapshot of a founder seeing the platform shift before his own organisation did, and using blunt force to get there first.

The takeaway: strategy is a cancel list, not a to-do list. Zuckerberg’s email names five good projects and kills them in one paragraph, because one thing — mobile messaging — mattered more than five good things. Most teams fail at this not because they can’t see the priority, but because they can’t bear to say the other things out loud.

AI • Product • A Nikita Bier Double

The Phone in Your Pocket Is the Next AI Frontier

From the saves — Nikita Bier and Nikita Bier

Two posts from Nikita Bier, saved back to back, make one argument from two directions. First: there would be a massive market opportunity unlocked if there were good local models that could run on a mobile device. There are vast amounts of data on our phones that are too private and too large to give to a frontier model — especially in today’s cybersecurity environment. The benefits may not accrue to the model developer, but it would be a big win for society. Second: when you are building a consumer app, you need near-photographic memory of every decision, every pixel on the app, and every data trend. Delegate key design decisions and you will not understand new patterns fast enough to iterate — and you won’t be able to explain those decisions to regulators, users, and the media.

Put them together and they describe the same job from both sides. The phone holds the richest, most private dataset in a person’s life — messages, photos, health, location, spending — and the only way to use it without shipping it to a frontier model is to run the model where the data already is. That is a hardware and distillation problem, not a capability problem: small models, on-device, private by architecture. And the builder’s side of the same coin is that the person shipping it has to hold the whole product in their head — every decision, every pixel, every trend — because the patterns that matter emerge across the details, and delegation breaks the feedback loop between seeing a pattern and being able to act on it.

There is a quiet challenge to the current AI stack in both posts. The frontier labs are racing on bigger models in bigger datacentres; the value Bier points to is in the opposite direction — smaller models, on the device, over data that can never leave. And the builder’s version is a challenge to the way AI-assisted building is evolving: if an agent makes your design decisions, you lose the memory that makes you fast. Speed, in consumer products, is a function of how much of the product lives in one person’s head.

The takeaway: the most valuable AI product of the next few years may not be a bigger model but a smaller one that never leaves your pocket — and the person who ships it will be the one who can still remember why every pixel is where it is.

Design • Taste Is the Moat

“If a Machine Can Grade It, a Machine Can Do It”

From the saves — mjs, both images transcribed in full; recommended watch: Pablo Stanley’s Designing a composer

mjs asked a question this week — “Is Math over? Is everything over?” — and answered it in two screenshots. Maths has an answer. Code compiles or it doesn’t. Both have an answer key. “If a machine can grade it, a machine can do it.” Creative tasks have no answer key, and that is the whole argument.

Give two great designers the same brief and you will get two completely different outputs. You can’t prove which one is better. You can only feel it, and it is dependent on your taste. He tells the story of cutting a video himself — blocky text, fast frames, “pure TikTok energy” — loving it, sending it to an experienced motion designer for a polish, and getting back a completely different take. He actually liked his own more. They posted the designer’s anyway. “The point isn’t who was right. It’s that nobody could prove who was right. There is no answer key.”

“There’s no large dataset of ‘taste.’ AI trains on consensus. Taste requires breaking it.”

The second image is the sharper one. In creative work, the moment a format works, everyone copies it. Then everyone copies the copies. A template’s strength decays exponentially toward slop. Instrument Serif is a beautiful typeface; now it is on every seed-stage landing page. So someone always has to keep inventing — new formats, new angles. There is no large dataset of taste. AI trains on consensus. Taste requires breaking it. “Anything with an objective answer will get automated. Anything that runs on taste is here to stay.”

Read next to the lead story, the piece lands harder. Maths, the most answer-keyed discipline there is, has just been done by a machine at a scale no human can follow. The answer-keyed world is being automated from the top down. What is left is the work with no answer key — taste, judgement, the format nobody has copied yet because nobody has invented it. That is not a consolation prize. It is the job description.

The takeaway: if your work has an answer key, assume the machine is coming for it. If it doesn’t, your taste is the moat — and taste is built by inventing formats, not copying them.

1. “Riemann Hypothesis proved… twice independently” — Latent Space, 20 November

Read the original

The newsletter’s framing of the Riemann week goes beyond the proof itself, and three of its threads are worth carrying. First, the weak-to-strong problem: Alpöge collaborated with Claude only in the early stages, then was left far behind on the critical parts involving automorphic forms on higher-rank groups — the opposite of the usual centaur pattern, where the human guides the machine. The agent economy is compounding in public, and the revenue lines are starting to look like real businesses rather than demos.

The takeaway: both pieces are about the same shift from different angles — capability is no longer the bottleneck; verification, distribution and revenue are. The Riemann proof needs checking, Perplexity needs to hold its run rate, and in both cases the numbers are now big enough to matter.
