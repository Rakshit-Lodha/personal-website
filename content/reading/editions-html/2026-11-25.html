Below the Line — Anthropic’s Stock Slips Under Its IPO Price as Its Builders Leave for OpenAI

Vijar Kohli · Arfur Grok · Market data: Finnhub, Reuters, TechCrunch, Motley Fool · 24–25 November 2026

Five days after the largest IPO in history, Anthropic’s shares closed below the price they were sold at. The stock fell 6.54% on Tuesday to $81.91 — under the $83 offer price, after opening its first day at $111.50 and touching $115.10 — and it has now fallen on every single trading day since its debut. The same morning, the account that tracks AI lab staff moves reported that Thariq Shihipar, the Claude Code engineer who had become the product’s most recognisable face on X, has joined OpenAI. He is the second senior Claude Code figure to cross over in a month. The market and the talent are telling the same story at the same time, and neither is flattering.

The numbers first. Anthropic priced 980 million shares at $83 on 18 November, raising $81.3 billion — the biggest IPO ever, taking the crown from SpaceX’s $75 billion. Demand was so strong that the stock opened at $111.50, 34% above the offer price, and its fully diluted valuation briefly topped $2 trillion. Then the air came out, day by day: $97.17 at the close on Thursday, $88.91 on Friday, $87.64 on Monday, $81.91 on Tuesday. The intraday low on Tuesday was $75.61. Finnhub puts the market value at about $1.18 trillion. Only about 6% of the shares can actually trade — 94% are locked up — which makes every move sharper than it looks: a small pond, and every seller is throwing a rock into it.

Anthropic (ANT) — close, 19–24 November 2026 ($)

The talent story runs on the same clock. Shihipar left Anthropic on 24 October after more than a year on Claude Code, where he worked on the skills system, context engineering and dynamic agent workflows, and became the product’s public face on X after founder Boris Cherny. Arfur Grok, the account that reported his departure hours before he announced it himself, now reports he has joined OpenAI — neither Shihipar nor OpenAI has publicly confirmed the move yet, and this paper marks it as reported, not confirmed. If it holds, he is the second senior Claude Code figure to go to OpenAI in a month: Catherine “Cat” Wu, Anthropic’s head of product for Claude Code, joined OpenAI on 5 November to lead product management for its core products team. Anthony Morris, who built the Claude Code desktop client, also left on 24 October. Three core members of the team that built Anthropic’s most important product have walked out within weeks of its IPO.

“We’ll continue to see the cycle, and see the best product teams continuously get hired away. It’s natural and I suspect employee retention will continue to be difficult for the best model companies.”— Vijar Kohli, on the Anthropic-to-OpenAI rotation

Kohli’s post, saved by Rakshit in the small hours, is the clearest statement of the mechanism. The moves are mostly compensation-driven, he argues, not product-driven — and they follow the money in a repeating cycle. In the first half of the year engineers left for Cursor, drawn by a great product and the rumour of a SpaceX acquisition and IPO. Then Anthropic’s own listing pulled people back from Cursor and Google DeepMind, because Claude Code had achieved tremendous growth and a trillion-dollar IPO was coming — and those who joined in the last nine months watched their stock vest and accelerate. Now, he says, everybody wants the next possible IPO, which is OpenAI, possibly as early as the first quarter of 2027. After that, a wave of smaller startups will try to go public — Cognition, Databricks, someone else — but most will not have the revenue or the capital raised to fuel the momentum OpenAI and Anthropic had. “All of the smart money has basically gone to the first three IPOs,” he writes. The cycle, in his telling, is not a verdict on any one company. It is the labour market doing what labour markets do when the prize moves: the best product teams get hired away, again and again.

There is a second, quieter signal in the price itself. A stock falling below its IPO price within a week is what the market calls a broken IPO — the banks priced it at $83, the public paid $111.50 on day one, and five days later the market says the banks were the generous ones. The usual defences apply: a broad tech sell-off hit AI stocks on Tuesday (Nvidia fell with it), and the float is tiny. But the comparison Kohli’s post invites is with SpaceX, which also debuted this year at $135 and took more than a month to first trade below its offer price; Anthropic did it in four sessions. The dates to watch now are 30 November, when ANT joins the MSCI indexes and index funds are forced to buy, and 15 December, the first lock-up unlock, when far more shares can be sold. Until then, the story of the biggest IPO in history is a stock that has only ever gone down — and a team whose builders are leaving for the rival that has not listed yet.

The takeawayThe IPO was supposed to be the moment Anthropic’s people got rich and stayed. Instead the wealth arrived, the stock slipped under its own offer price, and the people started leaving for the next listing. Compensation is the gravity in this market: it pulled engineers to Cursor, then to Anthropic, and now it is pulling them to OpenAI. The product matters — Claude Code is the reason anyone cares — but the retention problem is structural, and it will follow every lab that lists.
Featured · The AI Builder Myth

“Everyone’s an AI Builder” Is a Myth — Atlassian Tried It, and Everyone Went Back to Their Old Jobs

Lenny Rachitsky · Video, 1 minute 9 seconds · Transcribed in full

A year ago the narrative was that everyone was becoming an AI builder — someone who spends most of their time building software with AI, and that the old roles would merge into one. Atlassian took the idea seriously enough to test it: they moved product managers and designers onto teams where everyone was an AI builder, with no PM or designer roles at all, just builders from different crafts. For the first few weeks it was, by every account, amazing. A month in, everyone had gone back to doing their old jobs. Lenny’s clip, saved by Rakshit overnight, is the cleanest field report yet on what AI actually does to an organisation.

The arc is told in two check-ins. In the first weeks, a PM on one of the teams told the leader running the experiment that it was unbelievable — “you’re constantly building, it’s so good, I feel like we’ve moved so fast, we’ve moved faster than ever before.” After about a month, the same person came back with the opposite report: everyone had gone back to doing their traditional jobs. The diagnosis was not that the building had failed. It was that building had never been the constraint. “We realized throughput’s not our problem. Direction’s our problem. So I need more people to steer the ship.”

“AI is really good at amplifying your strengths and equally it amplifies your weaknesses.”— Atlassian leader, in Lenny’s clip

That sentence is the whole piece. The experiment’s lesson is that specialists are still important, and specialisation is still important, because access to a tool is not expertise in the domain the tool operates on. The clip’s closing image makes the point visually: think of each craft as a circle in a Venn diagram. AI makes every circle bigger — the PM can build, the designer can ship, the engineer can write — but the circles are not overlapping. They are just all equally getting bigger. The work of deciding what to build, and whether it is the right thing, still sits in the gaps between them, and it still belongs to people whose job is judgement, not output.

It is worth putting this next to the lead story. Anthropic’s problem this week is retention of its best product people; Atlassian’s experiment suggests why those people are so hard to replace. If throughput is cheap and direction is scarce, the people who steer are the bottleneck — and the market is currently bidding for them by the IPO cycle. The “AI builder” narrative promised that roles would dissolve. What Atlassian found is that they re-form, quickly, around the same questions they always answered: what should we build, and why this and not that.

The takeawayAI removes the cost of producing. It does not remove the cost of deciding. A team of builders with no one steering ships faster in no particular direction — and after a month, the builders themselves ask for the steering back.

Dot as a Teammate: Will’s Video Editor Works While He Plays Tennis

Greg Brockman · ChatGPT · Video, 1 minute 17 seconds · Transcribed in full

Every morning, Will wakes up with a new idea and asks the same question: how can I bring this idea to life today? The answer, in the clip Greg Brockman shared overnight, is his dot — OpenAI’s always-on agent, which he uses as an orchestration layer for video editing. “Will edits videos. So does Will’s dot. It handles the busywork, so Will can make the creative calls and still have time for tennis.” The clip is a day-in-the-life demo, and it is also the clearest picture yet of what OpenAI means by a dot as a teammate rather than a tool.

The demo runs on a real job: turning a live programme feed into finished social media clips. Will asks his dot to check the codebase for the video clipping tool they are testing, and to generate three caption options based on an email from Parth — then to schedule a follow-up with Jordan to go over them. The dot does the heavy lifting while Will does what he calls the ideas-and-approvals job: “take something from nothing and turn it into the best possible thing it can be.” Before the dot, he says, he was at fourteen hours a day of screen time; every new idea needed a new thread in ChatGPT or Codex. Now the dot holds the context across the day, and ideas that pop up while he is playing tennis or sitting in a coffee shop become version one of something by the time he is back at his computer. “I wake up, pop on the computer, and later in the day have a version one of my crazy idea.”

The mechanism matters more than the demo. Dots, launched in September, are always-on agents with their own cloud computer, and the pitch has always been that they are teammates with boundaries — do alone, ask first, never — rather than chatbots you prompt. This clip shows the boundary in practice: the dot is trusted to run the pipeline from a live feed to finished clips, and Will is explicit that his job has narrowed to ideas and approvals. It is the same division of labour the Atlassian story reaches from the opposite direction: the scarce work is direction, and the agent’s job is to make direction cheap to exercise. The open question, which the clip does not answer, is what happens to the approvals job when the dot gets good enough to make the creative calls too. For now, Will is clear about where the line sits: he is the ideas guy, and the dot makes sure the ideas become things.

The takeawayThe dot is not a faster way to edit video. It is a standing arrangement: the human owns taste and direction, the agent owns the pipeline between an idea and version one. Fourteen hours of screen time becomes a conversation and an approval.
Shipping · Claude, Now as Apps

Claude Artifacts Can Now Be Installed as Desktop Apps. Are We Reinventing PWAs?

Anthropic · ClaudeDevs · Announced 24 November 2026 · Video demo watched frame by frame (silent)

Starting this week, a Claude artifact can become a desktop app. Anthropic’s “Install as app” feature, rolling out in beta in Claude for Mac and Windows, lets you take an artifact you use every day and give it its own icon and its own window, outside the Claude chat. Arnav Gupta’s reaction, saved by Rakshit, is the question the whole feature has to answer: “Are we reinventing PWAs here?” The honest answer is: partly — but with a permission system and a brain attached that a PWA never had.

A still from the announcement video: the Debris Space Tracker artifact — every object in Earth orbit since 1957, 33,319 of them — running as its own desktop window.

The demo in the announcement video shows what that means in practice. A user asks Claude to “build me an app that tracks every piece of space junk in orbit”, and the result is a Debris Space Tracker with a globe of Earth, a catalogue of 33,319 objects in orbit and a chart of debris growth since 1957 — running as its own window. A second demo shows Amira’s corner shop: a shelf-management app with stock levels, sell-through percentages and a product panel for rose lemonade, 94% sold, 45 of 48 sold. The point of both is that these are not web pages you bookmark. They are tools that live in the dock, send notifications, add menu-bar items, and — with your permission — use Claude and your connectors, updating automatically whenever the underlying artifact is edited.

The permission system is the serious part, and Anthropic has built it in three tiers. By default an installed app can only do what it could do in a browser. With app permissions it can ask for desktop features Claude can tightly control: always-on-top windows, global shortcuts, notifications. Full computer access lets it run programs and read, change or delete files without asking each time — and that tier is restricted to apps you built yourself, or, on Team and Enterprise plans, apps built by someone in your organisation. Anthropic does not review apps before you install them, and says so plainly: only give full access to apps you trust. Permission covers only the current version, so Claude asks again after every update, even your own edits. Team plans get it on by default with full access off; Enterprise has it off until an owner turns it on; HIPAA-ready organisations and hybrid deployments cannot use it at all, and Docs, Slides and Sheets artifacts cannot be installed.

The catch, buried at the bottom of Anthropic’s own page: you need to be online and signed in to Claude to open an app. A desktop app that stops working without Wi-Fi is a real limitation — and every request the app makes to Claude counts toward your plan’s usage limits, so a busy app eats the same budget as your chats.

So is it a PWA? A PWA gives you an icon and a window. This gives you an icon, a window, a permission model, and a model that can keep building the app while you use it. The more interesting comparison is with the dot in the story above: both are Anthropic and OpenAI moving the agent out of the chat box and into the operating system, one as a teammate and one as an installed tool. The risk is the same in both cases — the more the agent can touch, the more the permission question matters. Anthropic’s answer, for now, is to make the user re-approve after every change. That is the right instinct. Whether it survives contact with people who just want their shop app to work is the test.

The takeawayThe artifact is no longer a thing you share in a chat. It is a thing you install. The feature’s real innovation is not the icon — it is the three-tier permission model that decides what an AI-built app is allowed to touch, and the rule that every new version has to ask again.

Provision Labs was launched in October 2026 and describes itself as building the data, evaluation and deployment infrastructure for physical AI systems — the unglamorous plumbing that lets robots understand visual and spoken instructions and turn them into physical movement. Its existing backers include Lachy Groom — who co-founded and backed Physical Intelligence, the San Francisco physical-AI company — plus General Catalyst, Bain Capital Ventures and Glade Brook Capital. The strategy, according to Moneycontrol’s sources, is to work with or bring multiple Pronto-like businesses across home services, manufacturing and hardware under Provision, so the company can access data from different physical environments to train AI systems. Pronto is the first: the process of making it a subsidiary is under way and would complete over the coming months, with Pronto’s existing investors becoming shareholders in Provision. Earlier this month Provision acquired Mumbai-based Basilisk Industries, bringing founder Shourya Watwani aboard to lead hardware development; the team had built a metric 3D hand-tracking model, redesigned perception hardware and built an initial tactile glove.

The logic is the data play, and it is worth spelling out. Physical AI’s bottleneck is not models — it is real-world data: what a home actually looks like when someone cleans it, how a hand actually moves, what a shelf actually contains. Pronto, founded in 2025, competes with Snabbit and Urban Company’s InstaHelp in instant home services, and it has already introduced Pronto Verified, an opt-in programme under which service sessions are recorded and anonymised footage is used for AI training. A home-services business is, in this framing, a sensor network that happens to clean houses: every visit generates the kind of embodied, physical data that a robot lab cannot scrape from the internet. That is why the same investors keep showing up on both sides of the deal — Lachy Groom, General Catalyst, Bain and Glade Brook backed Pronto’s $45 million extended Series B at a $200 million valuation earlier this year, and are already on Provision’s cap table.

“Pronto and Provision Labs are two fully independent brands and will continue to operate as such. There is no change to Pronto’s business strategy, operations or long-term ambitions.”— Anjali Sardana, to Moneycontrol

Sardana’s denial is specific and worth quoting in full, because it draws a line the sources cross. “We intend to work with businesses across industries through partnerships rather than bringing them under a common ownership structure,” she said — and on the fundraise, “we do not comment on speculation regarding fundraising, investors or ownership structures.” G Squared, Founders Fund and a16z did not respond to Moneycontrol’s queries. The gap between the two accounts is not unusual in a deal that is still being negotiated — the size of the round, the investor mix and the terms are all, by Moneycontrol’s own account, not finalised — but it matters for how to read the news. If the sources are right, this is a founder using a fast-growing consumer business as the data engine for a much bigger physical-AI platform, and the $40–50 million is Provision’s maiden fundraise. If Sardana is right, it is a partnership model, and Pronto stays a home-services company that happens to share a founder with a robotics-infrastructure venture. Either way, the direction of travel is the same: the most interesting asset in instant home services may not be the bookings. It is the footage.

The takeawayEvery home-services visit is a training run for a robot that does not exist yet. Pronto’s pivot — acquisition or partnership, the structure is still contested — is a bet that real-world data is the moat in physical AI, and that the companies that touch the physical world every day are the ones that will own it.
Voice · The Dictation Race

Canto-2: The Speech Model That Finally Gets Names Right — 69% of the Hard Ones, Up from 41%

Tanay Kothari, Wispr Flow · Video, 2 minutes 1 second · Transcribed in full · Launch post read in full

The hardest problem in dictation is not understanding words. It is understanding names — the person, the project, the term that sounds like three other things and that no generic model has ever seen. Wispr Flow’s Canto-2, launched on Monday, is built specifically for that problem, and its headline number is a jump: it recovers 69% of the hardest names and terms, up from 41% with Canto-1, without inserting them more often when they were never said. The demo, transcribed in full from Tanay Kothari’s launch video, shows how: a dictionary, reinforcement learning, and a refusal to guess.

Canto-2 vs Canto-1 — Wispr’s own benchmarks

The demo is the argument. Kothari dictates a message mixing English and Hindi — “Page three pe whisper mode wala demo daal dena” — and the model keeps up when he switches languages mid-sentence. Then the Dictionary feature: the name Reid, spelled R-E-I-D, is saved in his dictionary, and the same recording is run against other voice models. The others get it wrong; Canto-2 gets it right — and, crucially, does not insert “Reid” into recordings where the name was never said. That second half is the hard part. “Today’s speech models struggle to use context well,” Kothari says in the video. “The better a model gets at understanding your words, the more it adds them when you never said them at all.” Canto-2’s fix is a training method Wispr calls entity-GRPO with attention-shaping: reinforcement learning that rewards recovering the right term and penalises inserting words never said, trained against dictionaries full of sound-alike distractors. It holds up with a thousand-word dictionary, at 97% recall on easier terms.

The numbers around the headline matter too. Across 14 languages including Spanish, French, Hindi and Mandarin, the average error rate fell from 9.9% to 6.3% — a third fewer mistakes. On Wispr’s internal evaluation of ten hours of English dictations, Canto-2 had the lowest word error rate at 2.7%, ahead of Gemini 3.1 Pro, OpenAI’s GPT Transcribe and Meta’s Muse Transcribe. It is ten times larger than Canto-1, which launched only two months ago, and it was built, Kothari says, by a team of speech researchers set on “one of the hardest challenges in voice: a single model that worked for billions of people, and still feels like it was made for you.” It is rolling out in Wispr Flow this week, on Mac, Windows, iOS and Android in the Americas, with other regions to follow. The honest caveat, which Wispr states itself, is that these are the maker’s own benchmarks — the Hindi result on Hugging Face’s open benchmark is the independent anchor, and it is the one to watch.

The takeawayDictation fails on the words that matter most: names, terms, the things that identify you. Canto-2’s insight is that the fix is not a bigger model alone — it is a model trained to use your dictionary without hallucinating it everywhere else. Getting a name right is a small thing. Getting it right only when it was actually said is the product.

The takeawayEvery subscription is a bet on how much model you will actually use. Claude gives you the most API dollars per dollar — and the least subsidy. Whether that makes it overpriced or honest depends on whether you measure value in tokens or in tasks finished.
From the Substacks

OpenAI Shows Its Pretraining Curve — and Proposes a Speed Limit for Everyone

Latent Space, AI News · 23 November 2026 · Read in full

The most important document in this week’s AI News is not a product launch. It is a graph. OpenAI’s Jakub Pachocki has published “Perplexity and Intelligence”, five years of the company’s base-model log-perplexity on a fixed held-out dataset — numbers that were, until now, treated as sensitive intellectual property. The curve runs from GPT-3.5 at around 0.99 and GPT-4 at 0.91 to GPT-6 Astra at about 0.76, and Pachocki’s argument is that the curve is a speedometer for the whole field: when loss fell below 0.8, capabilities in cybersecurity and mathematics started emerging quickly, and he extrapolates that another jump could come around 0.68–0.70. His proposal is that labs agree on a first common limit to development, with further progress gated on milestones in understanding and monitoring AI behaviour — a successor to chain-of-thought monitoring, for example. Microsoft’s Mustafa Suleyman gave the first win by cosigning and joining in, running MAI-2 Base on OpenAI’s evaluation set and reporting 0.819 — GPT-5.6 Sol territory, on a shared tokenizer.

The reactions split along predictable lines. Greg Brockman highlighted the warning that alignment progress is unlikely to keep pace with pretraining acceleration; Mark Chen called the numbers formerly sensitive IP and asked other labs to disclose their key metrics. The critics raised real confounds: tokenizer bias could favour OpenAI over Anthropic in any common limit; post-training likely accounts for more than 75% of FLOPs, so pretraining perplexity may be the wrong pacing metric; part of the steep curve may come from a growing share of model-generated pretraining data; and without model sizes or compute figures it is hard to separate scaling from algorithmic progress. OpenAI’s staff replied that the eval is fixed over time and excludes model-generated code. Anthropic, notably, redacted pretraining metrics from its METR report — and researchers are now calling on it to publish them. Pachocki’s internal model already sits below the released Astra, and he says pretraining could see an “avalanche” like the recent one in maths as soon as 2027; community fits put 0.70 somewhere between January and March 2027.

The rest of the roundup is the week in miniature. Gemini 4 Neon sits between Carbon and Hydrogen for complex coding and enterprise work, takes the top spot on LiveBench 2.0 at 87.4 — against 84.9 for both Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.2 Astra, at roughly a quarter of Fable’s cost per task — and OpenRouter spend on Google models exceeded OpenAI’s for the first time since June. Z.ai’s GLM-5.4 is the first looped transformer at flagship scale: 750 billion parameters, 28 layers run twice, claiming two-to-three-trillion-class performance, with weights promised “responsibly”. Thinking Machines’ Inkling 2 Mini squeezes 48 passes per token out of 24 layers in about 20 gigabytes of open weights, and a research variant cut destructive actions from 26.6% at one pass to 3.1% at four. And the evals beat goes on: Epoch’s benchmark reviews rated three of four benchmarks flawed, with at least 44% of sampled tasks in Agents’ Last Exam carrying severe errors. The through-line is the same as the pretraining curve: the numbers are getting better faster than our ability to trust them.

The takeawayOpenAI has published the speedometer and proposed a speed limit. The curve says the next jump in capabilities is months away, not years — and the argument now is whether perplexity is the right dial to watch, and whether anyone will agree to be governed by it.

The Future Needs Furniture

Venkatesh Rao, Contraptions · 24 November 2026 · Read in full

Yancey Strickler wrote recently about the sadness of watching abilities that once constituted a life become things machines can simply do — a racecar driver in a world of driverless cars, a person proud of directions after Google Maps. Venkatesh Rao’s reply, the newest Contraptions essay, disagrees with the feeling but takes the predicament seriously. His metaphor: we have upgraded from a small tent to a mansion, but the mansion does not have enough furniture yet. The future has arrived with too few normalising elements — too few stable categories that make a new reality inhabitable, and worth caring about.

Rao’s framework is a taxonomy of ontological crisis, in three kinds. The first is a crisis of labels: the right kinds, wrong names — the kind Google Translate has already dissolved. The second is a crisis of learning: the wrong number and kinds of categories, in a mind still growing to accommodate them — growing pains, which AI now eases for adults too, since Claude Code makes any unfamiliar computing language accessible through plain language. The third is the one that matters: the wrong categories, a capacity for growth that has slowed, and — the twist — you still care about the distinctions you can no longer meaningfully make. A programming-language maven who cares deeply about the differences between languages, in a world where AIs invent languages on the fly, is living in a third-kind crisis. It is, Rao says, a crisis of care.

“Furniture is reality made locally inhabitable, which includes care-worthiness.”— Venkatesh Rao, “The Future Needs Furniture”

By furniture Rao means the stable categories that make caring agency possible without renegotiating reality from scratch every morning: a chair, money, mathematical notation, a protocol, a genre, a ritual, an API, a recipe, a map. A chair solves a recurring problem involving gravity, anatomy, posture, materials and fatigue, and we care about chairs. Civilisation, in Whitehead’s phrase, advances by increasing the number of operations we can do without thinking about them — while continuing to care about them. A future without enough furniture forces you to think too hard and care too little, and makes lazy utopian and dystopian understandings more tempting. The solution is not to retreat to the tent. It is to add furniture to the mansion — work that must encounter the inconvenience of unfurnished futures and design its way through, “with a profligate exuberance”.

The essay’s second half replaces the familiar “jagged frontier” map — machines excellent here, embarrassingly bad there — with a different geography: a playability archipelago. The knowable world is deeply learned islands, reliable channels of transfer, and encounter-bound interstices at many scales. What is fundamentally jagged is the playability of the world, not the border race between humans and machines. Humans and machines are on the same side of the consequential boundary: between the playable and the unplayable, the analytic and the constitutive. A mathematician and a theorem prover both operate deep inside a highly playable regime; a roboticist, a robot and a warehouse worker can all be defeated by the same patch of unexpected friction. And meaning, Rao argues, is made where playability falters — in encounters with constitutive reality, in the context of I–You relationships, in Buber’s sense: when an “it” becomes a “you”. His closing line is the one to carry: “Chop wood, carry water. AI or human, doesn’t matter. But who you chop and carry with does.”

The takeawayThe anxiety about AI is usually framed as a race over capabilities. Rao reframes it as a furnishing problem: the new world is bigger than the old one and emptier, and the work now is building the stable categories — the chairs, protocols, genres and relationships — that make it a place worth caring about. The future does not need more capability. It needs more furniture.
